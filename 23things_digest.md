# Study Digest: 23 Things They Don’t Tell You About Capitalism by Ha-Joon Chang

## Preface and Overview

This digest is a comprehensive academic study guide to Ha-Joon Chang's 2010 book. Chang is a heterodox economist at Cambridge University who challenges the free-market orthodoxy that has shaped global economic policy since the 1980s.

The book is NOT anti-capitalist. Chang argues capitalism is the best economic system humanity has devised. His target is a specific, extreme variant: free-market or neo-liberal capitalism, dominant since Thatcher and Reagan. His central claim is that this variant has produced slower growth, greater inequality, more financial instability, and worse development outcomes than the regulated capitalism of the post-war Golden Age or the developmental capitalism of successful East Asian economies.

Each of the 23 Things is a targeted rebuttal of a standard free-market claim, supported by historical evidence, cross-national comparisons, and concrete examples.

## THING 1: There Is No Such Thing as a Free Market

**Memory Hook:** *Every market is political — someone drew the boundary.*

### Central Thesis

All markets operate within a framework of rules, restrictions, and boundary decisions that are inherently political. The claim that certain markets are "free" while others are "regulated" is a false dichotomy — both involve political choices about what is and is not allowed to be bought, sold, and at what conditions. The real question is never "regulated vs. unregulated?" but rather "whose interests do the regulations serve?"

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

Markets should be left alone. Government interference distorts prices, misallocates resources, and removes incentives for innovation. Freedom of contract is the foundation of prosperity. If a market outcome seems wrong, the solution is more market, not more government.

### Chang's Counterargument

There is no objective line between a "free" market and a "regulated" one. Every market rests on political decisions: which goods can be bought and sold (not human beings, not votes, not prescription drugs without a licence), who can participate (not children, not unlicensed professionals), and under what conditions (consumer protection law, product liability, return policies). We accept many of these restrictions so thoroughly that they become invisible — but invisibility does not make them less political.

Immigration controls illustrate the point perfectly: wages in Sweden are fifty times those in India for the same bus-driving work, not because Swedish drivers are fifty times more productive but because political restrictions prevent Indian drivers from competing. This is a massive labour market intervention that free-market economists never describe as "distorting the free market" — revealing that they accept some political choices and not others, based on whose interests those choices serve.

When free-market economists argue against a new regulation, they are not defending some objectively neutral state of nature. They are defending the current distribution of market rights — a distribution that reflects previous political choices, typically ones favouring those with existing economic power. Claiming to be neutral while defending the status quo is itself a political position.

### Key Examples

- **Child labour (1819):** The UK Cotton Factories Regulation Act limited child work to twelve hours daily and was denounced as an assault on freedom of labour. Today no free-market economist argues for restoring child labour. The boundary of the "free market" shifted — politically.
- **Bush and TARP (2008):** President Bush defended the 00bn nationalisation of the financial sector as consistent with free enterprise, which "rests on the conviction that the federal government should interfere in the marketplace only when necessary." Who decides what is necessary? Political judgement, always.
- **Environmental regulations:** Pollution controls were fiercely opposed as market interference when introduced. Now they are "just the ambient conditions" of doing business. The market did not change; political consensus did.

### Why This Chapter Matters

Thing 1 is the epistemological foundation of the entire book. If markets are inherently political, the claim that free-market policy is scientifically neutral collapses. This opens the door for all subsequent arguments: since there is no objectively correct free market, choosing how to regulate markets is a legitimate political act, and we should choose rules that serve broader social goals.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- Chang conflates the existence of *any* rules with the claim that *all* market structures are equally arbitrary. A spectrum from less to more efficient regulation can still be meaningful even if no pure market exists.
- He does not provide a systematic method for determining which regulations are better — establishing that choice is political but leaving criteria underspecified.
- Some economists concede that social norms and law define market boundaries while still maintaining that *within* those boundaries, market processes have important efficiency properties.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Directly feeds into Thing 3 (immigration is the biggest hidden market distortion), Thing 7 (rich countries used protectionist rules to develop), Thing 18 (regulation can be pro-business), and the book's entire project of replacing "free market vs. government" with "what kinds of market rules serve social welfare?"

## THING 2: Companies Should Not Be Run in the Interest of Their Owners

**Memory Hook:** *Shareholders can sell in seconds — they are the least committed stakeholders.*

### Central Thesis

The principle of shareholder value maximisation — that companies should be run primarily to maximise returns to their shareholders — has been economically destructive. Because shareholders are the most mobile and least committed stakeholders, prioritising their short-term interests produces underinvestment, worker exploitation, and the long-term weakening of the firms themselves.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

Shareholders own companies and bear residual risk (they are paid last after all other obligations). This gives them the strongest incentive to ensure the company performs well. Managers should be aligned with shareholder interests through stock options and the threat of hostile takeover. Maximising shareholder returns maximises social value.

### Chang's Counterargument

Shareholders are the *least* committed stakeholders. Workers invest in firm-specific skills not easily transferred elsewhere; suppliers configure equipment for specific clients; communities depend on local employers. Shareholders, by contrast, can exit at any moment. The very feature of limited liability that makes it so powerful for capital formation — easy exit — also makes shareholders unreliable guardians of long-term corporate value.

The outcome has been an "unholy alliance" between professional managers and short-term shareholders: managers receive astronomical pay through stock options; shareholders receive rising dividends and share buybacks. The cost is borne by workers (squeezed wages, job cuts), suppliers (lower prices), and the company's own long-term investment capacity.

Data confirms the failure: US investment as a share of GDP fell from 20.5% in the 1980s to 18.7% in 1990-2009 despite rising corporate profits. US per capita income growth slowed from 2.6% in the 1960s-70s to 1.6% in 1990-2009.

### Key Examples

- **GM's buybacks:** Between 1986 and 2002, GM spent 0.4 billion on share buybacks. Had that money been retained (at 2.5% annual return), it would have covered the 5 billion needed to avoid bankruptcy in 2009.
- **Jack Welch:** GE's long-time CEO who coined "shareholder value" in 1981 and built a corporate culture around it. Later called it "the dumbest idea in the world."
- **Share buyback explosion:** US corporate share buybacks were below 5% of profits for decades until the early 1980s. They rose to 90% of profits in 2007 and 280% in 2008 (companies borrowed money to buy back shares).
- **Anglo-American vs. continental models:** Germany (worker board representation), Japan (cross-shareholding), Sweden (differential voting rights for founding families) — all maintaining longer-term ownership anchors — produce more durable investment and stronger long-term performance.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter challenges the central governance principle of Anglo-American capitalism. It explains why investment fell while profits rose; why wages stagnated despite productivity growth; and why the 2008 financial crisis was so severe (short-term profit-seeking by financial firms is the shareholder value problem applied to banks).

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- Shareholder pressure sometimes forces out incompetent management and generates genuine efficiency gains.
- German and Japanese models have their own weaknesses: resistance to restructuring, "zombie firms" in Japan in the 1990s, slow adaptation in some European firms.
- Worker representation and state ownership also carry governance problems (capture, political appointments, bureaucratic rigidity).

### Connections to Other Chapters

Closely linked to Thing 6 (short-termism reduces investment and growth), Thing 13 (shareholder capitalism is the primary mechanism of rising inequality), Thing 14 (CEO pay is a product of the shareholder-manager alliance), Thing 18 (what is good for shareholders is not necessarily good for the company or country), and Thing 22 (financial deregulation amplified the short-termism problem).

## THING 3: Most People in Rich Countries Are Paid More Than They Should Be

**Memory Hook:** *Sven earns fifty times Ram not because he drives better, but because Ram cannot get a visa.*

### Central Thesis

The wage premium of rich-country workers over their poor-country counterparts is primarily explained by immigration controls, not by genuine individual productivity differences. This reveals a fundamental inconsistency in free-market thinking: economists advocate labour market deregulation everywhere except for immigration — the single most powerful labour market intervention in existence.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

Workers are paid according to their marginal productivity. Large international wage differences reflect genuine gaps in human capital, skills, institutional quality, and efficiency. Allow the market to work and wages will converge as poor countries develop.

### Chang's Counterargument

A Swedish bus driver earns roughly fifty times an Indian bus driver doing a similar job. The Indian driver, navigating cows, rickshaws, and potholes, is arguably *more* skilled. The productivity explanation fails immediately. The actual explanation: Indian bus drivers cannot freely compete for Swedish jobs because of immigration controls.

More broadly, even genuinely high-productivity workers in rich countries owe much of their advantage to collectively built infrastructure — technologies, institutions, legal systems, and accumulated knowledge built over generations. Warren Buffett acknowledged this openly: his success would be impossible in Bangladesh. Individual talent is inseparable from the national systems that enable it.

Crucially, poor countries are poor not because of their poor people — who can often hold their own against poor-country equivalents — but because of their rich people, who cannot build institutions productive enough to raise average wages. The blame for poverty lies with elites who fail to create dynamic economies, not with the poor themselves.

### Key Examples

- **Swedish vs. Indian bus driver:** The wage gap of 50:1 cannot be explained by driving skill. The gap is a product of immigration control and the collective economic infrastructure Sven inhabits.
- **Warren Buffett:** "If you stick me down in the middle of Bangladesh or Peru, you'll find out how much this talent is going to produce in the wrong kind of soil." This is self-awareness about collective infrastructure, not modesty.
- **Rich of poor countries vs. poor of rich countries:** The Indian IT engineer competing with Americans on Upwork demonstrates that poor-country workers can be competitive. It is the inability of developing-country elites to build productive institutions — not the poverty of their workers — that holds countries back.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter challenges the meritocratic myth: that market wages accurately reflect individual worth. It has profound implications for development policy, immigration debates, and global justice. It also demonstrates the intellectual inconsistency of free-market economists who accept the biggest labour market distortion (immigration control) while complaining about minimum wage laws.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- Genuine productivity differences between countries exist beyond immigration control — capital stocks, technology, institutions all matter.
- Chang does not argue for open borders, making his point more about intellectual consistency than policy prescription.
- The argument could be seen as undermining individual agency and the value of self-improvement.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Links directly to Thing 1 (immigration is the biggest invisible market restriction), Thing 10 (US "high living standard" partly rests on cheap immigrant labour), Thing 15 (individual entrepreneurship is not the constraint — collective institutions are), and Thing 17 (education cannot bridge the wage gap — national economic systems determine wages).

## THING 4: The Washing Machine Has Changed the World More Than the Internet Has

**Memory Hook:** *We overestimate the new and underestimate the familiar — and build policy on that distortion.*

### Central Thesis

People systematically overestimate the impact of the most recent technological changes while underestimating older, familiar ones. The household appliance revolution of the early-to-mid twentieth century — led by the washing machine — had more fundamental economic and social consequences than the internet has yet achieved. Similarly, the telegraph was proportionally far more revolutionary than the internet. This "recency bias" drives misguided economic and development policy.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

The ICT revolution has fundamentally transformed the economy. We live in a "borderless world" where distance is dead, national policies are obsolete, and continuous flexibility is essential. The knowledge economy demands ever-higher investment in education and internet infrastructure.

### Chang's Counterargument

The household appliance revolution transformed women's economic participation, family structure, fertility rates, and social mobility on a scale the internet has not yet matched. By automating domestic labour — washing machines, vacuum cleaners, refrigerators, piped water, gas stoves — these technologies freed tens of hours per week, primarily for women, enabling their mass entry into the paid labour force.

The internet's productivity impact has been remarkably hard to detect in aggregate data ("everywhere but in the numbers" — Solow). The telegraph, by contrast, reduced transatlantic message transmission time from two weeks to seven minutes — a speed improvement of 2,500 times, compared to the internet's roughly 5-100x improvement over the fax.

The myth of a revolutionary ICT transformation has led rich countries to neglect manufacturing, misdirected development aid toward laptops rather than wells, and justified sweeping deregulation on the ground that "everything has changed."

### Key Examples

- **Domestic service collapse:** US domestic service employment fell from 8% of the workforce in 1870 to under 1% today. The primary explanation is household technology, not cultural change alone.
- **Female labour force participation:** Married US women aged 35-44 in paid work: a few percent in the 1890s; nearly 80% today. Household appliances made this possible.
- **Telegraph vs. internet:** Speed improvement: telegraph 2,500x (three weeks to seven minutes). Internet: 5-100x improvement on fax. In relative terms, the telegraph was the more revolutionary technology.
- **"Digital divide" misdirection:** Billions donated for laptops and internet centres in developing countries. Basic water access, electrification, and affordable washing machines might do more for human welfare per dollar spent.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This is a methodological argument about how to think about technology and policy. It warns against "recency bias" that drives policy to neglect manufacturing as "old economy," overspend on fashionable technology, and dismantle regulations on the grounds that technology has made them obsolete.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- The internet is still relatively young; its full effects on AI, logistics, and remote work may yet dwarf the washing machine.
- The comparison mixes different types of impact (productivity vs. social transformation) which may not be comparable.
- Chang does not engage seriously with the internet's role in enabling the financial instability he criticises in Things 6 and 22.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Connects to Thing 9 (the "internet economy" myth feeds the post-industrial illusion), Thing 17 (the knowledge economy narrative overstates education's economic importance), and Things 6 and 22 (financial technology enabled the instability of the neo-liberal era).

## THING 5: Assume the Worst About People and You Get the Worst

**Memory Hook:** *"Work to rule" strikes reduce output by 30-50% — the economy runs on goodwill, not contracts.*

### Central Thesis

Free-market economics assumes people are entirely self-interested. This is empirically wrong. More importantly, designing institutions around this assumption tends to make it self-fulfilling: treating people as untrustworthy destroys the goodwill on which organisations depend. Successful companies and economies are built on trust, loyalty, and multiple motivations — not self-interest alone.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

Adam Smith showed that self-interest, not benevolence, drives economic life. The market channels self-interest productively. Public officials, exempt from market discipline, are especially prone to self-dealing, hence the case for limiting government and using market mechanisms wherever possible.

### Chang's Counterargument

The "work-to-rule" strike — where workers following their contracts precisely reduce output by 30-50% — proves that normal economic output depends on voluntary effort, initiative, and goodwill never specified in contracts. This goodwill cannot be purchased through incentives alone; it derives from genuine professional pride, loyalty, and moral commitment.

Good management practice, everywhere from Japan to the US, emphasises trust, inspiration, and teamwork — not surveillance and punishment. The Japanese production system (Toyota), which achieved extraordinary quality through worker autonomy and collective problem-solving, outcompeted the Taylorist surveillance model wherever the two competed. More generally: designing systems that assume and reward self-interest tends to attract and produce self-interested behaviour, creating a self-fulfilling prophecy of the worst kind.

### Key Examples

- **Kobe Steel manager:** A senior steel executive told World Bank economists: "If I assumed everyone is out for himself, the company would grind to a halt. I approve most proposals because I trust our employees' professionalism — I don't fully understand them." This is how large organisations actually function.
- **Work-to-rule:** Workers strictly following contracts reduce output 30-50%. Normal output depends on voluntary goodwill not covered by contracts.
- **Japanese production system:** Worker autonomy, collective problem-solving, and trust in employees as creative agents produced quality levels and productivity that Taylorist systems never matched.
- **Why don't we cheat taxi drivers?** In a world of pure self-interest, cheating a taxi driver you'll never see again is rational. Almost no one does it — because people have genuine moral commitments.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter undermines the microeconomic foundations of free-market policy design. If people are not purely self-interested, then high-powered incentive schemes (executive stock options, performance pay, marketised public services) often backfire — producing gaming, short-termism, and destruction of professional ethics rather than genuine improvement.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- Free-market economists can accept multiple motivations while maintaining that incentive design should assume self-interest as the "lowest common denominator" for robustness.
- Trust-based institutions have sometimes failed when bad actors exploit them.
- The Japanese production model has faced its own challenges in a globally competitive environment.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Links to Thing 2 (stock option pay assumes and reinforces self-interested management), Thing 14 (CEO pay designed on self-interest assumptions produces self-interested CEOs), Thing 16 (people are not fully rational either — the free-market model is doubly flawed), and Thing 19 (planning within firms works partly because employees are not purely self-interested).

## THING 6: Greater Macroeconomic Stability Has Not Made the World Economy More Stable

**Memory Hook:** *Taming inflation while creating more financial crises and job insecurity is not stability.*

### Central Thesis

Anti-inflation policy has been central to economic management since the 1980s. Inflation has been tamed. But the world has become less stable in the ways that matter most to most people: financial crises have become more frequent and severe, job insecurity has increased, and growth has actually slowed. The obsession with price stability has primarily served holders of financial assets, not the broader economy.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

Inflation is the primary economic threat. Independent central banks mandated for price stability, plus fiscal discipline, create the stable environment needed for long-term investment and growth. The Great Moderation of the 1990s-2000s proved this formula worked.

### Chang's Counterargument

Studies — including by free-market economists and IMF researchers — show that inflation below 8-10% has no negative effect on growth. South Korea (20% average inflation in the 1960s-70s) and Brazil (42%) grew very fast during supposedly dangerous inflationary periods. The German hyperinflation of 1923 — always invoked to justify inflation panic — was a product of war reparations and extreme political crisis: pathological, not typical.

Meanwhile, anti-inflation policies — high real interest rates, capital market liberalisation, labour market "flexibility" — have reduced investment, slowed growth, and created conditions for financial crises. The proportion of countries in banking crisis rose from essentially zero in 1945-mid1970s, to 5-10% in the late 1970s-80s, to 20% in the 1990s (when inflation was supposedly tamed), to 35% after 2008.

The policy package primarily served holders of financial assets: inflation erodes the real value of fixed-income assets; capital mobility allows finance to seek the highest short-term returns; labour "flexibility" shifts risk onto workers.

### Key Examples

- **German hyperinflation 1923:** Prices rose 10 billion-fold. But this is not a model for thinking about moderate inflation; it is the product of extreme war reparations demands and the occupation of the Ruhr industrial region.
- **South Korea and Brazil:** Grew extremely fast during periods that would today trigger IMF emergency intervention.
- **Rogoff-Reinhart data:** No banking crisis anywhere between WWII and the mid-1970s — the "dangerous" high-inflation era. Banking crises surged in the supposedly stable 1990s and catastrophically after 2008.
- **Japan's central bank in 2000s:** Refused to ease money supply for fear of hyperinflation — even as Japan experienced deflation. The extreme form of inflation phobia has consequences.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter shows how a policy framework supposedly designed for general welfare was actually calibrated for financial interests. It also explains why the neo-liberal era produced slower growth: high real interest rates discourage real investment while encouraging financial speculation.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- Price stability does have genuine long-term planning benefits for firms and households.
- Central bank independence may prevent political business cycles.
- Chang underemphasises that sustained moderate inflation has cumulative distributional costs for those on fixed incomes.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Closely linked to Thing 2 (short-termism driven by financial deregulation is part of the same policy package), Thing 13 (anti-inflation policy is one component of the shift of income toward financial asset holders), and Thing 22 (financial liberalisation — the other half of the neo-liberal package — created the instability anti-inflation was supposed to prevent).

## THING 7: Free-Market Policies Rarely Make Poor Countries Rich

**Memory Hook:** *Hamilton, Lincoln, Grant — every US president on the dollar bill was a protectionist.*

### Central Thesis

Almost all of today's rich countries built their wealth through active state intervention, protectionism, and industrial policy — not through free trade and free markets. The countries that most faithfully followed free-market prescriptions since the 1980s have grown slowest. Rich countries tell developing nations to adopt policies the rich nations themselves never used.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

Free trade and open markets are the path to development. State intervention distorts comparative advantage and wastes resources. Post-war state-led development in the Global South produced stagnation; market liberalisation has corrected this. Countries should exploit their comparative advantages rather than trying to create new ones.

### Chang's Counterargument

The historical record is the reverse of the conventional story. Britain built its industrial dominance behind protectionist walls from the 1720s to the 1850s, adopting free trade only when it had absolute industrial supremacy. The US maintained the world's highest industrial tariffs from the 1830s to the 1940s. Japan, Korea, Taiwan, Germany, France, Finland, Norway, and Austria all built their industrial bases through state-guided development.

Per capita income growth in the developing world fell from 3% per year in the 1960s-70s to 1.7% in 1980-2000 — precisely during the era of market-oriented reform. Latin America's growth fell from 3.1% to 1.1%. Sub-Saharan Africa grew at 0.2% per capita over three decades.

The logic: infant industries, like children, need protection before they can compete with adults. Forcing premature competition destroys nascent industries before they acquire competitive capabilities. This is Hamilton's "infant industry" argument — first articulated by the founding US Treasury Secretary.

### Key Examples

- **The US in 1880:** 40-55% industrial tariffs; rampant corruption; weak intellectual property rights; discrimination against foreign capital. By today's IMF criteria, a governance and policy failure. In reality, one of the fastest-growing economies in world history.
- **"Dead presidents don't talk":** Hamilton ($10) designed American protectionism; Lincoln ($5) raised tariffs to their highest levels during the Civil War; Grant ($50) explicitly said the US would adopt free trade only after "getting out of protection all that it can offer."
- **Britain's switch to free trade:** Britain adopted free trade in the 1860s — after its manufacturing dominance was absolute, when it no longer needed protection.
- **China and India:** The two fastest-growing developing economies during the neo-liberal era were precisely those that partially liberalised but refused full free-market orthodoxy.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter fundamentally challenges the legitimacy of IMF/World Bank conditionality and the Washington Consensus. It introduces "kicking away the ladder" — rich countries used the policies they now forbid developing countries to use. This historical hypocrisy has cost developing countries decades of foregone growth.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- Historical success with protectionism does not mean all protectionism works. Many developing country import substitution programmes were corrupt and inefficient.
- East Asian success required specific institutional conditions (disciplined bureaucracies, export targets, competitive pressure) that may not be replicable everywhere.
- Some trade liberalisation has produced genuine gains even in developing countries.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Directly connects to Thing 1 (free trade prescription is a political choice, not economic law), Thing 11 (Africa's stagnation largely explained by structural adjustment), Thing 12 (industrial policy works — governments can pick winners), Thing 19 (successful development involves indicative planning), Thing 23 (East Asian miracles run by lawyers and engineers, not free-market economists).

## THING 8: Capital Has a Nationality

**Memory Hook:** *When Daimler took over Chrysler, the board went from half-American to 90% German within years.*

### Central Thesis

Despite globalisation rhetoric, most transnational corporations remain firmly based in their home countries for the most valuable activities: strategic decision-making, high-end R&D, and top employment. Treating foreign and domestic investment as equivalent is a policy mistake.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

Capital is truly transnational. Corporations locate activities wherever returns are highest. National policies favouring domestic over foreign investment are futile and counterproductive.

### Chang's Counterargument

Home-country bias is strong and persistent because a company's core capabilities — knowledge, routines, management networks, institutional relationships — are deeply embedded in home-country context and cannot easily be transplanted. When a foreign firm acquires a domestic company, the highest-value activities gradually migrate to the acquirer's home country.

### Key Examples

- **Chrysler:** Under Daimler (German), board became 90% German. Under Cerberus (American), mostly American. Under Fiat (Italian), Italians came to dominate. Nationality of ownership = nationality of management = nationality of benefit.
- **Carlos Ghosn:** The Lebanese-Brazilian CEO of Renault-Nissan is explicitly an "exception that proves the rule." Truly transnational management is rare.
- **R&D home bias:** Most transnationals conduct 60-90% of R&D in their home country.
- **Lord Mandelson:** The arch-free-trader admitted that Britain's permissive attitude toward foreign ownership might harm UK manufacturing.

### Why This Chapter Matters

"It doesn't matter who owns the factory as long as it creates jobs" is dangerous oversimplification. Ownership nationality matters for where strategic decisions are made, where high-value employment is created, and which communities capture corporate success.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Connects to Thing 7 (industrial policy requires national decision-making capacity), Thing 9 (de-industrialisation partly reflects failure to maintain nationally rooted industrial development), Thing 18 (what is good for a company is not always good for the host economy).

## THING 9: We Do Not Live in a Post-Industrial Age

**Memory Hook:** *Switzerland — supposedly a service economy — is actually one of the world's most industrialised countries per capita.*

### Central Thesis

Rich countries have not entered a post-industrial stage in which manufacturing is irrelevant. Most apparent de-industrialisation reflects falling relative prices of manufactured goods (due to faster productivity growth in manufacturing), not actual decline in manufacturing output. Manufacturing remains the engine of productivity growth and export capacity.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

Manufacturing has naturally declined with rising prosperity; people demand more services. Knowledge-based services have replaced manufacturing as the engine of growth. Developing countries can leapfrog industrialisation and go straight to services.

### Chang's Counterargument

What looks like shrinking manufacturing is partly a statistical illusion. Manufacturing productivity grows faster than service productivity — computers get faster and cheaper while haircuts remain expensive relative to labour costs. This makes manufacturing's share of GDP shrink in monetary terms even as physical output grows. Adjusting for relative price changes, manufacturing's real share has declined far less than reported.

No country has become rich primarily on services. Switzerland and Singapore, often cited as post-industrial service economies, are actually among the world's most industrialised per capita.

### Key Examples

- **Computers vs. haircuts:** Computer prices fall dramatically each year; haircut prices do not. So it looks like we spend proportionally less on computers and more on haircuts — even though we consume more computers than ever. Much of de-industrialisation is this relative price effect.
- **Britain's manufacturing:** Manufacturing share fell from 37% to 13% of GDP in current prices, but much less in constant prices.
- **Switzerland:** Famous for banking and tourism. In reality, one of the world's highest per capita manufacturing outputs. Novartis, Nestle, ABB, Swatch — all manufacturing.
- **Singapore:** Also among the world's five most industrialised economies by manufacturing value-added per capita.

### Why This Chapter Matters

The neglect of manufacturing in the US and UK has produced persistent trade deficits, stagnant wages, and vulnerability to financial sector failures. For developing countries, the post-industrial fantasy is actively dangerous: services have lower productivity growth, are less tradable, and high-value services depend on manufacturing knowledge.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Links to Thing 4 (the internet economy myth contributed to complacency about manufacturing), Thing 8 (foreign ownership cannot substitute for nationally rooted industrial development), Thing 17 (knowledge economy narrative overstates education's role), Thing 18 (GM's decline illustrates what happens when industrial firms abandon manufacturing for finance).

## THING 10: The US Does Not Have the Highest Living Standard in the World

**Memory Hook:** *Americans work 30% more hours than Norwegians — per hour worked, the US ranks eighth, not first.*

### Central Thesis

While US per capita income in purchasing power parity terms is among the world's highest, it is a misleading indicator of living standards. The US has far greater inequality, longer working hours, worse health and crime statistics, and lower social mobility than comparable countries with larger welfare states.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

The US has the world's highest living standard (adjusting for purchasing power), proving the superiority of free-market capitalism.

### Chang's Counterargument

PPP income per capita overstates US living standards because: (1) extreme inequality makes the average unrepresentative of how most people live; (2) cheap US services reflect cheap poorly-protected labour and cheap immigrant workers, not genuine efficiency; (3) Americans work 10-30% more hours than most Europeans. Per hour worked, the US ranks eighth among rich nations.

### Key Examples

- **Hours worked:** Americans work roughly 30% more than Dutch or Norwegian workers. Per PPP income per hour worked, the US ranks eighth — behind Norway, France, Belgium, Austria, Netherlands, Luxembourg, and Ireland.
- **Health and crime:** Despite the highest average income, the US ranks around 30th in life expectancy and infant mortality. Prison population: 8x Europe, 12x Japan per capita.
- **Cheap US services:** The US is unusual among rich countries in that PPP income approximately equals market exchange rate income. In other rich countries, PPP income is lower (expensive services). The US is different because service workers are cheap — through low-wage immigration and weak labour protections.

### Why This Chapter Matters

Demythologises the American model and introduces multidimensional welfare measures. High average income is not the same as broadly shared prosperity. The American model's apparent success depends substantially on inequality and overwork.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Links to Thing 3 (US cheap services partly from immigration), Thing 13 (high inequality makes average income unrepresentative), Thing 21 (European welfare states explain non-income welfare differences).

## THING 11: Africa Is Not Destined for Underdevelopment

**Memory Hook:** *Africa grew at a respectable 1.6% per capita in the 1960s-70s with all its "structural" problems. The problems didn't change in 1980 — the policies did.*

### Central Thesis

Africa's growth failure since 1980 is not primarily explained by structural factors (geography, climate, ethnic diversity, resource curse, culture). All these factors existed when Africa was growing at 1.6% per capita per year in the 1960s-70s. The primary explanation is the shift to free-market policies imposed through structural adjustment programmes (SAPs). Policy — not destiny — explains the stagnation.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

Africa is held back by structural handicaps: tropical diseases, landlocked geography, resource curse, ethnic divisions, colonial legacy producing bad institutions, and a culture unsuited to development. These make standard development prescriptions insufficient; Africa needs special aid and trade concessions.

### Chang's Counterargument

Every "structural" factor cited for Africa's failure has been present — often in more severe form — in today's rich countries at comparable levels of development. Landlocked Switzerland and Austria are among the world's richest countries. Finland and Sweden thrived despite arctic climates. Belgium, Switzerland, and Spain have serious ethnic and religious divisions. Bad institutions were universal before economic development — they are a symptom, not a cause.

Most importantly, if structural factors explained Africa's failure, growth should have been consistently poor. Instead, it collapsed in the 1980s — exactly when structural adjustment programmes began. The prime suspect is policy change, not geography or culture.

Structural adjustment programmes forced African countries to liberalise trade suddenly, collapsing nascent manufacturing sectors, and to cut government spending, undermining infrastructure. Countries were pushed back to relying on commodity exports, which then faced supply-gluts as multiple countries simultaneously increased exports of the same commodities (e.g., coffee, cocoa), driving prices down.

### Key Examples

- **Africa's growth record:** 1.6% per capita annual growth in the 1960s-70s (respectable); -0.7% in the 1980s-90s; 0.2% average for 1980-2009. The collapse correlates precisely with structural adjustment.
- **Switzerland and Austria:** Landlocked, cold, resource-poor — wealthy. Climate and geography are not destiny.
- **Rwanda:** Nearly as ethnically homogeneous as South Korea — yet experienced genocide. "Ethnicity" is a political construct, not a natural constraint on development.
- **Culture as scapegoat:** Until the early twentieth century, the Japanese were described by Western observers as lazy, the Germans as too individualistic and emotional to develop economically. Culture is shaped by economic development, not the reverse.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter is a direct critique of the "structural" fatalism used to justify continuing failed policies in Africa. If the problem is policy rather than geography or culture, then policy change can fix it. Africa is not condemned to poverty.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- Some structural factors are real even if not determinative — malaria, landlockedness, and fragile states impose genuine costs.
- Structural adjustment programmes varied; some countries implemented reforms better than others.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Links directly to Thing 7 (same free-market policies that failed in Africa also slowed growth in Latin America), Thing 12 (African governments were prevented from pursuing industrial policy that worked elsewhere), Thing 23 (orthodox economic advice has been harmful, not helpful).

## THING 12: Governments Can Pick Winners

**Memory Hook:** *POSCO — described as the worst business proposal in history — became one of the world's great steel companies.*

### Central Thesis

Contrary to free-market theory, governments can and do pick industrial winners — sometimes spectacularly. The claim that government decisions are necessarily inferior to business decisions is logically flawed and empirically false. Proximity to a situation does not guarantee good decisions, private firms make catastrophic errors too, and what is good for an individual firm may not be good for the national economy.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

Governments lack the information, incentives, and capabilities needed to make good business decisions. Political motivations lead them to prefer prestige over profit. The result is always "white elephant" projects — expensive failures that distort the economy. Government should not try to pick winners because it will always pick losers.

### Chang's Counterargument

The empirical record is clear: governments have picked spectacular winners. POSCO (Korea's state-owned steel company, now one of the world's largest and most efficient); LG Group (pushed into electric cables by the government, which became the foundation of its world-leading electronics business); Hyundai shipbuilding (created under government pressure, now one of the world's biggest). France's post-war industrial planning turned it into Europe's second industrial power. The US government funded the research that created computers, the internet, semiconductors, GPS, and biotechnology.

The argument that government cannot have better information than firms is also logically flawed: proximity to a situation can impair judgement (the AOL-Time Warner merger, the worst deal of the century, was enthusiastically endorsed by the "insiders"). Governments can acquire good information through state-owned enterprises, mandatory reporting requirements, or deliberation councils (Japan's famous MITI model).

The key insight is that what is good for an individual firm may not be good for the national economy — industrial policy is justified precisely when this divergence exists.

### Key Examples

- **POSCO (1968):** A state-owned steel company led by a former army general, making a product Korea was supposedly unsuited for, importing all raw materials from halfway around the world. The World Bank advised donors not to support it. Today it is the world's fourth-largest steel company.
- **LG Group:** Banned from its desired textile business, forced into electric cables — which became the foundation of its world-leading electronics empire.
- **Hyundai shipbuilding:** The founder initially refused. He relented when the dictator personally threatened his business group. Now one of the world's biggest shipbuilders.
- **US government R&D:** 47-65% of total US R&D in the 1950s-80s was government-funded. Computer, internet, GPS, semiconductors, biotech — all seeded by public money.
- **Concorde (failure):** The British-French supersonic aircraft was a technological triumph and a commercial disaster. Governments fail too — but so do private firms (AOL-Time Warner, Nokia N-Gage, Microsoft Vista).

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter directly rehabilitates industrial policy as a legitimate and potentially effective tool. It challenges the claim that state ownership and intervention are inherently inefficient. The relevant question is not whether governments can pick winners (they obviously can) but how to improve their "batting average" — which requires good information channels, clear performance criteria, and willingness to exit failures.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- For every POSCO there is a Concorde or an Indonesian aircraft industry. Spectacular government failures are real.
- Institutional prerequisites for successful industrial policy — disciplined bureaucracies, performance metrics, capacity to exit failures — may not be present everywhere.
- East Asian success occurred partly under authoritarian regimes; democratic contexts may produce different political economy dynamics.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Directly links to Thing 7 (industrial policy was central to all today's rich countries' development), Thing 18 (regulation that constrains individual firms can serve the national economic interest), Thing 19 (successful development combines planning and markets), Thing 23 (East Asian miracles ran on pragmatic, non-dogmatic economic management).

## THING 13: Making Rich People Richer Doesn't Make the Rest of Us Richer

**Memory Hook:** *The Golden Age of Capitalism — highest growth ever — occurred when the rich got the smallest slice.*

### Central Thesis

"Trickle-down economics" — enriching the wealthy to generate investment and growth that benefits everyone — has failed on its own terms. Since the 1980s, pro-rich policies have increased inequality without generating faster growth. Investment has actually fallen despite rising profits.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

You must create wealth before distributing it. The rich invest; the poor consume. Tax cuts for the rich increase returns to capital, stimulate investment and growth, and eventually benefit everyone through higher wages and more jobs. Taxing the rich to fund the poor reduces incentives and shrinks the pie.

### Chang's Counterargument

The empirical record directly contradicts this. The top 1%'s share of US income doubled from 10% to 23% between 1979 and 2006. Yet investment as a share of GDP fell across all G7 economies. World per capita income growth slowed from over 3% in the 1960s-70s to 1.4% after 1980.

The Golden Age of Capitalism (1950-73) — the fastest growth period in rich countries' history — occurred during the era of most progressive taxation and strongest welfare states. The classical assumption that capitalists invest while workers consume has been superseded: financialised capitalists are as likely to distribute profits as dividends and share buybacks as to invest them.

Even when upward redistribution does generate growth, trickle-down requires an active pump — the welfare state — to distribute those gains. Without it, the top 10% of US income-earners captured 91% of income growth between 1989 and 2006.

### Key Examples

- **US income distribution:** Top 1% doubled their income share 1979-2006. Top 10% captured 91% of income growth 1989-2006.
- **Investment vs. profit:** Rising profit shares since 1980 have NOT been matched by rising investment. Investment/GDP in the US fell from 20.5% to 18.7%.
- **Golden Age data:** Per capita income growth: 2-3% in US and UK, 4-5% in Western Europe, 8% in Japan — all during the era of high taxes and strong welfare states that free-marketeers say kills growth.
- **Pre-tax inequality:** Before taxes and transfers, Germany and Belgium are MORE unequal than the US. After redistribution, they are more equal. The welfare state is the "electric pump" that makes trickle-down work.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter directly refutes the central justification for tax cuts, deregulation, and welfare state reduction. Inequality and slow growth have gone together since the 1980s — the opposite of what trickle-down theory predicts.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- The Golden Age may have been exceptional for non-distributional reasons (post-war reconstruction, baby boom, stable energy prices).
- Some inequality may be necessary for entrepreneurial incentives.
- The relationship between inequality and growth is genuinely complex and context-dependent.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Closely linked to Thing 2 (shareholder capitalism is the primary mechanism driving inequality), Thing 6 (pro-rich macroeconomic policy is part of the same package), Thing 20 (equality of opportunity without some equality of outcome is hollow), Thing 21 (the welfare state is the pump that makes trickle-down work).

## THING 14: US Managers Are Over-Priced

**Memory Hook:** *American CEOs earn up to 20x their Japanese counterparts while running companies that consistently lose to Japanese competitors.*

### Central Thesis

US executive pay has increased roughly ten times in relative terms since the 1960s without any commensurate increase in performance. American CEOs are paid vastly more than counterparts in comparable countries while running companies that frequently underperform those rivals. This is explained not by market forces but by the growing economic and political power of the managerial class.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

High executive pay reflects genuine market forces. Top talent is scarce; for giant corporations, a better CEO can generate hundreds of millions in extra value. Attempts to cap executive pay are misguided populism that would drive talent away.

### Chang's Counterargument

If executive pay were determined purely by market forces, the CEO:worker pay ratio would not have jumped from 30-40:1 in the 1960s-70s to 300-400:1 today in the US. It would not diverge so sharply from other rich countries: Swiss CEOs earn 64% of American, German 55%, Swedish 44%, Japanese 25% (excluding stock options — including them, Japanese CEOs might earn as little as 5% of their American counterparts). Yet Japanese and German companies consistently beat American competitors.

The rise in US executive pay is better explained by the growing power of the managerial class to manipulate corporate governance: interlocking directorships, capture of compensation committees, and the use of economic and political influence to resist any accountability. The 2008 financial crisis revealed this power: almost no bankers responsible for the crisis were punished, and compensation caps at bailed-out institutions were successfully resisted.

### Key Examples

- **Historical comparison:** US CEO:worker pay ratio rose from 30-40:1 to 300-400:1 since the 1960s. American workers earn only 15% more than counterparts in comparable countries; American CEOs earn at least 100% more (excluding stock options) — up to 20x more than Japanese counterparts.
- **Jill Barad/Mattel:** When forced to resign for poor performance, the board gave her forgiven loans and additional payments worth over $30 million on top of contractual severance — pure generosity with shareholders' money.
- **RBS/Fred Goodwin:** The disgraced CEO of the bailed-out Royal Bank of Scotland received a £15-20 million pension payout while taxpayers paid for the rescue.
- **2008 financial crisis aftermath:** Few bankers who caused the crisis were punished. Congress's attempt to cap pay at bailed-out institutions was effectively resisted.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter shows that market outcomes can be systematically manipulated by those with sufficient economic and political power. It has implications for corporate governance reform and for the broader understanding of how inequality is produced — not just by technology and globalisation, but by power.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- Some genuinely exceptional executives may generate enormous value relative to their pay.
- International comparisons of executive pay are complicated by different compensation structures (stock options, deferred pay).
- Competitive pressure does eventually discipline some worst cases.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Links to Thing 2 (CEO pay is a product of the shareholder-manager alliance), Thing 5 (pay structures based on pure self-interest assumptions produce self-interested executives), Thing 13 (CEO pay inflation is one primary driver of rising income inequality).

## THING 15: People in Poor Countries Are More Entrepreneurial Than People in Rich Countries

**Memory Hook:** *Bangladesh has ten times more self-employed people than the US — yet it is poor. Individual entrepreneurship alone is not the answer.*

### Central Thesis

The poverty of developing countries is not caused by a lack of individual entrepreneurial energy. People in poor countries are more entrepreneurially active by any measure. What is missing is not individual drive but collective institutions — large firms, industry associations, cooperatives, functioning legal systems, financial infrastructure — that enable individual energy to be channelled into productive activity.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

Entrepreneurship is the engine of development. Poor countries are poor because their people lack the entrepreneurial spirit. Policy should focus on enabling individual entrepreneurs, especially through microcredit and the removal of obstacles to starting businesses.

### Chang's Counterargument

Self-employment rates in developing countries are 30-88% of the non-agricultural workforce, compared to 6-13% in rich countries. Bangladeshis are ten times more entrepreneurially active than Americans. Yet Bangladesh is poor and the US is rich. This "paradox" resolves when you recognise that individual entrepreneurship without collective organisation produces limited productivity growth.

The microfinance experience illustrates the limits: microcredit at market rates requires 40-100% interest rates because subsidies are needed to make lower rates viable. Few businesses can cover such rates. And even when individual businesses succeed, the "fallacy of composition" means that when everyone opens a mobile phone kiosk, they all earn less. Individual entrepreneurship cannot break through technological and organisational constraints.

Rich countries are rich because they have giant firms, cooperative industrial districts, and collective research and training infrastructure — not because they have more entrepreneurs.

### Key Examples

- **Self-employment rates:** US 7.5%; Bangladesh 75.4%; Benin 88.7%. Poor countries have far more entrepreneurs proportionally — and those entrepreneurs operate under much harder conditions.
- **Grameen "telephone ladies":** When Grameen Bank financed women to buy mobile phones and rent them to villages in 1997, early participants earned $750-$1,200 per year (national average ~$300). By 2005, so many had entered the market that income fell to $70/year. The fallacy of composition: what works for a few cannot work for everyone.
- **Dairy cooperatives:** Danish and Dutch dairy farmers organised into cooperatives with state support and became world leaders. Balkan dairy farmers, each working alone with microcredit, did not.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter challenges the "entrepreneurship-led development" paradigm and the entire microfinance movement's premise. It shifts focus from individual to collective action, from microcredit to institution-building, from entrepreneurial culture to industrial organisation.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- Self-employment in poor countries often reflects necessity rather than entrepreneurial ambition ("survival entrepreneurship").
- Some microcredit programmes have genuinely helped specific groups, particularly in terms of women's empowerment.
- Collective institutions themselves require individual initiative to build.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Links to Thing 3 (individual productivity is inseparable from collective infrastructure), Thing 12 (state support is essential to building the collective institutions that make entrepreneurship productive), Thing 17 (education alone cannot substitute for productive organisations), Thing 19 (the modern economy is organised within firms, not purely through markets).

## THING 16: We Are Not Smart Enough to Leave Things to the Market

**Memory Hook:** *Even Nobel Prize-winning financial economists couldn't correctly model their own investments — twice.*

### Central Thesis

Free-market economics assumes people are rational — that they know their interests and can calculate how to achieve them. This assumption is false. Human rationality is "bounded"; the world is too complex for us to fully understand. Paradoxically, this is an argument *for* well-designed regulation: rules that restrict complexity actually help people make better decisions.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

People know their own circumstances best and should be free to act on them. Government intervention is based on inferior information and cannot improve on individual market decisions. The market's "invisible hand" aggregates dispersed knowledge that no central authority can replicate.

### Chang's Counterargument

Herbert Simon's concept of "bounded rationality" shows that rational choice theory is built on a false premise. People cope with complexity not by calculating optimal solutions but by using rules of thumb, habits, and deliberate restrictions on their own freedom of choice — a chess master uses heuristics to navigate 10^120 possible positions, not comprehensive calculation.

This has a profound implication for regulation: regulations often work not because the government knows better than individuals but because they simplify the environment, allowing individuals to make better decisions. If financial products were limited to those that experts can understand, the 2008 crisis would not have occurred — not because regulators were smarter than bankers, but because the complexity that defeated everyone would not have been allowed to accumulate.

### Key Examples

- **Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM):** Founded by Nobel laureates Merton and Scholes using their own prize-winning asset-pricing model. Went spectacularly bankrupt in 1998, nearly taking the financial system down.
- **Scholes's second fund (Platinum Grove):** Went bust in 2008. "Even a monkey can fall from a tree. When it happens twice, you know the first wasn't a mistake."
- **AIG's CFO:** Six months before AIG's $180 billion government bailout, the CFO said it was "hard to even see a scenario in any realm of reason that would see us losing one dollar" on their credit default swap portfolio.
- **Queen Elizabeth at the LSE:** After the 2008 crash, the Queen asked a Cambridge professor: "How come nobody could foresee it?" The assembled experts had no good answer.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter provides the intellectual foundation for financial regulation. It replaces the free-market argument ("leave markets alone because people know best") with a complexity argument ("regulate because people cannot cope with unlimited complexity"). It also supports consumer protection laws, drug safety regulations, and other restrictions that reduce cognitive burden.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- Governments are also bounded-rational actors; they cannot necessarily design regulations that reduce complexity effectively.
- Some regulatory complexity actually increases cognitive burden on individuals.
- The argument can justify broad paternalism; the line between beneficial simplification and excessive restriction is hard to draw.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Links to Thing 5 (people are neither fully rational nor purely self-interested), Thing 22 (financial innovations that no one understood created 2008 crisis conditions), Thing 19 (planning within firms addresses the same complexity problem that markets fail to handle alone).

## THING 17: More Education in Itself Is Not Going to Make a Country Richer

**Memory Hook:** *Switzerland had the lowest university enrolment rate in the rich world until the 1990s — and the highest productivity.*

### Central Thesis

There is remarkably little evidence that more education, by itself, leads to greater national prosperity. Most education does not directly raise worker productivity. The link between higher education and growth is especially tenuous. What determines national prosperity is not individual educational attainment but the quality of collective institutions and productive organisations.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

Education is the key to prosperity. The East Asian miracle was built on educational excellence. The knowledge economy makes education even more essential. Investment in education is the surest path to development.

### Chang's Counterargument

The historical data do not support the education-growth link. In 1960, Taiwan had a lower literacy rate than the Philippines but grew far faster. Korea had a lower literacy rate than Argentina but dramatically outperformed it. Sub-Saharan Africa's literacy rates rose from 40% to 61% between 1980 and 2004 while per capita income actually fell.

Switzerland had the lowest university enrolment rate in the rich world (10-15%) until the early 1990s, yet maintained one of the world's highest productivities. The "Swiss paradox" reveals that education serves a "sorting" function (signalling general intelligence and discipline to employers) more than a direct productivity function — and that when everyone goes to university, the signal inflates and everybody has to go just to maintain their position.

What really distinguishes rich from poor countries is how well they organise people into productive enterprises and support them with appropriate institutions (patent law, bankruptcy law, welfare state, industrial policy, R&D infrastructure).

### Key Examples

- **Taiwan vs. Philippines (1960):** Philippines had higher literacy (72% vs 54%) and higher per capita income ($200 vs $122). Today Taiwan's per capita income is ten times the Philippines'.
- **Korea vs. Argentina (1960):** Korea had lower literacy (71% vs 91%) and much lower income ($82 vs $378). Today Korea's income is three times Argentina's.
- **Switzerland:** University enrolment rates half the OECD average until the 1990s. Consistently among the world's top two or three in per capita manufacturing productivity.
- **"Sorting" function:** Higher education signals employability (intelligence, self-discipline) more than it teaches job-relevant skills. Once most people have degrees, the signal deflates and everyone must get postgraduate degrees — "degree inflation" with minimal productivity payoff.
- **Norwegian 8th-graders:** Citizens of the world's richest country (by market exchange rate) score below Lithuanian, Czech, Slovenian, and Serbian students in mathematics. Wealth does not require excellent maths scores.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter challenges the dominant development narrative prioritising human capital accumulation. It redirects attention from individual education toward collective organisation, industrial policy, and institutional development. It also raises uncomfortable questions about higher education expansion in rich countries — much of it may be an expensive zero-sum arms race.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- Education has important non-productivity benefits (civic engagement, personal fulfilment, health behaviours) that Chang partly acknowledges.
- The relationship between education and growth may operate with long lags.
- Switzerland's success partly reflects a high-quality apprenticeship system — still a human capital story, just a different kind.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Links to Thing 3 (rich-country workers' productivity advantage is primarily institutional, not educational), Thing 9 (the knowledge economy narrative obscures the continued importance of manufacturing), Thing 15 (collective institutions matter more than individual capabilities for development).

## THING 18: What Is Good for General Motors Is Not Necessarily Good for the United States

**Memory Hook:** *GM armed both sides in WWII, then spent decades avoiding making better cars — until it went bankrupt.*

### Central Thesis

The interests of a firm — even a very large and economically important one — are not the same as the interests of the national economy. Giving businesses maximum freedom can harm both the national economy and the firms themselves. Good regulation prevents firms from destroying collective resources they depend on, and forces investments that raise long-term collective productivity.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

Firms are at the heart of capitalism. What is good for business is good for the country. Government should give maximum freedom to firms; over-regulation stifles investment and innovation.

### Chang's Counterargument

GM provides the perfect case study. Rather than making better cars in response to competition from Japan and Germany, GM: lobbied for import quotas; shifted profits into its financial arm (GMAC); acquired foreign competitors to harvest their technologies rather than developing its own. These were individually rational short-term strategies for GM's managers and shareholders — but catastrophically bad for the US economy (which had to bail GM out for $57.6 billion) and for GM itself (which went bankrupt).

Good regulation often benefits business collectively even as it constrains individual firms: child labour laws raise long-run workforce quality; environmental regulations preserve common resources; training mandates prevent the free-rider problem; restrictions on aggressive bank lending prevent systemic collapse.

### Key Examples

- **GM's decline:** Dominant global car company for decades. Consistently chose financial manipulation and political lobbying over product investment. Went bankrupt 2009. US taxpayers spent $57.6 billion on bailout.
- **GM and Opel:** GM's Opel subsidiary supplied the German army with vehicles and weapons during WWII while GM also supplied the Allies — the ultimate example of corporate interest diverging from national interest.
- **South Korea's 299 permits:** Despite requiring 299 permits from 199 agencies to open a factory, South Korea grew at over 6% per capita for three decades. Many regulations were genuinely useful.
- **Fish farming analogy:** Individual fish farms benefit from intensive practices that degrade water quality for all fish farms. Regulation protecting the commons is pro-business for the sector collectively.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter rehabilitates the concept of economic regulation against "government is always the problem" narratives. The quality and purpose of regulation matters far more than the quantity, and pro-business can mean pro-regulation when regulations protect collective goods.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Links to Thing 2 (what is good for shareholders often isn't good for the firm or country), Thing 12 (government industrial policy can serve national interests that individual firms would not pursue), Thing 19 (even capitalist economies are heavily planned), Thing 22 (the financial sector's freedom to innovate created systemic crisis).

## THING 19: Despite the Fall of Communism, We Are Still Living in Planned Economies

**Memory Hook:** *A Martian looking at our economy would see large green (planned) areas connected by red (market) lines — not the reverse.*

### Central Thesis

The collapse of Soviet central planning does not mean planning has disappeared. Modern capitalist economies are extensively planned — by governments (through R&D funding, industrial policy, indicative planning, state-owned enterprises) and by corporations (which plan their activities in great detail, often across borders). The question is not "plan or market?" but "plan what, at what level, and how well?"

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

The failure of communism proved that planning is ineffective. Only decentralised market decisions can manage the complexity of a modern economy. The less planning, the better.

### Chang's Counterargument

Even the most "free-market" capitalist economies contain enormous amounts of planning. The US government funded 47-65% of all R&D in the 1950s-80s. Most capitalist governments own significant enterprises in key sectors. Many countries have practised explicit "indicative planning" (France in the 1950s-60s, Japan, Korea, Taiwan, Finland, Norway, Austria) with considerable success.

More fundamentally, modern corporations — the dominant actors in capitalist economies — are internally planned hierarchies. Between one-third and one-half of international trade consists of transactions within multinational corporations (planned transfers, not market exchanges). Herbert Simon's "Martian" thought experiment: an outside observer would see large planned organisations connected by market networks, not a world of atomistic market actors.

The failure of Soviet communism was the failure of comprehensive, legally binding central planning of an entire complex economy. Limited, non-binding, collaboratively designed planning can work very well within capitalism.

### Key Examples

- **US government R&D:** 47-65% of total US R&D in the 1950s-80s was government-funded. Computer, internet, GPS, semiconductor, biotech — all developed with government money.
- **Indicative planning successes:** France overtook Britain as Europe's second industrial power in the 1950s-60s through indicative planning. Finland, Norway, Austria, Japan, Korea, Taiwan all used it successfully.
- **Intra-firm trade:** 30-50% of international trade is transactions between different parts of the same corporation — planned by corporate hierarchy, not market forces.
- **Herbert Simon's Martian:** A visitor seeing our economy for the first time would describe it as an "organisational economy" — most activity happens within firms, not between them.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter rehabilitates planning as a normal and legitimate feature of capitalism. It shifts the debate from "plan vs. market" to "what kinds of planning, at what levels, and for what purposes?" — a far more useful frame for economic policy.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- Chang's definition of "planning" is broad enough to encompass almost anything, risking analytical emptiness.
- Corporate planning is disciplined by profit motives in ways that government planning is not.
- The Soviet failure does provide genuine lessons about limits of comprehensive central planning worth preserving.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Links to Thing 5 (firms work partly because employees are not purely self-interested — internal planning relies on non-market motivations), Thing 12 (industrial policy is one form of government planning that can be highly effective), Thing 23 (good economic management requires pragmatic, non-dogmatic approaches that combine market, planning, and institutional elements).

## THING 20: Equality of Opportunity May Not Be Fair

**Memory Hook:** *Equal opportunity to run a race is meaningless if some runners have sandbags on their legs.*

### Central Thesis

Equality of opportunity is necessary but not sufficient for a genuinely fair society. When some people face severe disadvantages from birth — poverty, malnutrition, poor schooling, discriminatory networks — formal equality of opportunity is hollow. A degree of equality of outcome (a floor below which no one falls) is necessary to make equality of opportunity real.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

What matters is equality of opportunity, not equality of outcome. Equalising outcomes reduces incentives and leads to inefficiency. The market provides the best mechanism for rewarding genuine merit.

### Chang's Counterargument

Equality of opportunity without any equalisation of starting conditions is not genuine equality. A hungry child who cannot concentrate in class does not have equal educational opportunity even if the schoolroom is formally open to all. Former South African black citizens may now legally attend any university, but if their schools are still poorly funded and their parents still unemployed, the formal right is meaningless for most of them.

International comparison of social mobility confirms this: Scandinavian countries (with large welfare states) have higher social mobility than the UK; the UK has higher mobility than the US. The stronger the welfare state, the more meaningful formal equality of opportunity becomes in practice.

### Key Examples

- **South Africa's "cappuccino society":** Post-apartheid formal equality of opportunity has produced a society with a mass of brown at the bottom, a thin white froth above, and a sprinkling of cocoa representing token black advancement. Formal rights without economic transformation produce little actual mobility.
- **Social mobility data:** Scandinavian countries > UK > US in social mobility. The stronger the welfare state, the more real the opportunity.
- **Alejandro Toledo:** For every poor Peruvian boy who makes it to Stanford (like former President Toledo), millions didn't even finish high school. One exception does not prove the system is fair.
- **Korean Confucian caste legacy:** Korea enforced caste restrictions on who could take civil service exams even more rigidly than China — sons of merchants and artisans were excluded regardless of ability. Only with industrialisation did genuine opportunity expand.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter provides the philosophical foundation for welfare state policies. It challenges the meritocracy narrative — the idea that current outcomes reflect genuine differences in talent and effort — by showing that outcomes are heavily shaped by starting conditions which are beyond individual control.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Links to Thing 3 (wages reflect collective systems, not just individual merit), Thing 13 (trickle-down has not produced real equalisation of opportunity), Thing 21 (the welfare state is the mechanism that makes real equality of opportunity achievable).

## THING 21: Big Government Makes People More Open to Change

**Memory Hook:** *Cars go faster because they have better brakes — not despite them.*

### Central Thesis

Counter-intuitively, large welfare states make economies more dynamic and open to change, not less. When people have a safety net — healthcare, unemployment insurance, retraining support — they are willing to take career risks, accept industrial restructuring, and change jobs. Without a safety net, rational people cling to existing employment at all costs, even when restructuring would benefit the economy overall.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

Big government creates dependency and reduces incentives to work. The welfare state makes workers less open to change, increasing resistance to industrial restructuring. High taxes reduce incentives to invest and create wealth. Countries with smaller governments are more dynamic.

### Chang's Counterargument

Korean youngsters flooding into medicine and law — not because they want to be doctors and lawyers but because those are the only secure jobs in a country with a minimal welfare state and extreme labour market flexibility — illustrates the problem. Flexibility without security misallocates talent.

Conversely, Scandinavian workers accept industrial restructuring more readily than American workers because losing a job in Sweden is not catastrophic: they keep healthcare, housing support, generous unemployment benefits (up to 80% of last pay), and access to government-funded retraining. American workers rationally resist change because losing their job means losing healthcare, potentially their home, and often their ability to re-enter the labour market.

The welfare state is "the bankruptcy law for workers" — it provides second chances, reducing the cost of risk-taking in career choices and allowing more efficient long-run talent allocation.

### Key Examples

- **Korean medicine obsession:** 80% of Korea's top science students want to study medicine — not for intellectual passion but for job security in a country with minimal welfare. This misallocates enormous talent (engineers, scientists) into medicine.
- **US trade protectionism:** US workers demand protection from import competition because job loss means catastrophic personal consequences. European workers are more accepting of restructuring because welfare states buffer the cost.
- **Scandinavian growth:** Finland (2.6% per capita 1990-2008) and Norway (2.5%) — both with very large welfare states — outgrew the US (1.8%) during the supposed "American Renaissance."
- **Welfare state as bankruptcy law:** Modern bankruptcy law gives entrepreneurs a second chance by limiting personal liability — enabling risk-taking. The welfare state does the same for workers.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter inverts the standard argument about the welfare state. Rather than being a drag on economic dynamism, a well-designed welfare state actively promotes it by enabling risk-taking and reducing resistance to necessary restructuring.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- Poorly designed welfare states can create dependency and reduce work incentives.
- Fiscal sustainability of large welfare states becomes challenging as populations age.
- The Scandinavian model requires specific institutional features (active labour market policies, universal rather than means-tested benefits) that may be hard to replicate elsewhere.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Links to Thing 13 (welfare state is the "pump" that makes trickle-down work), Thing 20 (welfare state makes real equality of opportunity possible), Thing 23 (countries with strong welfare state automatic stabilisers managed the 2008 crisis better).

## THING 22: Financial Markets Need to Become Less, Not More, Efficient

**Memory Hook:** *A financial market that moves faster than real businesses can respond is an engine without brakes.*

### Central Thesis

The rapid development of financial markets has not improved the allocation of resources to productive investments. Instead, the hyper-efficiency of financial markets — their ability to respond instantly to any profit opportunity — has made them destructive to the real economy. Financial markets need to be deliberately slowed down to allow long-term productive investment to take place.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

Financial innovation and liberalisation improve capital allocation, enable risk diversification, and allow economies to respond quickly to changing opportunities. Sophisticated financial systems are the key to prosperity. Regulating financial markets stifles innovation and reduces efficiency.

### Chang's Counterargument

The ratio of financial assets to GDP in the world rose from 1.2 to 4.4 times between 1980 and 2007. In the US, financial assets reached over 900% of GDP. This enormous financial superstructure on top of the same underlying real economy did not reflect genuine productivity gains — it reflected the creation of increasingly complex and opaque financial instruments that few people understood.

The fundamental problem is the "speed gap" between finance and the real economy. Building a world-class company takes decades. Financial assets can be moved in milliseconds. This mismatch means that finance, dominated by short-term thinking, starves real businesses of the "patient capital" they need for long-term investment. The efficiency of financial markets is precisely what makes them destructive — they make it easy to capture short-term gains while undermining long-term value creation.

### Key Examples

- **Iceland:** Privatised and liberalised banks 1998-2003. Banking assets reached 1,000% of GDP by 2007. All three major banks went bankrupt in 2008. Economy shrank 8.5% in 2009. McDonald's closed its Icelandic outlets.
- **CDO alphabet soup:** Mortgages — mortgage-backed securities — CDOs — CDOs-squared — CDOs-cubed — credit default swaps. The same underlying house backing an incomprehensible stack of leveraged financial instruments. "Weapons of financial mass destruction" (Warren Buffett — before the 2008 crisis).
- **Financialisation of industry:** In 2004, 80% of GM's profits came from GMAC (financial arm). In 2003, 45% of GE's profits came from GE Capital. Manufacturing companies became finance companies.
- **James Tobin's "sand in the wheels":** Nobel laureate Tobin argued for a financial transaction tax to deliberately slow financial flows — "excessively efficient international money markets" need to be slowed down.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter provides the most direct analysis of what caused the 2008 financial crisis. It shows that financial "efficiency" — the ability to move capital quickly — is not the same as economic efficiency (allocating resources to their most productive uses). It argues for a fundamental restructuring of the relationship between finance and the real economy.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- Some financial innovation genuinely does improve capital allocation (index funds, improved payment systems).
- "Slowing down" finance is technically complex: Tobin-tax proposals have faced significant implementation challenges.
- The relationship between financial development and real growth is complex; some degree of financial sophistication clearly aids development.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Links to Thing 2 (short-termism in corporate governance is amplified by impatient financial capital), Thing 6 (financial deregulation is part of the same neo-liberal policy package), Thing 16 (financial complexity exceeded everyone's ability to understand or regulate it), Thing 18 (financialised companies like GM abandoned real-economy activities).

## THING 23: Good Economic Policy Does Not Require Good Economists

**Memory Hook:** *Japan's MITI was staffed by lawyers; Korea's industrial drive was designed by an engineer; Taiwan's economic officials were scientists. All ran economic miracles.*

### Central Thesis

The most successful economies in modern history — the East Asian "miracles" — were managed primarily by lawyers, engineers, and scientists, not by free-market economists. Free-market economics as practised over the last three decades has not produced good economic outcomes. Good economic management requires the right analytical tools and empirical knowledge — not adherence to free-market orthodoxy.

### What Free-Market Economists Claim

Developing countries need better-trained economists to design good economic policies. Free-market policies are superior because they are simpler and less demanding on bureaucratic capacity. The global diffusion of economics education has improved economic management.

### Chang's Counterargument

Japan's post-war economic miracle was run by lawyers (MITI bureaucrats). Korea's heavy industry drive in the 1970s was designed by Oh Won-Chul, an engineer. Taiwan and China's economic policies have been run by engineers and scientists. These countries achieved some of the greatest economic growth records in history — without relying on free-market economists.

Meanwhile, countries with many free-market economists — Latin American countries with "Chicago Boys" under Pinochet, and later much of sub-Saharan Africa — performed worse. The Keynesian, Listian, Schumpeterian, and Simonean schools of economic thinking that guided East Asian development have been far more practically useful than neoclassical free-market economics.

More seriously, free-market economists contributed to the conditions that caused the 2008 crisis: providing theoretical justifications for financial deregulation, arguing markets are rational and self-correcting, and dismissing warnings from heterodox economists about systemic risks.

### Key Examples

- **MITI lawyers (Japan):** Japan's Ministry of International Trade and Industry — which coordinated industrial policy during the miracle years — was staffed primarily by lawyers.
- **Oh Won-Chul (Korea):** The engineer who designed Korea's heavy and chemical industry drive in the 1970s transformed Korea from a low-wage exporter into a world-class industrial power.
- **Taiwan and China:** Economic policies run by engineers; both have extraordinary growth records.
- **Queen Elizabeth at the LSE (2008):** After the crash, the Queen asked: "How come nobody could foresee it?" The assembled experts from the most prestigious economics faculty in Britain had no good answer.
- **Chicago Boys in Chile:** Some of the world's most credentialed free-market economists ran Chile's economy under Pinochet. Chile has respectable but not exceptional development performance.

### Why This Chapter Matters

This chapter challenges the economics profession's intellectual monopoly on policy advice. It argues that practical economic management requires good analytical tools, empirical knowledge, and good institutional design — not adherence to a particular theoretical framework that has demonstrably failed. It also rehabilitates heterodox schools of economic thought (Keynes, List, Schumpeter, Simon, Hirschman) that have been marginalised by free-market orthodoxy.

### Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- East Asian economies also benefited from specific historical circumstances (Cold War US support, post-war reconstruction dynamics) not available to today's developing countries.
- The absence of trained economists in government does not prove economics is useless — correlation is not causation.
- Some heterodox economists are as dogmatic in their own way as free-market economists.

### Connections to Other Chapters

Links to Thing 7 (the economic advice given to developing countries was the opposite of what rich countries used), Thing 11 (Africa's growth failure partly reflects imposition of orthodox economic advice), Thing 12 (successful industrial policy requires empirical, pragmatic approaches), Thing 19 (good planning involves multiple disciplines, not just economic dogma).

## PART TWO: MAJOR THEMES, SYNTHESIS, AND CHANG'S OVERALL ARGUMENT

---

### The Major Recurring Themes of the Book

#### Theme 1: The Political Nature of Markets

The single most fundamental theme is that all markets are politically constituted. There is no neutral "free market" against which government intervention can be measured as distortion. Market boundaries — what can be traded, who can participate, on what terms — are always the product of political choices. Free-market economists who claim political neutrality are themselves making political choices, typically ones that favour existing economic power. This theme runs through almost every chapter and is the epistemological foundation of the entire critique.

#### Theme 2: The Historical Hypocrisy of Rich Countries

Almost all today's rich countries achieved their wealth through state intervention, protectionism, and industrial policy — and then told developing countries to adopt the free-market policies they never used themselves. Chang calls this "kicking away the ladder." Britain liberalised trade only when it had absolute industrial dominance. The US was the world's most protectionist major economy for over a century. Japan, Korea, and Taiwan used dirigiste industrial policy. Yet these same countries, through the IMF, World Bank, and bilateral pressure, have imposed free-market orthodoxy on developing countries with predictably poor results.

#### Theme 3: The Failure of the Neo-Liberal Era

The period of free-market dominance (roughly 1980-2008) produced worse economic outcomes than the "mixed economy" era it replaced. Growth slowed in virtually all regions. Inequality rose sharply. Financial crises became more frequent. Investment fell. The single best counter-argument to free-market economics is the record of the thirty years in which it was most fully implemented.

#### Theme 4: Short-Termism and the Primacy of Finance

The financialisation of the economy — the growing dominance of financial interests over productive interests — has been a major driver of the problems Chang identifies. Shareholder value maximisation, executive pay structures based on stock options, financial market deregulation, and the growing power of finance over industrial companies have all combined to shorten time horizons, reduce investment, and ultimately undermine the productive capacity of capitalist economies.

#### Theme 5: The Collective Nature of Productivity and Prosperity

Individual productivity is inseparable from the collective systems within which individuals operate. Workers in rich countries earn more not purely because of their individual abilities but because of collectively built infrastructure — technologies, institutions, legal systems, educational systems. Individual entrepreneurs in poor countries are not poor because of lack of personal initiative (they are more entrepreneurially active than their rich-country counterparts) but because collective institutions are underdeveloped.

#### Theme 6: Bounded Rationality and the Limits of Market Self-Regulation

People are not fully rational. The world is too complex for anyone — including the world's smartest financial engineers and Nobel Prize-winning economists — to model adequately. This is an argument for deliberate simplification through regulation, not for unregulated complexity. Well-designed rules reduce the cognitive burden on market participants and enable better decisions.

#### Theme 7: The State as Essential Partner of the Market

Throughout the book, Chang argues that successful capitalism has always involved an active state: funding R&D, building infrastructure, nurturing infant industries, providing social insurance, managing industrial policy. The question is not whether the state should be involved but how to make its involvement effective. The dichotomy between "state" and "market" is false — successful economies combine both in complex, mutually reinforcing ways.

---

### Chang's Overall View of Capitalism

Chang is a pragmatic, reformist defender of capitalism who wants to make it work better. His position:

1. **Capitalism is the best available system.** Its combination of profit incentives, price signals, and competitive discipline has generated more economic progress than any alternative yet tried.

2. **But capitalism needs active management.** Left entirely to its own devices, capitalism tends toward short-termism, financial instability, worsening inequality, and periodic crises. These are not accidents; they are structural tendencies that require institutional correction.

3. **There are many ways to organise capitalism.** American capitalism, Scandinavian capitalism, German capitalism, and East Asian developmental capitalism are all capitalist systems but produce very different outcomes. There is no single optimal model.

4. **The version of capitalism dominant since the 1980s is particularly bad.** Free-market or neo-liberal capitalism has produced slower growth, greater inequality, more financial instability, and worse development outcomes than the more managed capitalism of the post-war period. The 2008 crisis is its most dramatic failure, but the underlying trends were visible throughout the period.

5. **Better capitalism is possible.** Active industrial policy, stronger labour protections, regulated finance, redistribution through progressive taxation and welfare states — these do not destroy capitalism. The Golden Age of Capitalism (1950-73) demonstrated that high growth and broadly shared prosperity are achievable with managed capitalism.

---

### Chang's Criticism of Free-Market Ideology

Chang's critique of free-market ideology operates on several levels:

**Empirical failures:** The track record of the neo-liberal era (1980-2008) is comprehensively worse than the mixed-economy era it replaced, on virtually every metric: growth, investment, stability, inequality, development.

**Historical falsification:** The free-market narrative about how rich countries developed is flatly wrong. They used protectionism, industrial policy, and state intervention. The advice given to developing countries is the opposite of the policies rich countries used to develop.

**Logical flaws:** The core assumptions of free-market economics are demonstrably incorrect. Markets are not objectively defined; people are not fully rational; humans are not purely self-interested; the "free market" is not neutral. Each of these foundational assumptions fails on close examination.

**Ideological function:** Free-market ideology serves the interests of specific groups — financial asset holders, top managers, and established corporate interests — while being dressed up as scientific neutrality. The political choices embedded in "free market" prescriptions systematically favour capital over labour, finance over industry, and the short term over the long term.

---

### Chang's View on Key Economic Actors and Domains

#### The State

Chang's view of the state is neither naive statism nor reflexive anti-statism. The state can fail, sometimes spectacularly. But the premise that markets are naturally efficient and state intervention is naturally distorting is false. States have been essential to virtually every major development success in economic history. The question is how to design state interventions that are effective and accountable — not whether to have them at all.

Active state roles that Chang defends:
- Industrial policy and infant industry protection
- Funding of basic and applied R&D
- Provision of public infrastructure
- Regulation of financial markets to slow them down to the pace of the real economy
- Welfare state provision to enable risk-taking and genuine equality of opportunity
- Indicative planning to coordinate long-term investment

#### Firms and Corporate Governance

Chang sees the standard Anglo-American corporate governance model — shareholder primacy, aggressive cost-cutting, stock option pay — as fundamentally flawed. Better models involve longer-term ownership structures (founding family stakes, cross-shareholding, state ownership in key sectors), worker representation in governance, and corporate cultures that treat employees as partners rather than costs to be minimised.

Successful firms, across cultures, are built on trust, professionalism, and long-term investment — not on the assumption that everyone is purely self-interested. The best management thinking has always known this; the worst mainstream economics has obscured it.

#### Labour and Workers

Workers are stakeholders in firms, not just factors of production. They invest firm-specific skills and have long-term interests in the firm's viability that typically exceed those of floating shareholders. Worker representation in governance (as in German co-determination), strong labour protections, and welfare state provision all serve economic as well as social goals: they encourage investment in firm-specific skills, enable industrial restructuring, and promote the trust-based organisational culture that underpins productivity.

The welfare state is not charity for the poor — it is a productivity-enhancing institution that provides second chances (like bankruptcy law for businesses), enables career risk-taking, and reduces resistance to the industrial restructuring that capitalism requires.

#### Development and Poor Countries

Poor countries are poor primarily because their collective institutions — firms, financial systems, legal infrastructure, public administration — are underdeveloped, not because their individual people lack talent or entrepreneurial drive. Development policy should focus on building these collective institutions rather than on removing "distortions" or expanding microcredit to individual entrepreneurs.

The most successful development experiences (East Asia) have involved active industrial policy, infant industry protection, disciplined state support for strategic industries, and the gradual building of world-class firms. Developing countries should be given the "policy space" to use these tools rather than being locked into free-market orthodoxy by international conditionality.

#### Education

Education has genuine social value — for personal fulfilment, citizenship, and social mobility. But its effect on economic growth is modest and complicated. More important than the educational level of individual workers is the quality of the organisations and institutions that deploy those workers. Countries should invest in education for the right reasons (human development) not because it is a simple formula for economic growth.

#### Finance

Finance is essential for capitalism — it enables the pooling of capital, the management of risk, and the long-term investment that productive firms need. But "more finance" and "more financial freedom" are not always better. The hyper-efficient, hyper-liquid financial system created since the 1980s has shortened investment horizons, destabilised economies, and ultimately caused the catastrophic 2008 financial crisis. Finance needs to be regulated to serve the real economy rather than to extract value from it.

#### Inequality

Rising inequality in the neo-liberal era is not an incidental side-effect of otherwise successful policies. It is a product of deliberate structural changes — weaker labour protections, lower top tax rates, financial deregulation, shareholder primacy — that have systematically shifted income and wealth toward the top. This inequality has not generated the promised investment and growth; it has instead coincided with lower growth, lower investment, and greater financial instability. Moreover, extreme inequality undermines both the social mobility that justifies inequality's incentive effects and the genuine equality of opportunity that a fair society requires.

---

### Final Synthesis: The Big Picture

Chang's book can be understood as making one fundamental argument with many supporting sub-arguments:

**The fundamental argument:** The free-market narrative — that unregulated markets are efficient, that they generated prosperity for rich countries, that the path to development is to adopt them, and that all departures from them are politically motivated distortions — is empirically false, historically inaccurate, logically flawed, and in practice serves narrow class interests rather than the common good.

**The alternative vision:** Capitalism is a powerful but inherently unstable and inherently unjust system when left to itself. It requires active state management — industrial policy, regulated finance, labour protections, welfare states, and some redistribution — to produce broadly shared prosperity, stable growth, and sustainable development. This is not a vision of socialism; it is a vision of managed capitalism of the kind that produced the Golden Age, the East Asian miracles, and the welfare state societies of northern Europe.

**The political challenge:** The main obstacle to better capitalism is not technical — economists do not lack the knowledge to design better institutions. The obstacle is power: the financial sector, top managers, and those who benefit from existing distributions of income and wealth have the economic and political power to defend arrangements that serve their short-term interests, even when those arrangements harm the long-run interests of the whole economy, including themselves.

**The call to action:** Active economic citizenship — understanding how economies actually work rather than how free-market ideology says they work — is the precondition for demanding better arrangements from governments, corporations, and international institutions.

### 20 Most Important Ideas in the Whole Book

1. **All markets are political.** There is no such thing as an objectively defined "free market." Every market rests on political choices about boundaries, participants, and conditions. Free-market advocacy is itself a political position, not a neutral scientific stance.

2. **The neo-liberal era (1980-2008) produced slower growth, not faster.** World per capita income growth fell from over 3% in the 1960s-70s to 1.4% after 1980, precisely during the period of free-market reforms.

3. **Rich countries did not become rich through free trade and free markets.** They used protectionism, industrial policy, and active state intervention — then told developing countries to do the opposite.

4. **Shareholder value maximisation has harmed companies, workers, and the economy.** Shareholders are the least committed stakeholders; prioritising them produces short-termism, underinvestment, and eventually company failure.

5. **Immigration control is the world's biggest labour market distortion.** The wage gap between rich and poor countries is primarily explained by immigration restrictions, not genuine productivity differences. Free-market economists who ignore this are being intellectually inconsistent.

6. **People are not purely self-interested, and economies depend on goodwill.** The "work to rule" strike proves that normal economic output depends on voluntary effort beyond what contracts require. Designing for pure self-interest produces it.

7. **Trickle-down economics has failed empirically.** Enriching the wealthy since the 1980s has not generated faster growth or higher investment. The Golden Age of highest growth occurred when inequality was lowest.

8. **Financial markets need to be slowed down, not speeded up.** The "speed gap" between financial assets and real productive investment makes hyper-efficient financial markets destructive to long-term economic health.

9. **Human rationality is bounded.** The 2008 financial crisis was caused partly by the fact that financial complexity exceeded everyone's — including experts' — ability to understand. This is an argument for regulation that limits complexity.

10. **Governments can pick winners.** POSCO, LG, Hyundai, the internet, semiconductors — all produced partly through deliberate state industrial policy. The "government cannot pick winners" claim is empirically false.

11. **Africa is not destined for underdevelopment.** Africa grew at 1.6% per capita in the 1960s-70s with all its "structural" problems. Growth collapsed when free-market structural adjustment policies were imposed. Policy, not geography or culture, explains the stagnation.

12. **The welfare state makes economies more dynamic, not less.** By providing second chances and buffering the costs of industrial restructuring, welfare states enable risk-taking and reduce resistance to change. The Scandinavian countries outgrew the US after 1990 despite (or because of) their larger welfare states.

13. **De-industrialisation is mostly a price effect, not a real decline.** Manufacturing's apparent shrinkage is largely because manufacturing productivity grows faster (making goods cheaper) than service productivity. Real manufacturing output has declined much less than the GDP share suggests.

14. **Education's impact on growth is weak and indirect.** Switzerland had the lowest university enrolment rate in the rich world until the 1990s — and one of the world's highest productivities. Collective organisational capabilities matter far more than individual educational attainment.

15. **Capital has a nationality.** Most transnational corporations keep their highest-value activities — R&D, strategic decisions, senior management — in their home countries. Treating foreign and domestic investment as equivalent is a policy mistake.

16. **The US does not have the highest living standard in the world** once inequality, long working hours, health outcomes, and social mobility are factored in. The American model is a template for high average income with low average welfare.

17. **We still live in planned economies.** Between government R&D funding, state-owned enterprises, industrial policy, and internal corporate planning, modern capitalist economies are extensively planned. The question is not "plan or market?" but "plan well or badly?"

18. **Equality of opportunity requires a floor of equality of outcomes.** Formal equal rights are meaningless without the material conditions needed to exercise them. The welfare state is the precondition for genuine equal opportunity.

19. **CEO pay is political, not market-determined.** The ten-fold increase in US CEO pay relative to workers since the 1960s reflects growing managerial class power, not a ten-fold increase in productivity. Comparable companies in Germany and Japan pay CEOs a fraction of US rates.

20. **Good economic policy does not require free-market economics.** Japan, Korea, and Taiwan ran their miracles with lawyers and engineers. Free-market economists' track record — slower growth, more crises, greater inequality — is worse than the heterodox alternatives they displaced.

### 10 Strongest Examples Used by the Author

1. **The US in 1880 vs. today's "basket cases" (Thing 7):** The US at that time had 40-55% industrial tariffs, rampant corruption, weak property rights, and discrimination against foreign capital — exactly what the World Bank today calls a governance failure. Yet it was one of the fastest-growing economies in world history. This single comparison demolishes the Washington Consensus.

2. **POSCO: The "worst business proposal in history" (Thing 12):** The Korean state-owned steel company that every donor refused to fund — wrong country, wrong product, wrong management, wrong inputs — became one of the world's four largest and most efficient steel producers. Direct empirical refutation of the claim that governments cannot pick winners.

3. **The "dead presidents" (Thing 7):** Hamilton, Lincoln, Grant, and Washington — every president on US paper money — was a committed protectionist. The country that today most loudly advocates free trade was built entirely on protectionist foundations. The hypocrisy is historically documented on every dollar bill.

4. **GM's buybacks and bankruptcy (Things 2 and 18):** GM spent $20.4 billion buying back its own shares between 1986 and 2002 — money that would have prevented its 2009 bankruptcy. Shareholder value maximisation destroyed one of the world's greatest companies.

5. **LTCM's collapse — twice (Thing 16):** When Nobel Prize-winning economists Merton and Scholes, using their own award-winning pricing models, managed funds that went spectacularly bankrupt — not once but twice — the irrationality assumption underlying free-market finance theory is exposed at its most extreme.

6. **Swedish vs. Indian bus driver (Thing 3):** The wage gap of 50:1 between an Indian and Swedish bus driver cannot be explained by productivity differences (the Indian driver navigates cows and rickshaws). It is explained by immigration controls. This single comparison reveals the political nature of labour markets.

7. **Iceland's financial rise and collapse (Thing 22):** A country of 300,000 people that built a banking sector ten times its GDP, then suffered an 8.5% economic contraction when it collapsed. Iceland is an extreme but pure case of what financial deregulation does when taken to its logical conclusion.

8. **The Grameen telephone ladies (Thing 15):** When Grameen Bank financed women to rent out mobile phones in 1997, early adopters earned $750-$1,200 per year. By 2005, market saturation drove income to $70 per year. The "fallacy of composition" — what works for a few cannot work for everyone — explains why microfinance cannot be a general development strategy.

9. **Washing machine vs. internet (Thing 4):** The household appliance revolution transformed female labour force participation, family structure, and social mobility on a scale the internet has not yet matched. This counterintuitive comparison challenges the recency bias that drives misguided policy.

10. **South Africa's "cappuccino society" (Thing 20):** Post-apartheid formal equality of opportunity has produced a society with a mass of black at the bottom and thin layers of white and mixed-race at the top. Formal rights without economic transformation produce minimal actual mobility — the most powerful illustration of why equality of opportunity alone is insufficient.

### 10 Possible Essay Questions with Model Answer Directions

**Essay Question 1:**
*"Free markets are the most efficient way to allocate resources." Critically evaluate this claim with reference to Ha-Joon Chang's arguments.*

**Model Answer Direction:**
- Start by noting that Chang does not dispute that markets are powerful allocation mechanisms; his target is the claim that markets are *self-evidently optimal* when unregulated.
- Address Thing 1: there is no objectively defined "free market" — all markets are politically constituted. The claim presupposes a neutral benchmark that does not exist.
- Address Thing 16: market participants are not fully rational. Bounded rationality means complex financial markets can produce catastrophic outcomes even when everyone is trying to act in their best interests.
- Address Thing 5: markets depend on non-market motivations (trust, professionalism, loyalty) to function. Pure self-interest would grind economies to a halt.
- Address empirical record (Thing 7, 13): the neo-liberal era of relatively free markets produced slower growth, greater inequality, and more financial crises than the preceding era of managed markets.
- Conclude: markets are valuable but inherently unstable coordination mechanisms that require active institutional management to serve broad social welfare.

**Essay Question 2:**
*"The history of economic development shows that free trade is the path to prosperity." Assess this view.*

**Model Answer Direction:**
- Core argument from Thing 7: virtually all today's rich countries became rich through protectionism and industrial policy, not free trade.
- Use the "dead presidents" example: US protectionism 1830s-1940s; British protectionism 1720s-1850s; German, Japanese, Korean, Taiwanese industrial policy.
- Distinguish between free trade as theory (promoting comparative advantage) and free trade as historical practice (abandoned by all rich countries during their developmental phases).
- Address "kicking away the ladder" — the imposition of free trade on developing countries contradicts the actual developmental experience of rich countries.
- Discuss exceptions: some small, resource-rich, or uniquely positioned economies may develop differently.
- Conclude with the empirical record: developing-country growth was faster in the "protectionist" 1960s-70s than in the "liberalising" 1980s-2000s.

**Essay Question 3:**
*"Shareholder value maximisation is the best governance principle for large corporations." Discuss.*

**Model Answer Direction:**
- Explain the intellectual case for shareholder primacy (Thing 2): shareholders bear residual risk, aligning their interests with corporate performance.
- Then deploy Chang's counter: shareholders are actually the *least* committed stakeholders because of their ease of exit. Workers and suppliers have larger long-term stakes.
- Document the historical rise of shareholder value maximisation since the 1980s and its consequences: rising executive pay, increasing dividend payouts, share buybacks, declining investment, weakening of long-term capabilities.
- Use the GM example: decades of shareholder-friendly behaviour (buybacks, financial diversification, lobbying) rather than product investment; ended in bankruptcy.
- Compare with the German, Japanese, and Swedish governance models: longer-term ownership structures produce more durable companies with stronger investment patterns.
- Conclude: shareholder primacy has served managers and short-term shareholders while harming companies, workers, and the economy.

---

**Essay Question 4:**
*"Poor countries are poor because their people lack education, entrepreneurship, and good institutions." Critically evaluate this claim.*

**Model Answer Direction:**
- Address entrepreneurship (Thing 15): people in developing countries are MORE entrepreneurially active than those in rich countries (30-88% self-employment vs. 6-13%). Lack of individual entrepreneurship is not the constraint.
- Address education (Thing 17): the evidence linking education to economic growth is weak. Taiwan had lower literacy than the Philippines in 1960 but dramatically outperformed it. Switzerland had the lowest university enrolment in the rich world and the highest productivity.
- Address institutions (Thing 11): today's rich countries had far worse institutions at comparable levels of development than today's poor countries. Institutions are largely outcomes of development, not preconditions.
- Address what actually matters (Things 12, 15, 19): collective institutions — large firms, cooperatives, industrial associations, state support for key industries, R&D infrastructure — are the main differentiators.
- Address policy environment (Things 7, 11): structural adjustment policies imposed on Africa and Latin America actively destroyed nascent industrial capacity.
- Conclude: the proximate factors cited are symptoms, not causes. Policy and institutional design, not individual deficiencies, explain development outcomes.

---

**Essay Question 5:**
*"Greater inequality is the price we must pay for economic dynamism." Evaluate this argument.*

**Model Answer Direction:**
- State the standard argument: tax cuts for the rich increase returns to investment; inequality reflects and rewards differential productivity; redistributive policies reduce incentives.
- Deploy empirical counter from Thing 13: the Golden Age of Capitalism (highest growth in history) occurred during the period of LEAST inequality in rich countries. Growth slowed after the inequality-increasing neo-liberal reforms of the 1980s.
- Address investment: rising profit shares since the 1980s have NOT translated into higher investment. Investment as a share of GDP fell across all G7 economies.
- Address trickle-down failure: the top 10% captured 91% of US income growth 1989-2006. The welfare state "pump" is needed to distribute any gains.
- Address institutional effects (Thing 21): extreme insecurity (the other side of inequality) misallocates talent, as Korean youngsters all study medicine rather than engineering.
- Conclude: inequality and slow growth have gone together since the 1980s. The trade-off between equality and dynamism is not borne out by the evidence.

**Essay Question 6:**
*"Government industrial policy almost always fails and should be avoided." Assess this view.*

**Model Answer Direction:**
- State the standard critique (Thing 12 "what they tell you"): governments lack information, have wrong incentives (prestige over profit), and cannot bear financial consequences of bad decisions.
- Deploy counter-evidence: POSCO (Korea), LG, Hyundai, French indicative planning (1950s-60s), MITI Japan, Taiwanese SOEs, US defence R&D producing internet, computers, semiconductors, GPS, biotech.
- Address the "picking losers" examples: Concorde, Indonesian aircraft industry — but note that private firms also fail spectacularly (AOL-Time Warner, Nokia N-Gage, Windows Vista).
- Address the informational argument: proximity to a situation does not guarantee good decisions. Governments can acquire good information through deliberation councils (Japan MITI model), mandatory reporting, or state ownership.
- Address the national interest point: what is good for an individual firm is not always good for the national economy. This is precisely when industrial policy is justified.
- Conclude: governments can pick winners; the question is how to improve the "batting average" through good institutional design.

**Essay Question 7:**
*"The welfare state reduces economic dynamism by creating dependency and reducing incentives." Critically discuss.*

**Model Answer Direction:**
- State the standard argument: unemployment benefits reduce the pressure to work; high taxes reduce incentives for the wealthy; labour protections make hiring and firing costly.
- Deploy Chang's counter (Thing 21): welfare states provide "second chances" that enable risk-taking, just as bankruptcy law does for entrepreneurs.
- Use the Korean example: Korea's highly flexible labour market without adequate welfare has produced mass talent misallocation into secure professions (medicine, law) rather than dynamic ones.
- Compare US and European attitudes to trade liberalisation: US workers resist change because they cannot afford to lose their jobs; European workers accept restructuring because welfare states buffer the cost.
- Use Scandinavian data: Finland and Norway — two of the world's biggest welfare states — outgrew the US after 1990. Sweden performed similarly with the world's largest welfare state.
- Address the grain of truth: poorly designed, means-tested, stigmatising welfare states do create some dependency. The Scandinavian model of universal, activation-oriented welfare is different.
- Conclude: well-designed welfare states are complements to, not substitutes for, economic dynamism.

**Essay Question 8:**
*"The living standards of the world's poorest people are best served by expanding free trade and investment." Evaluate this view.*

**Model Answer Direction:**
- Note that Chang accepts some benefits from trade and investment.
- Address the historical evidence (Thing 7): developing-country growth was faster in the "protectionist" 1960s-70s than in the "liberalising" 1980s-2000s. Latin America and sub-Saharan Africa, which most faithfully adopted free-market reforms, grew slowest.
- Address structural adjustment's specific harms (Thing 11): sudden trade liberalisation destroyed nascent manufacturing sectors; budget austerity degraded infrastructure; commodity export promotion caused supply gluts.
- Address the nationality of capital issue (Thing 8): foreign direct investment tends to keep high-value activities at home; the "potato chips vs. microchips" problem means developing countries may get low-value activities.
- Address what actually works (Things 7, 12): infant industry protection, strategic industrial policy, state-owned enterprises in key sectors — policies used by all today's rich countries during their development.
- Address the asymmetry: rich countries retain policy space to bend WTO rules; poor countries are constrained by conditionality. This structural inequality needs to be corrected.
- Conclude: free trade and investment are not inherently harmful, but forcing developing countries to adopt full-scale free-market policies before they have built domestic industrial capacity is harmful.

---

**Essay Question 9:**
*"The 2008 financial crisis was an unavoidable shock caused by global imbalances. It could not have been prevented by better regulation." Critically evaluate this view.*

**Model Answer Direction:**
- Address the "could not have been foreseen" argument: some economists (Minsky, Kindleberger) did predict that financial liberalisation would produce periodic crises. The Queen's question to the LSE is the starting point.
- Address Chang's structural argument (Thing 22): the crisis was the inevitable product of the financial system that had been deliberately built over three decades — hyper-liquid, hyper-efficient, generating paper returns while starving the real economy of patient capital.
- Address the CDO/derivative alphabet soup (Thing 22): financial instruments of increasing complexity and opacity, built on the same underlying assets, created a superstructure that was inherently fragile.
- Address the LTCM/Nobel laureate problem (Thing 16): even the world's best financial models could not handle real-world complexity. This is an argument for banning instruments that no one fully understands.
- Address the historical context (Thing 6): the era of greatest financial stability (1945-mid 1970s) was also the era of tightest financial regulation. Banking crises surged as regulation was loosened.
- Conclude: the crisis was avoidable with better regulation. It was produced by deliberate policy choices — financial deregulation — that created the conditions for it.

---

**Essay Question 10:**
*"Education is the most important investment a country can make for long-term economic growth." Assess this claim with reference to empirical evidence.*

**Model Answer Direction:**
- State the standard argument: human capital theory — more education raises individual productivity, and aggregate human capital raises national productivity.
- Deploy Chang's counter (Thing 17): very little evidence links more education to faster growth. Sub-Saharan Africa's literacy rates rose from 40% to 61% while per capita income fell. Taiwan grew faster than the Philippines despite lower initial literacy.
- Use the Swiss paradox: lowest university enrolment in the rich world until the 1990s; one of the world's highest productivities. How?
- Address the "sorting" function: higher education signals employability (intelligence, discipline) more than it teaches job-relevant skills. Degree inflation is a zero-sum game.
- Address what the evidence does show matters: the quality of organisations and institutions that deploy educated workers — large firms, industrial policy, R&D infrastructure — is more important than the educational level of individual workers.
- Distinguish between education's intrinsic value (personal fulfilment, citizenship, health behaviours) and its instrumental value for economic growth. The first is real; the second is overstated.
- Conclude: education matters, but it is not a simple formula for growth. Collective organisational capacity and institutional quality matter more.

## 10-PAGE HIGH-DENSITY REVISION SHEET

### Core Framework: What Chang Is Arguing

**Chang's position:** Capitalism YES; free-market capitalism NO. Capitalism needs active institutional management to produce broadly shared prosperity. The version dominant since 1980 has failed on its own terms: slower growth, more inequality, more financial crises.

**The false dichotomy:** "Free market vs. government interference" is the wrong frame. All markets are politically constituted. The question is always: what rules, serving whose interests?

**The alternative:** Managed capitalism — combining markets, regulated finance, industrial policy, welfare states, and strategic state investment — produces better outcomes than laissez-faire. This is what actually produced the Golden Age and the East Asian miracles.

### Key Theoretical Concepts

**Infant industry argument (Hamilton):** Young industries need protection from competition before they develop the capabilities to stand on their own feet. This is why we send children to school rather than making them compete with adults in the labour market.

**Kicking away the ladder (List/Chang):** Rich countries used protectionism and industrial policy to develop, then told developing countries to adopt free trade and free markets — policies the rich countries themselves never used.

**Bounded rationality (Simon):** Human cognitive capacity is insufficient to process all relevant information. We cope by using rules of thumb and deliberately restricting our own choices. This is an argument for regulations that reduce complexity.

**Shareholder value maximisation:** The principle that companies should be run to maximise returns to shareholders. Chang argues this produces short-termism, underinvestment, and long-term company decline.

**Trickle-down economics:** The claim that making rich people richer will benefit everyone through higher investment and growth. Chang argues this has failed empirically.

**Speed gap:** The enormous difference between the speed at which financial assets can be rearranged (milliseconds) and the speed at which real productive investments can be made and generate returns (years to decades). This mismatch makes financial markets destructive to long-term investment.

**Fallacy of composition:** What works for a few cannot work for everyone simultaneously. The microfinance "telephone ladies" example: when one woman rents out a phone, she profits; when three hundred do, they all earn less.

**Home-country bias:** The tendency of multinational corporations to keep their highest-value activities (R&D, strategic decisions, senior management) in their home countries.

## 2-PAGE ULTRA-CONDENSED EXAM SHEET

### The Core Argument in One Paragraph

Ha-Joon Chang argues that free-market capitalism — the variant dominant since the 1980s — has produced slower growth, more inequality, and more financial instability than the managed capitalism it replaced. Its intellectual foundations are wrong: markets are politically constituted, not natural; people are neither fully rational nor purely self-interested; the "free market" narrative of how rich countries developed is historically false. The countries that grew fastest (East Asian miracles, post-war Europe) used active industrial policy, regulated finance, and welfare states. The countries that most faithfully adopted neo-liberal prescriptions (Latin America, sub-Saharan Africa) grew slowest. Better capitalism is possible — not socialism, but regulated, welfare-state capitalism with an active state.

### 5 Arguments That Recur Across Multiple Chapters

**1. Markets are political, not natural** (Things 1, 3, 5, 7, 13, 18): The fundamental critique — wherever free-market economists claim to be describing natural market outcomes, Chang shows political choices are embedded.

**2. Rich countries' historical practice contradicts their current advice** (Things 7, 9, 10, 11, 12, 19, 23): The policies that worked for developed countries (protectionism, industrial policy, planning, regulated finance) are the policies they tell developing countries to avoid.

**3. Short-termism vs. long-term investment** (Things 2, 6, 13, 14, 22): The financialisation of economies has systematically shortened time horizons and reduced investment in real productive capacity.

**4. Collective organisation trumps individual capability** (Things 3, 9, 15, 17, 19): Individual talent, education, or entrepreneurship cannot substitute for the collective institutions — firms, cooperatives, industrial policy, research infrastructure — that make individual capabilities productive.

**5. Institutions are outcomes as well as causes of development** (Things 7, 11, 17, 20, 21): The "institutional quality" argument for why poor countries are poor is circular: poor countries have poor institutions partly because they are poor. The direction of causality runs both ways.
