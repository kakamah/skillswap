## THE MASTER ARGUMENT (MEMORISE THIS FIRST)

> Mainstream textbooks present a conservative, market-fundamentalist ideology as value-free science. They: (1) define economics to favour efficiency over equity, (2) use perfect competition as a universal tool even though real competitive markets barely exist, (3) suppress contrary empirical evidence, (4) make corporate power invisible, and (5) ignore that externalities, inequality, and limited rationality make market failure the norm. The result is a political project disguised as economics.

## 7 CORE THEMES WITH CHAPTER LINKS

**1. IDEOLOGY AS SCIENCE** (All chapters)
The positive/normative distinction is fictitious. Every definitional choice is normative. The textbook's ideology: classical liberalism (individual property rights, minimal government, markets as default). Textbooks make this invisible by not acknowledging alternative paradigms.

**2. POWER IS INVISIBLE** (Ch. 1, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, PS)
Olson (1971): concentrated industries → collective action; dispersed citizens → rational ignorance. Corporate lobbying shapes regulation, deregulation, and tax law. This is a rational economic decision — the most profitable investment a firm can make — but textbooks treat it as outside economics.

**3. EXTERNALITIES ARE PERVASIVE** (Ch. 7 + links to 3, 4, 5, 9, 10, PS)
Not exceptional side effects but central features: pollution kills millions; asbestos kills thousands; financial risks are systemic; consumption competition is a welfare-destroying arms race. The textbook's placement of externalities at the back of the book is a structural choice, not a pedagogical one.

**4. COMPETITIVE MARKETS ARE RARE** (Ch. 3, 5, 6)
S&D = competitive model. Almost no real firms are price-takers. Applying S&D everywhere loads every analysis against government intervention. The model is an "enabling myth" (Stiglitz) that survives because it "has served certain interests."

**5. INEQUALITY COSTS ARE REAL AND IGNORED** (Ch. 1, 4, 9)
Three distinct harms: (a) Easterlin — relative position matters for well-being; (b) Marmot — social gradient of health kills people; (c) Olson/Galbraith — wealth concentration → political power → democratic corruption. All three ignored in standard texts.

**6. INFORMATION ASYMMETRIES UNDERMINE THE MARKET** (Ch. 3, 4, 5, 6, PS)
Stiglitz (epsilon argument): even tiny information imperfections push competitive markets to monopoly pricing. Information asymmetries are unavoidable — job applicants vs. employers, borrowers vs. lenders, patients vs. doctors. Perfect information is not a simplifying assumption; it is an impossible condition.

**7. EMPIRICAL EVIDENCE IS SUPPRESSED** (Ch. 2, 3, 4, 9, 10)
Minimum wage consensus broken down — not reported. Rent control empirical literature — not reported. Equity–efficiency trade-off not observed — not reported. Easterlin paradox — not reported. Social gradient of health — not reported. Pattern: contrary evidence is systematically omitted.

## KEY CONTRASTS FOR ESSAY WRITING

**Efficiency vs. Equity**: Textbook says there's a trade-off. Anti-text: Lindert shows no trade-off in practice; Marmot shows inequality has enormous costs; Okun's bucket is full of holes in the textbook but barely leaks in reality.

**Markets vs. Government**: Textbook: markets are default; government "interferes." Anti-text: markets require legal frameworks, trust, and regulation to function at all; "free markets" is a misleading term for heavily legally-constructed institutions.

**Growth vs. Well-being**: Textbook assumes growth = welfare. Anti-text: Easterlin paradox; time scarcity; positional arms races mean that in wealthy countries, growth may add no welfare while redistribution, leisure, and public goods might.

**Information**: Textbook assumes (or requires) perfect information. Anti-text: information asymmetries are unavoidable and are the key source of market failure in healthcare, finance, food, labour markets, insurance — essentially everywhere.

**Power**: Textbook: absent. Anti-text: explains why regulation is weak, inequality persists, wages deviate from productivity, financial crises occur, and trade rules favour the powerful.
