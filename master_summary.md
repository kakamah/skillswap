# 1. New Imperialism (c. 1870–1917)

---

## Overview: The Imperial System

**Concept Summary**

The Imperial Powers unit is best understood as **a single interacting system**: industrial capability + strategic geography + prestige politics + ideological legitimation + enabling technology produced rapid imperial expansion, especially after the 1870s. This expansion then fed back into great-power rivalry and diplomatic realignment.

The Scramble for Africa is the core laboratory: measurable acceleration from roughly **10% European control in the 1870s to ~90% by 1914** (Ethiopia and Liberia as the famous exceptions). A top answer must show *why* acceleration occurred — not merely *that* it happened.

**Model Judgement Line (memorise this):**
> *"Economic pressures made expansion attractive, but strategic chokepoints and prestige competition determined where and when empires acted; technology lowered costs, while diplomacy institutionalised competition — so imperialism was simultaneously a product of capability and insecurity."*

---

## The Big Causal Debates

### Debate 1: Was Imperialism Mainly Economic?

**Key Arguments**
- Industrial capitalism created demand for raw materials, markets, and investment outlets.
- Hobson (1902): imperialism serves sectional financial interests.
- Lenin (1916): "export of capital" becomes typical in late-stage monopoly capitalism.
- Economic logic explains *why expansion was attractive* but not always *why particular territories mattered immediately* (e.g., Suez/Egypt) or *why states would risk crises for marginal colonies*.

**Historical Evaluation**
- Top-band judgement: **economics = structural pressure; strategy/prestige = decision driver in key moments.** That is a judgement about causation hierarchy, not a "both mattered" summary.
- 🚨 **Trap:** Claiming "everything was economic." Economics explains background incentives more reliably than specific target selection. You must still explain why strategic chokepoints and prestige crises repeatedly appear.

---

### Debate 2: How Important Were Strategic Motives?

**Key Arguments**
- Strategy focuses on routes, bases, and signalling capacity.
- Suez Canal opening + Britain's control of Egypt: single chokepoint shifting imperial priorities.
- US strategy: infrastructure (Panama Canal) + bases + naval thinking transforms reach.
- A strong judgement distinguishes strategic **"needs"** (chokepoints, naval logistics) from strategic **"ambitions"** (status projection).
  - Britain's Egypt policy = the former; Germany's "place in the sun" = the latter.

**Historical Evaluation**
- Pure strategy can understate domestic politics and economic interest — why publics and elites were willing to pay costs.

---

### Debate 3: Nationalism and Prestige — Cause or Packaging?

**Key Arguments**
- **Prestige-as-cause:** "Great Power status" functioned like a currency in diplomacy and domestic legitimacy. Germany's *Weltpolitik* (contrasted with Bismarck's caution) illustrates a shift toward symbolic and global positioning.
- **Prestige-as-justification:** governments sell strategic/economic choices as national glory to mobilise support.

**Historical Evaluation**
- High-level evaluation treats prestige as **causal** when it changes risk tolerance (e.g., crises, hard-line stances) and as **rhetorical** when it follows rather than drives policy choices.

---

### Debate 4: Racial Ideology / "Civilising Mission" — Cause or Justification?

**Key Arguments**
- **Ideology-as-driver (limited):** Social Darwinism was a rationalisation for imperialist and racist policy; helped explain why coercion and violent "pacification" could be framed as moral duty.
- **Ideology-as-legitimation (stronger):** *The White Man's Burden* shows how empire was presented as moral responsibility, including in a US–Philippines context.

**Historical Evaluation**
- Strongest formulation: *"Ideology was rarely sufficient to trigger annexation alone, but it was crucial in sustaining empire and justifying coercion; it amplified other motives."*
- 🚨 **Trap:** Treating ideology as the main cause without proof. Use ideology as a legitimation mechanism (public consent), then test it against event choice (why Egypt?).

---

### Debate 5: Did Technology Make Imperialism Possible?

**Key Arguments**
- Headrick's work: 19th-century technologies (transport, communications, medicine, weaponry) functioned as "tools" enabling penetration and control.
- Technology **alters feasibility and cost**, not desire.
- Technology does not explain *why* states wanted empire; it explains *why* they could secure it rapidly.

**Historical Evaluation**
- Treat technology as a **multiplier**: raises the payoff of strategic and prestige decisions and lowers the risk — therefore *accelerating* the Scramble once competition begins.

---

### Debate 6: Did Imperial Expansion Create More Conflict or More Cooperation?

**Key Arguments**
- **Cooperation case:** Berlin Conference created shared rules; Fashoda ended without war and contributed to clearer understanding of spheres.
- **Conflict case:** Imperial incidents repeatedly harmed relations (Kruger Telegram provoking anti-German sentiment); colonial wars like the Boer War reveal costs and brutalities of rivalry.

**Historical Evaluation**
- Top judgement: imperialism created **"managed competition"** at the diplomatic level but **increased structural rivalry and suspicion**, raising the temperature of international politics.

## The General Scramble for Africa

### New Imperialism and the Scramble for Africa

**Concept Summary**

The Scramble accelerates under a **"security–prestige spiral"**: each acquisition raises fears of rival encirclement, making further acquisitions appear necessary. The acceleration from 10% → 90% European control demonstrates that explanation must include capability (technology, medicine, transport) *and* competitive pressure (prestige and rivalry), not only opportunistic trade.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Economic incentives create pressure → strategic geography channels urgency to specific chokepoints → prestige spiral converts "optional expansion" into "status necessity" → technology lowers barriers → Berlin Conference creates legal pathway → competitive annexation accelerates

**Key Arguments**
- Economic incentives explain background attractiveness; strategic geography and prestige explain urgency and timing.
- Berlin Conference (1884–85) did not stop imperialism — it **regularised and accelerated it** by clarifying what counted as a valid claim.
- The highest-level answers turn Berlin and Suez into **mechanisms** — rules and chokepoints that convert motives into action.

**Historical Evaluation**
- **Weak:** "Europe wanted resources and took Africa."
- **Strong:** "Economic incentives mattered, but crisis points and strategic geography determined urgency, while technology lowered barriers and Berlin created a legal pathway for competitive annexation."

📝 **Model Conclusion:** *"The Scramble for Africa was driven by a hierarchy of causes: economic pressures made expansion attractive, but strategic geography and prestige rivalry determined decisive choices; technology enabled speed and control, while ideology legitimised coercion — so empire expanded through a feedback loop of capability and insecurity."*

## Britain

### British Imperialism: Core Case

**Concept Summary**

Britain's empire was a global system; Africa mattered because it contained routes and nodes. The Suez Canal made Egypt pivotal for imperial communications. The core question: was British imperialism primarily **defensive** (protecting routes and empire) or aggressively expansionist — and how far did fear of rivals drive policy?

**Big-Picture Judgement:** Britain often acted to **defend an imperial network** — expanding at pressure points to prevent rival encirclement.

**Key Arguments**
- **Strategic imperialism:** securing the route to India via Egypt/Suez; ensuring naval mobility.
- **Competitive defence:** acquiring territories to block rivals (especially in East and Southern Africa).
- **Prestige and domestic politics:** imperial crises became reputational tests; "backing down" risked Great Power credibility.
- Britain's imperial choices were **region-specific:** Egypt/Suez = route security; East Africa = corridor defence against rivals; South Africa = resources + settler politics + prestige.

**Motives Ranked (typical top judgement):** Strategy (primary in Egypt/Suez) > Rivalry-prestige (primary in crises like Fashoda) > Economics (stronger in South Africa context).

**Historical Evaluation**
- Tensions/contradictions: moral mission language vs coercive practice; high cost and reputational damage in Boer War.
- The "extent" question often turns on whether you emphasise strategy or economics region-by-region.

---

### Suez Canal Opens (1869)

**Concept Summary**
New sea-level waterway connecting Mediterranean and Red Sea; shortened trade and military routes between Europe and Asia.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Long-term trade and strategic interest in faster routes → Canal opened 1869 → raised strategic stakes in Egypt → made control of Egypt/Suez a core imperial security issue → triggered later British occupation

**Key Arguments**
- Made leadership in Egypt/Suez a **core imperial security issue**; key "trigger" for later occupation.
- Shifts strategic geography — a single chokepoint can change imperial priorities.

**Historical Evaluation**
- Pivotal turning point: supports strategic motives argument, showing how geography converts motives into action.

---

### Britain Buys Suez Shares (1875)

**Concept Summary**
Britain purchased Egypt's significant shareholding in the canal company due to Egypt's financial troubles and Britain's strategic interest.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Egypt's financial crisis + Britain's strategic interest in canal → share purchase → Britain gained leverage within canal governance → "soft-power" step toward "hard-power" control

**Key Arguments**
- Classic example of the **informal-to-formal empire** trajectory.
- Bridges informal influence to formal domination.

---

### British Occupation of Egypt (1882)

**Concept Summary**
Military intervention leading to prolonged control/occupation following local unrest and strategic fears around Suez.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Local unrest + strategic fears around Suez + European influence competition → Britain invaded/occupied Egypt 1882 → secured Britain's position in canal zone → anchored British imperial route strategy → intensified rivalry and helped propel Scramble dynamics

**Key Arguments**
- Strategic control disguised by arguments of order and stability; becomes a "**fixed point**" in Britain's imperial geometry.
- Illustrates strategic "imperial defence" logic: control over a chokepoint triggers broader competitive moves.

**Historical Evaluation**
- Strong evidence for **strategic motives as primary driver**; supports rivalry escalation and "triggers of Scramble" arguments.

---

### Uganda and East Africa Protectorates + Uganda Railway (1896–1901)

**Concept Summary**
Protectorate establishment and railway construction linking the coast to the Great Lakes region.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Corridor strategy + need to consolidate influence against rivals → Britain formalised protectorates (Uganda 1894; East Africa Protectorate 1895) → built Uganda Railway (1896–1901) → strengthened administrative and military reach inland

**Key Arguments**
- Shows **technology as an imperial tool** — imperialism was not just annexation but infrastructure control.
- Railway supports arguments about strategic consolidation beyond conquest.

---

### First Boer War (1880–81) and Second Boer War (1899–1902) + Peace of Vereeniging (1902)

**Concept Summary**
British wars against Boer republics; the 1899–1902 war ended with a treaty bringing Transvaal and Orange Free State under British administration.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Tensions over independence, imperial control, and regional power; imperial ambition meets settler nationalism → prolonged warfare → British victory with controversial methods → reputational costs and diplomatic criticism

**Key Arguments**
- Demonstrates **imperial conflict cost**; affects diplomatic climate and public opinion on empire.
- Shows how strategic position and resource zones became linked.

**Historical Evaluation**
- British victory but with high material and reputational costs — contradicts the "easy" narrative of imperial expansion.

---

### Jameson Raid (1895–96) and Kruger Telegram (1896)

**Concept Summary**
Failed incursion to destabilise Transvaal; German Emperor Wilhelm II congratulated Boer leader Kruger, triggering British public hostility.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Imperial pressure + mining/settler interests in South Africa + rivalry sensitivity → Jameson Raid collapsed → Kruger Telegram sent by Germany → triggered public hostility in Britain → worsened Anglo-German relations

**Key Arguments**
- Strong evidence that **imperial incidents escalated great-power antagonism**.
- Demonstrates prestige politics: a colonial crisis becomes a metropolitan psychological turning point in Anglo-German relations.

**Historical Evaluation**
- 🚨 **Trap:** Treating Anglo-German rivalry as simply economic — the Kruger Telegram shows how symbolic gestures and prestige politics reshape the rivalry dynamic.

---

### Fashoda Incident (1898)

**Concept Summary**
Anglo-French crisis on the Upper Nile; near-war scare resolved diplomatically.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Competing corridor ambitions: Britain's north–south Nile linkage vs France's west–east vision → confrontation at Fashoda → French withdrawal + later agreements clarifying limits → acute tension but no war → step toward later Anglo-French diplomatic accommodation

**Key Arguments**
- Evidence of **both rivalry intensity and crisis management capacity**.
- Supports arguments about "managed competition" — rivalry reaching war-scare levels but resolved diplomatically.

**Historical Evaluation**
- Fashoda changed the risk of Anglo-French war in that theatre, but **did not end imperial competition overall**.
- 🚨 **Trap:** Confusing Fashoda as a "settlement of imperialism" — it clarified spheres but intensified the broader rivalry system.

📝 **Model Conclusion:** *"Fashoda demonstrates that imperialism produced 'managed competition' at the diplomatic level — rivalry intense enough to risk war, but controlled through negotiation — while structural antagonism persisted."*

## Germany

### German Imperial Expansion: Core Case

**Concept Summary**

Germany's empire-building must be read through its newly unified status. Under **Bismarck**, colonies were treated as costly and diplomatically risky. Later, under **Weltpolitik** impulses tied to industrial rise and status anxiety, colonies became symbols of world-power identity. Germany's empire was **disproportionately about status and domestic mobilisation**; colonies provided symbols of world power.

**Key Arguments**
- **Prestige/status:** "place in the sun" rhetoric indicates empire as **recognition strategy**, not merely profit.
- **Domestic politics:** colonial issues could mobilise nationalist sentiment and support pro-government blocs.
- **Economic interests:** present, but often weaker than status claims.
- **Diplomatic bargaining:** empire as leverage in negotiations rather than an end in itself.
- Gestures and crises (Kruger Telegram) **worsened rivalry even when colonial gains were limited**.

**Motives Ranked:** Prestige/status (often highest) + domestic politics; economics as secondary; diplomacy as tool.

**Historical Evaluation**
- Germany is a classic **"rising power status"** case: colonies had outsized symbolic value relative to material gain, making prestige a plausible primary driver.
- Key distinction: **Bismarck-era caution** vs. **later Weltpolitik ambitions**.

---

### German Unification (1871) and Colonial Entry (1880s–)

**Concept Summary**
Foundation of German Empire; later colonial expansion driven by new great power status and industrial rise.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- New great power status + industrial rise → pressure for international recognition → Bismarck-era cautious colonial additions → later Weltpolitik posture → Germany joins colonial competition → status-chasing dynamic feeds rivalries

---

### Berlin Conference (1884–85)

**Concept Summary**
Negotiations establishing rules for claims, especially the doctrine of **"effective occupation."**

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Rising risk of conflict over competing colonial claims → powers negotiate common framework → agreed criteria for recognising claims → standardised claim-making → institutionalised competition → **accelerated partition** by making "occupation" a race condition

**Key Arguments**
- Germany hosted the Conference, illustrating how it could **structure rules and legitimise claims** even as a colonial latecomer.
- Did not stop imperialism — it **regularised and accelerated it**.
- Creates a recognition framework: what counts as a valid claim, how rivals should be notified.

**Historical Evaluation**
- Best evidence for **cooperation vs conflict** debate: Berlin shows that managed competition existed, yet the rules it created intensified the competitive dynamic.

---

### German Colonies: Togoland (1884), Kamerun (1884), German East Africa (1880s–1891)

**Concept Summary**
German protectorates/colonies in West and East Africa; formal entry into African competition.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Latecomer empire-building + commercial agents + state recognition → German claims formalised → administration increasingly state-driven in East Africa → new rival presence → reinforces status narrative

**Key Arguments**
- Shows **company-to-state empire** pattern: private commercial agents precede formal annexation.

---

### Heligoland–Zanzibar / Zanzibar Treaty (1890) and Caprivi Strip

**Concept Summary**
Spheres-of-influence arrangement between Britain and Germany; defined spheres, Germany gained Heligoland and Caprivi Strip, Britain's Zanzibar authority recognised.

**Key Arguments**
- Evidence that imperialism could produce **negotiated cooperation even amid rivalry**.
- Complicates simplistic "constant rivalry" narratives about Anglo-German relations.

---

### "Place in the Sun" Speech (1897)

**Concept Summary**
Bernhard von Bülow's Reichstag speech calling for Germany's rightful status; becomes ideological marker of assertive Weltpolitik policy.

**Key Arguments**
- Programmatic marker of shift toward assertive global policy after Bismarck.
- Use as direct evidence for **prestige-driven imperialism**.

---

### Herero and Nama Genocide (1904–08)

**Concept Summary**
German colonial war and mass death in South West Africa; brutal suppression of resistance to colonial rule and dispossession.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Resistance to colonial rule + dispossession → brutal suppression with catastrophic mortality → colonial control through terror and coercion

**Key Arguments**
- Undercuts civilising claims; supports arguments about **violence as a core imperial mechanism**.
- Best evidence for **ideology vs reality** gap: shows how civilising mission rhetoric masked systematic coercion.

**Historical Evaluation**
- Widely characterised as genocide by scholars.
- 🚨 **Trap:** Treating ideology as a genuine cause of empire — Herero/Nama shows it was a justification for violence, not a guide to restrained governance.

## USA

### American Imperial Expansion: Core Case

**Concept Summary**

The Monroe Doctrine is not pure isolation; it asserts separate spheres and warns Europe against intervention in the Americas. The key historical change is the move from **hemispheric doctrine to overseas war and empire**, especially after 1898.

**Key Arguments**
- **Economic pressures in the 1890s:** crises strengthen expansionist arguments for markets (Panic of 1893).
- **Frontier closure thinking:** Frederick Jackson Turner (1893) frames expansion outward — expansionist imagination moves outward once continental limits are reached.
- **Navalism/sea power:** Alfred Thayer Mahan argued sea power underpinned national greatness; US policymakers applied lessons to bases and routes.
- **War-as-turning point:** 1898 conflict produced territories and forced a permanent debate over empire.

**Motives Ranked:** Strategic + navalism (bases, canal) + economic stress (1890s) + ideological justification; war as trigger.

**Historical Evaluation**
- Strong judgement frames US as moving to **selective imperial activism** rather than complete abandonment of restraint.
- 🚨 **Trap:** Using the weak binary "isolationist → interventionist." The US did not join European alliance systems but exercised regional hegemony and developed overseas reach through war, bases, law, and infrastructure.

---

### Monroe Doctrine (1823)

**Concept Summary**
Doctrine asserting non-colonisation/non-intervention principles; separation of hemispheres.

**Key Arguments**
- Essential for **continuity/change questions**: the Monroe Doctrine warns Europe against interference; the later Roosevelt Corollary reverses this to claim US right to intervene.

---

### Frontier Closure Discourse (1890s) and Turner Thesis (1893)

**Concept Summary**
Turner's argument that the frontier era closing changes American development trajectory; expansionist imagination moves outward.

**Key Arguments**
- Helps explain why overseas expansion became **imaginable and politically sellable**.
- Best used as supporting cause, not sole driver of US expansion.

---

### Panic/Depression of the 1890s (1893, 1896)

**Concept Summary**
Severe economic crises; strengthened arguments for overseas markets and national assertiveness.

**Key Arguments**
- One factor pushing policy debate toward expansion; best used as **supporting cause** rather than sole driver.

---

### Spanish-American War (1898) and Treaty of Paris (1898)

**Concept Summary**
War ending Spain's empire; US victory transferred Cuba (independence), Guam, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines — turning the US into an overseas colonial power.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Cuba conflict + imperial crisis + strategic and ideological currents → US-Spain war → US gains Guam, Puerto Rico, Philippines; Cuba independent → US becomes overseas colonial power → sets up later doctrines and base strategy

**Key Arguments**
- **Turning point** for "world power" claims.
- Produced territories and forced a permanent debate over empire.

---

### Philippine-American War (1899–1902)

**Concept Summary**
Following Spanish-American War, Filipino nationalists resisted US control, forcing coercive governance.

**Key Arguments**
- Shows that empire generated resistance and compelled coercive governance.
- Best for essays on "costs of imperialism" and **ideology-vs-reality** gap.

---

### Platt Amendment (1903)

**Concept Summary**
Treaty/provisions enabling extensive US involvement in Cuba; "formal independence, constrained sovereignty."

**Key Arguments**
- Best example for **informal empire** and "imperialism without outright annexation."
- Shows how legal constraints can substitute for direct territorial control.

---

### Panama Independence Recognition (1903) and Panama Canal (1903–1914)

**Concept Summary**
Canal built under Roosevelt; opened 1914; converted doctrine and ambition into effective global military and commercial mobility.

**Key Arguments**
- Strong evidence of structural world-power capacity — infrastructure enabling global reach.
- US strategy shows how infrastructure + bases + naval thinking **transforms reach**.

---

### Roosevelt Corollary (1904)

**Concept Summary**
Expansion of Monroe Doctrine claiming US responsibility/right to intervene to preserve order in the Western Hemisphere.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Concerns about instability and European debt interventions in Caribbean/Latin America → Doctrine tied order-preservation to US intervention → marks shift from *warning Europe* to *policing region*

**Key Arguments**
- **Key continuity/change turning point**: from defensive to interventionist posture.

## Japan

### Japan's Rise to World Power: Core Case

**Concept Summary**

Japan's rise is a **two-stage mechanism**: (1) state modernisation under Meiji created capacity; (2) war and diplomacy converted capacity into recognised status. The catalyst was fear of Western domination after forced "opening."

**Key Arguments**
- **Security against Western threat → prestige/status → strategic depth/resources**: motives ranked.
- **External shock → reform:** opening pressure destabilised old order and justified centralising reform.
- **War as status proof:** victories create international recognition and leverage.
- **Internal reform is the necessary cause; wars/alliance are the sufficient proof** that turns potential into recognised status.

**Motives Ranked:** Security against Western threat → prestige/status → strategic depth/resources.

**Historical Evaluation**
- Japan's rise proves the imperial system was not solely European.
- Modernisation for defence → imperialism **mirroring Western practice**; internal militarist emboldening after 1905.
- 🚨 **Trap:** Treating Japan's rise as only military. Make Meiji reforms and conscription the **necessary cause**; wars the proof.

---

### Perry Expedition (1853–54) and Treaty of Kanagawa (1854)

**Concept Summary**
US naval mission forcing the opening of Japan; first treaty with a Western power; ends seclusion.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Western demand for trade and coaling/supply points + gunboat diplomacy → Japan's ports opened for supplies → end of seclusion → Japan's sovereignty crisis accelerates reform pressures → root cause of Meiji transformation and later Japanese imperial logic

**Key Arguments**
- Crucial root cause: **external shock → reform** mechanism.

---

### Meiji Restoration (1868) and Conscription (1873 Marker)

**Concept Summary**
Political revolution restoring imperial system, dismantling feudal order, and enacting nationwide reforms including conscription.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Fear of Western encroachment after forced opening → centralisation and modernisation reforms → conscription instituted 1873 → increased state capacity and military strength → **necessary precondition** for later victories and world-power recognition

**Key Arguments**
- Conscription (1873) is a **key marker** for internal reform capacity.

---

### First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95), Treaty of Shimonoseki, and Triple Intervention (1895)

**Concept Summary**
Japan defeats China; treaty yields gains (Taiwan + concessions); European Triple Intervention forces retrocession of Liaodong Peninsula — turning victory into humiliation.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Rivalry over Korea and influence in East Asia → Japan defeats China → Treaty of Shimonoseki grants Taiwan and concessions → Triple Intervention forces Japan to retrocede Liaodong → victory becomes humiliation → strengthens motive for alliance and further militarisation

**Key Arguments**
- Bridges **internal reform to external assertiveness**.
- Triple Intervention: critical because it turns victory into humiliation, driving further militarisation and alliance-seeking.

---

### Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902)

**Concept Summary**
Alliance to safeguard interests in China and Korea, directed against Russian expansion.

**Key Arguments**
- Diplomatic recognition as a **near-equal partner** — key for "world power" criteria.
- Britain and Japan agree mutual support conditions, strengthening Japan's strategic position.

---

### Russo-Japanese War (1904–05), Mukden, Tsushima, Treaty of Portsmouth (1905)

**Concept Summary**
War over rival imperial ambitions in Manchuria and Korea; Japan's major victories forced peace.

**Mechanism (Cause → Event → Effect)**
- Rival claims in Manchuria and Korea + Russian expansion → war → major victories (Mukden; Tsushima naval battle) → Treaty of Portsmouth: affirmed Japanese presence in south Manchuria and Korea, ceded southern Sakhalin → Japan's prestige surged → Russia destabilised (1905 revolution linkage)

**Key Arguments**
- Best "world-power by 1905" evidence: Japan defeats a **European great power**.
- Treaty of Portsmouth: landmark proof — recognition and regional primacy formalised.

**Historical Evaluation**
- 📝 **Model Conclusion:** *"Japan's victory in 1904–05 completed the two-stage mechanism of imperial rise: Meiji reforms created the capacity; the Russo-Japanese War converted that capacity into internationally recognised great-power status — making Japan's rise both internally driven and externally validated."*

---

### Korea Annexation (1910)

**Concept Summary**
Formal annexation and colonial rule; confirms Japan as an imperial power using European-style colonial methods.

**Key Arguments**
- Confirms Japan as an **imperial power** using European-style colonial rule.
- Shows consolidation of a formal empire after 1905 recognition.

## Comparative Framework

### Comparing Imperial Motives and Methods

| Power | Primary Motive | Method | Key Turning Point(s) | Key Tension |
|-------|---------------|--------|---------------------|-------------|
| **Britain** | Route security (strategic) | Protectorates, administration | Suez 1869; Egypt 1882; Fashoda 1898 | Moral mission vs coercive practice |
| **Germany** | Prestige/status recognition | Company-to-state annexation | Berlin Conference 1884–85; "Place in the sun" 1897 | Symbolic value vs limited material gain |
| **USA** | Hemispheric hegemony → Pacific reach | Annexation + treaty-based informal control | Spanish-American War 1898; Panama Canal 1914 | Anti-imperialist ideals vs colonial realities |
| **Japan** | Security → status equality | War-as-proof + diplomatic alliance | Meiji 1868; 1902 Alliance; Portsmouth 1905 | Reform for defence → imperialism mirroring West |

---

### Britain vs Germany (Motives)

- Britain often acts from **imperial network defence** (routes, chokepoints).
- Germany's late empire often reflects **status competition and domestic mobilisation**.
- This changes how you **rank factors**: strategic necessity tends to dominate Britain's Egypt policy; prestige logic tends to dominate Germany's late-1890s global posture.

### Britain vs Germany (Rivalry)

- Kruger Telegram shows how colonial incidents become **metropolitan hostility and psychological escalation**.
- Direct evidence for "imperial rivalry increased tensions" essays.

### Britain vs USA (Imperial Methods)

- Britain: long-term administration and protectorates (**formal empire**).
- US: blend of annexation (territories) with treaty-based control (Cuba via Platt Amendment) (**formal + informal empire**).
- This is a comparison of **instruments**, not a ranking of "who was more imperialist."

### USA vs Japan (Emerging Powers)

- Both use **war as turning point** (1898; 1904–05).
- Japan's elevation rests heavily on **defeating a European power** and alliance recognition.
- US rests on overseas possessions + doctrines + infrastructure enabling global reach.
- Use **criteria-based judgement**: recognition; force projection; territory; doctrine.

### Old vs New Empires

- Old empires (Britain): preserve routes and balance — **system maintenance**.
- New powers (Germany, US, Japan): use empire to **prove status** and secure entry into global hierarchy.
- That distinction is a powerful **thesis frame** for essay introductions.

## Exam Traps and Model Conclusions

🚨 **Trap:** "Everything was economic."
- Fix: Treat economics as structural background; then show at least one case where strategy/chokepoint explains urgency (Suez/Egypt; Panama).

🚨 **Trap:** Ideology treated as the main cause without proof.
- Fix: Use ideology as legitimation mechanism (public consent), then test it against event choice (why Egypt?).

🚨 **Trap:** Narrative retell of wars/crises.
- Fix: Every event paragraph must end "Therefore this shows…" linking to the question's command word.

🚨 **Trap:** No weighting (fake balance).
- Fix: Rank factors explicitly and justify using criteria (timing/scale/uniqueness).

🚨 **Trap:** Treating "isolationism" as absolute for the US.
- Fix: Use Monroe Doctrine as continuity, then show how Corollary changes intervention logic; conclude "selective activism."

🚨 **Trap:** Treating Japan's rise as only military.
- Fix: Make Meiji reforms the necessary cause; wars the proof. Use conscription (1873) as the key marker.

🚨 **Trap:** Confusing cooperation with peace.
- Fix: Berlin/Fashoda show managed rivalry; Kruger Telegram/Boer War show deepened antagonism.

📝 **Model Conclusion (General Imperialism):**
*"Imperialism after 1871 was driven by a hierarchy of causes: economic pressures made expansion attractive, but strategic geography and prestige rivalry determined decisive choices; technology enabled speed and control, while ideology legitimised coercion — so empire expanded through a feedback loop of capability and insecurity."*

📝 **Model Conclusion (Was Imperialism Mainly Conflict?):**
*"Imperialism created 'managed competition' at the diplomatic level — Berlin and Fashoda show that rules and negotiation could contain rivalry — but structural antagonism deepened; incidents like the Kruger Telegram demonstrate that colonial crises repeatedly poisoned metropolitan relations, raising the long-run temperature of great-power competition."*
