# INTRODUCTION: OUR GOALS, AUDIENCE AND PRINCIPAL THEMES

## Standard Textbook View Being Addressed

Introductory economics textbooks claim to be value-free, scientific, and consensual. They present economics as a discipline that objectively studies choice, scarcity and efficiency, with little disagreement among professionals on major empirical questions.

## The Authors' Central Critique

The textbooks are not value-free. From their first page they embed ideological commitments: defining economics as the study of unlimited wants and scarce resources already foregrounds efficiency over equity; treating all wants as equal already marginalises normative questions about distribution; presenting the invisible hand as a near-universal truth already endorses market fundamentalism.

The books claim to teach students *how* to think, but they are also telling students *what* to think. The range of views expressed in introductory textbooks is far narrower than the range of views held even by mainstream economists.

## Key Points

- The textbooks are **uniform and narrow**: they present a single paradigm — neoclassical economics — without acknowledging alternative frameworks.
- They claim **scientific consensus** where none exists (e.g., on the employment effects of minimum wages).
- They embed **implicit value judgements** in definitional choices, topic selection, and what they omit.
- The book is *not* anti-economics; it is anti-**textbook** economics. The authors draw on Nobel laureates and mainstream economists to make their points.
- The authors identify with post-Keynesian and social democratic perspectives, but the Anti-Textbook is not a manifesto for any alternative school — it is a tool for critical thinking.

## Recurring Themes Introduced Here

- Value judgements pervade so-called positive economics.
- What textbooks *omit* is as important as what they include.
- Power — of corporations, of ideology — shapes what counts as legitimate economics.
- The financial crisis of 2008 illustrates precisely the topics textbooks downplay: imperfect information, externalities, power, limited rationality.

## Memory Hook

**"The map is not the territory — and the textbook map has been drawn by someone with an agenda."**

## Why This Matters

Students who read only a mainstream textbook will absorb a pro-market, anti-government worldview without realising it. Critical thinking requires awareness that *any* presentation of economics is a selection, not a description of reality itself.

## Connections to Other Chapters

Every subsequent chapter implements the critique introduced here. Chapter 1 starts immediately with the ideological content of the definition of economics. The postscript demonstrates the real-world cost of the textbook's blind spots.

# CHAPTER 1: WHAT IS ECONOMICS? WHERE YOU START INFLUENCES WHERE YOU GO

## Standard Textbook View

Economics is defined as the **science of choice**: how individuals and societies allocate scarce resources among unlimited wants. The core framework is **rational choice theory** — self-interested, utility-maximising individuals responding predictably to incentives. Markets, when competitive, lead to **Pareto-efficient** outcomes. Government has a limited role: correcting market failures and redistributing income. There is an **efficiency–equity trade-off**: redistribution is costly and creates incentive distortions (Okun's leaky bucket). The discipline is value-free; normative questions belong to politics.

Key textbook concepts: scarcity, opportunity cost, marginal thinking (MB = MC), rationality, self-interest, the invisible hand, Pareto optimality, the efficiency–equity trade-off.

## The Anti-Textbook Critique

### The Definition of Economics Is Already Ideological

Defining economics as the study of unlimited wants and scarce resources is a *choice*, not a fact. Alternative definitions exist:

- **Keynes**: the economic problem is satisfying *absolute needs* — food, shelter, healthcare. This puts equity and distribution at the centre.
- **Post-Keynesians**: in a recession, resources are *not* scarce — workers are unemployed, factories idle. Scarcity is not a universal truth; it depends on whether the economy is at full employment.
- **Northrop**: "unlimited wants" are not innate — they are *socially constructed* through advertising, social norms, and the destruction of community. If some people can choose simplicity, wants are not naturally unlimited.
- **Marglin**: the destruction of community ties — partly caused by the spread of markets — creates an existential vacuum that goods substitute for. Wants are unlimited because community has been hollowed out.

### All Wants Are Not Equal

The textbook treats a billionaire's desire for a yacht as equally worthy of satisfaction as a poor person's need for food. This equality of wants is a **value judgement** — one that many people reject — yet it is presented as neutral.

### The Individual Versus the Community

The textbook makes individuals the fundamental unit of analysis. But:
- Community ties have real value that cost-benefit analysis ignores.
- Free trade agreements can destroy communities: winners could theoretically compensate losers, but in practice never do. And communities cannot be monetarily compensated for their destruction.
- **Marglin** quotes evidence: exposure to economics makes people *less* cooperative. The ideology that selfishness is virtue (because the invisible hand converts it into social good) erodes the norms of reciprocity that make societies function.

### The Individual Versus the Corporation

The textbook world is populated by individuals and small firms. **John Kenneth Galbraith** argues this is the economy "as it fast disappears." The real economy has two parts:

1. Many small businesses where markets operate as described.
2. A few hundred enormously powerful corporations that shape government policy, public opinion, and the legal framework.

By focusing on individuals rather than corporations, textbooks make power invisible. The corporation is a *political instrument*, not merely an economic one. Textbooks become "an ally of those whose exercise of power depends on an acquiescent public."

### The Efficiency–Equity Trade-off Reconsidered

The textbook presents this trade-off as inevitable. But:

- Taxes are not necessarily inefficient. Taxes on *bads* (pollution, carbon) improve efficiency — the "double dividend."
- **Inequality is bad for efficiency** through multiple channels: higher crime; worse health; less educational opportunity; reduced social cohesion and trust; more political conflict. Knack and Keefer (1997) show greater inequality reduces trust; lower trust reduces growth.
- Persson and Tabellini (1994) and Alesina and Rodrik (1994) find greater inequality is associated with *lower* growth rates.
- The assumption that higher taxes necessarily reduce work incentives ignores that public services (education, health) increase productivity.

### Rationality Reconsidered

The textbook defines rationality so broadly it becomes unfalsifiable: rational means maximising utility, and utility is whatever rational people maximise. This is tautological.

**Behavioural economics** (Kahneman and others) documents systematic departures:
- **Bounded rationality**: people make predictable cognitive errors (e.g., the bat-and-ball problem: most people answer 10 cents when the correct answer is 5 cents).
- **Bounded willpower**: people fail to follow through on decisions (procrastination, failure to save despite stated intention to save).
- **Bounded selfishness**: people exhibit altruism and concern for fairness. The **ultimatum game** shows people routinely reject offers below 30% of the total even at significant personal cost.
- **Crowding out**: financial incentives can *reduce* prosocial behaviour (paying blood donors reduced both quantity and quality; fines for late nursery pickup increased lateness).
- Framing effects: whether a medical risk is described as a 10% chance of dying or a 90% chance of living changes decisions, even though the content is identical.

Despite this evidence, most textbooks relegate behavioural economics to a box of "anomalies." The rational-choice framework remains central — because abandoning it would undermine the entire edifice of invisible-hand efficiency.

## Hidden Assumptions

1. Individuals (not corporations or communities) are the relevant unit of analysis.
2. All wants are equal in moral weight.
3. Unlimited wants are innate, not constructed.
4. Redistribution always reduces efficiency.
5. Rationality can be assumed without empirical verification.
6. The state is neutral, correcting market failures in the public interest.

## Main Examples and Evidence

- Marwell and Ames (1981): economics students cooperate less than others.
- Frank et al. (1993): exposure to economics increases uncooperative behaviour.
- Kahneman's bat-and-ball problem and framing experiments.
- Titmuss (1970): paying blood donors reduced quality and quantity.
- Gneezy and Rustichini (2000): nursery fines increased lateness.
- Thaler's "Save More Tomorrow" programme: commitment devices overcome bounded willpower.
- Knack and Keefer (1997), Persson and Tabellini (1994): inequality reduces trust and growth.

## Ideological Issues

The definition of economics is not neutral. Choosing scarcity and unlimited wants as the central problem shifts attention away from poverty (Keynes's absolute needs), distribution, and power. Treating the market as the default and government as the intervener encodes a laissez-faire bias. Reducing values to preferences makes selfish and altruistic behaviour morally equivalent, which undermines ethical reasoning and community norms.

## Why This Chapter Matters

It establishes that ideology begins on page one. Students who do not recognise this will absorb a conservative, market-fundamentalist framework while believing they are learning neutral science.

## Possible Weaknesses and Counterarguments

- Post-Keynesian rejection of scarcity is context-specific (recessions); the general problem of allocating scarce resources remains relevant.
- The tautological definition of rationality makes the framework flexible, not vacuous — it can accommodate altruism and community through preference structures.
- Behavioural anomalies may be market-context-specific; competitive markets may discipline irrational behaviour.
- The efficiency gains from markets are real even if they come with distributional costs.

## Standard vs. Anti-Textbook Contrast

| Standard Textbook | Anti-Textbook |
|---|---|
| Economics = science of choice under scarcity | The definition of economics is itself a value judgement |
| All wants are equal | Some wants are more pressing (absolute needs) |
| Unlimited wants are innate | Wants are socially constructed through advertising and community destruction |
| Efficiency–equity trade-off is real and unavoidable | Evidence suggests the trade-off is weak or non-existent in practice |
| Rationality is a reasonable approximation | Systematic cognitive biases undermine the rational-choice framework |
| Individuals are the unit of analysis | Corporations and communities are equally important |

## Deeper Takeaway

The starting point of any economic analysis determines its conclusions. If you define the problem as allocating scarce resources among unlimited individual wants, you will end up defending markets and worrying about government interference. If you define the problem as meeting basic needs and organising collective life, you will end up caring deeply about distribution, power, and community.

# CHAPTER 2: INTRODUCING ECONOMIC MODELS

## Standard Textbook View

Economics is a science. Models are simplified representations of reality. The key methodological principle: models should be judged by the accuracy of their **predictions**, not the realism of their assumptions (following Friedman). The textbook illustrates model-building with:
- The production possibility frontier (PPF).
- The theory of comparative advantage and gains from trade.

A key normative/positive distinction: **positive economics** describes the world as it is; **normative economics** involves value judgements about how it should be. Economists disagree on normative issues but agree on positive ones. The textbook cites survey evidence: 79–93% of economists agree that minimum wages cause unemployment, tariffs reduce welfare, rent ceilings reduce housing supply.

## The Anti-Textbook Critique

### The Textbooks Fudge Their Own Methodology

Having declared that models must be judged by predictive accuracy, the textbooks immediately present the comparative advantage model *without testing any of its predictions*. This pattern continues throughout: the methodology is invoked to give economics the aura of science, but is never actually applied.

### Predictive Power Is Not What It Seems

In natural science, falsification is possible because experiments can isolate variables. In economics:

- There are too many confounding variables to control reliably.
- Results are **non-robust**: different research teams consistently find opposing evidence on the same questions.
- Dewald et al. (1986) found it impossible to replicate many published results even using the same data and methods.
- McCullough and Vinod (2003) confirmed replication problems had not improved.

**The minimum wage as case study**: textbooks claim 79-93% of economists agree minimum wages cause unemployment. But:
- In 1979, 68% "generally agreed"; by 2003, only 46% generally agreed — a clear breakdown of consensus.
- Card, Krueger, and others published work in the 1990s showing minimum wages have zero or positive employment effects in some contexts.
- The debate became heated enough for accusations of "politically correct economics" and "conscious or unconscious author biases" (Levine 2001).
- The textbooks simply ignore this breakdown of consensus.

### Core Propositions Cannot Be Refuted

**Imre Lakatos** (1978) explained why: any theoretical framework's core propositions are surrounded by a "protective belt" of auxiliary assumptions. Negative evidence is always attributable to failure of some auxiliary assumption, not the core hypothesis. The minimum wage prediction is an article of faith.

The result: "ideas — good or bad — never die in economics" (Deepak Lal).

### Economics as Rhetoric

**Deirdre McCloskey** argues that economists do not actually use the official methodology (predictive power and hypothesis testing). Instead they use:
- Analogies and metaphors (the invisible hand, Friedman's pool-player analogy).
- Thought experiments and model-building.
- Natural experiments.
- Historical precedents.
- Appeals to authority.

This unofficial methodology — **rhetoric** — is actually how economic arguments are constructed and persuade. The official scientific methodology is largely ornamental, giving arguments authority they might not deserve.

**Heilbroner's critique of McCloskey**: she correctly describes what economists do but fails to recognise the deeper problem — economics is not merely persuasive; it is **ideological**. Values permeate so-called positive economics.

### Paradigms and Ideology

A **paradigm** is a world-view shared by a scientific community that determines what questions to ask and what methods are legitimate. Economics is taught within a single paradigm — **neoclassical economics** — without acknowledging that alternatives exist.

Paradigms are not value-free. The neoclassical paradigm's roots:
- Classical liberalism (Locke, Mill): individual responsibility, freedom of choice, sanctity of private property, minimal government.
- Today: conservative or libertarian political philosophy.

David George (1996) shows that the language of textbooks encodes this ideology: government "uses" resources (implying waste), while private spending does not; government "imposes" taxes (implying coercion); regulations are "burdens." The rhetorical framing stacks the deck.

### The Comparative Advantage Model: A Rhetorical Device

The textbook presents comparative advantage as demonstrating that free trade benefits all countries. But:

1. **The model is not tested**: predictions are stated as demonstrated rather than empirically verified.
2. **Historical evidence contradicts the argument**: the United States, Canada, and Germany industrialised behind high tariff walls, contradicting the message that free trade is universally superior.
3. **Trade produces winners and losers**: within countries, workers in import-competing sectors lose — and community destruction from unemployment is permanent and uncompensated.
4. **The exchange rate problem**: comparative advantage assumes barter. Real trade uses money; exchange rates are dominated by capital flows that have nothing to do with comparative advantage.
5. **Offshoring**: Paul Samuelson (2004) showed that offshoring can produce net economic losses for the home country, calling the claim that free trade must always benefit all a "popular polemical untruth."
6. **Industrialised countries trade mainly with each other** — inconsistent with comparative advantage theory, which predicts trade between dissimilar countries (Pugel 2007).

## Hidden Assumptions

1. Models can be judged solely by predictive accuracy (not realism of assumptions).
2. There is broad consensus among economists on positive questions.
3. Normative and positive economics are clearly distinct.
4. Comparative advantage demonstrates the benefits of free trade.
5. The neoclassical paradigm is the only legitimate framework.

## Main Examples and Evidence

- Minimum wage surveys (Kearl 1979, Alston 1992, Fuller and Geide-Stevenson 2003): declining consensus.
- Card and Krueger (1994, 1995): minimum wages do not necessarily cause unemployment.
- Dewald et al. (1986): replication failures in empirical economics.
- Blinder et al. (1998): price stickiness — testing alternative models is nearly impossible.
- Pugel (2007): over 70% of industrialised countries' exports go to other industrialised countries — inconsistent with comparative advantage.
- Samuelson (2004): offshoring can cause net losses.

## Ideological Issues

- The pretence of scientific methodology gives economics an authority it does not earn.
- The suppression of alternative paradigms constrains legitimate debate.
- The comparative advantage model is used rhetorically to support free trade ideology without adequate empirical testing.
- The positive/normative distinction obscures value judgements embedded in positive claims.

## Why This Chapter Matters

Without understanding how economic models are actually built and used — as rhetorical tools embedded in paradigms, not as pure science — students cannot evaluate what they are being taught. The claim to scientific consensus is one of the most powerful rhetorical tools in the textbook arsenal.

## Possible Weaknesses

- McCloskey's critique of falsificationism does not mean all economic claims are equally valid.
- Some economic hypotheses *have* been tested and rejected (e.g., the strict quantity theory of money).
- The comparative advantage model has genuine explanatory power for some trade patterns.
- Behavioural economists themselves use formal models and empirical testing.

## Standard vs. Anti-Textbook Contrast

| Standard Textbook | Anti-Textbook |
|---|---|
| Models judged by predictive accuracy | Models are rarely tested; methodology is rhetorical |
| Broad consensus on positive questions | Consensus is invented; debate is suppressed |
| Positive and normative economics are distinct | Value judgements pervade positive economics |
| Comparative advantage shows gains from trade | The model is untested and historically contested |
| One legitimate paradigm | Multiple paradigms; choice reflects ideology |

## Memory Hook

**"The textbook claims to be a thermometer but is actually a thermostat — setting the temperature of thought."**

# CHAPTER 3: HOW MARKETS WORK (IN AN IMAGINARY WORLD)

## Standard Textbook View

The demand and supply framework is a generic model applicable to all markets. In competitive markets:
- Prices are determined by the intersection of supply and demand.
- Prices move toward equilibrium automatically.
- Price floors (like minimum wages) cause surpluses; price ceilings (like rent control) cause shortages.
- Taxes create deadweight losses and are split between buyers and sellers depending on relative elasticities.

The model is introduced early and applied universally — presented as a workable approximation even for non-competitive markets.

## The Anti-Textbook Critique

### Supply and Demand = Perfect Competition

The textbooks reveal this identity only much later: "The supply and demand model, which we introduced in Chapter 3 and have used repeatedly since then, is a model of a *perfectly competitive market*" (Krugman and Wells 2005: 207). But:

- Supply curves exist only in perfectly competitive markets. Non-competitive firms face downward-sloping demand curves; there is no unique relationship between price and quantity supplied.
- Applying supply and demand to non-competitive markets applies it where it is not valid.

### How Many Markets Are Perfectly Competitive?

Almost none:

- **Joseph Stiglitz** (Nobel 2001): even a tiny search cost is enough to push a competitive market to monopoly pricing (the "epsilon" argument). Information imperfections are pervasive and unavoidable.
- **Blinder et al. (1998)**: 200 representative US firms (outside agriculture) — *all* are price-makers, not price-takers. Prices are changed on average 1.4 times per year, not continuously. This directly contradicts the competitive model.
- Agriculture (where competition is closest) is only about 2% of production in industrialised economies.
- Even agricultural markets have non-competitive elements: input suppliers (seeds, fertiliser) and buyers (supermarkets, fast food) have market power over farmers.

### Does the Competitive Model Give Good Predictions?

**Minimum wages**: Evidence is mixed; consensus has broken down (see Chapter 2). The competitive prediction of unemployment from minimum wages is empirically contested.

**Rent control**: 
- The textbook predicts persistent, worsening shortages. But most post-1970s "second-generation" rent controls are flexible: they allow cost-based increases, exclude luxury housing, and permit decontrol between tenants.
- Housing economists recognise substantial non-competitive elements: search costs are high (hence agents' fees), apartments are heterogeneous, information is imperfect.
- Arnott (1995): the empirical effects of modern rent controls "have been almost imperceptible" except in New York (which retained rigid first-generation controls).
- The textbook prediction holds only for the crude version of rent control and the imaginary competitive housing market.

**Tax incidence**: The competitive model predicts that tax burden is split according to relative elasticities. But:
- Supply curves do not exist in non-competitive markets — so elasticity of supply cannot be measured.
- Textbooks illustrate tax incidence using cigarettes and gasoline — both highly non-competitive (oligopolies). No evidence is presented because it cannot be gathered from markets where supply curves do not exist.

### Internal Inconsistencies

**Arrow's Paradox**: In perfect competition, all agents are price-takers. But if no one sets prices, how do prices change when there is excess supply or demand? The standard "solution" invents an **auctioneer** — a fictional character with no economic objective who adjusts prices. This is an admission that the model lacks a theory of price-setting.

**Out-of-equilibrium behaviour**: When there is excess supply, do firms still supply as if they could sell everything? If so, the supply curve is meaningless. If firms notice the surplus and reduce supply, the supply curve does not describe supply. The competitive model assumes market participants continue to act as if in equilibrium even when they are not.

**Sraffa (1926)**: The conditions necessary for supply and demand independence (firms are price-takers in input markets) are incompatible with the conditions necessary for large numbers of small firms. The model cannot satisfy both requirements simultaneously.

**Multiple equilibria**: The labour supply curve may be backward-bending — or may have multiple bends if subsistence needs are considered (Prasch). This creates multiple stable equilibria, including a poverty-trap equilibrium. Minimum wages and maximum hours laws may shift the economy to a better equilibrium without appearing to be binding once there.

**Self-fulfilling prophecies**: Expected price changes shift both demand and supply. This means prices can be at whatever level people expect them to be — markets are fundamentally indeterminate in this regard.

**The SMD Theorem ("Anything Goes")**: In a general equilibrium system of competitive markets, equilibrium may not be unique, and there is no reliable price adjustment process that guarantees convergence to any equilibrium. The competitive economy is neither determinate nor stable in the aggregate.

## Hidden Assumptions

1. Supply curves exist (implying competitive markets are the norm).
2. Prices adjust instantaneously to equilibrium.
3. There is a unique equilibrium that the market finds.
4. Price controls have predictable, universal effects regardless of market structure.
5. The legal and regulatory framework is irrelevant or secondary.

## Main Examples and Evidence

- Blinder et al. (1998): 200 firms — all price-makers, sticky prices.
- Arnott (1995): rent controls have minimal empirical effect in most North American cities.
- Card and Krueger (1994): NJ/PA minimum wage natural experiment — no unemployment increase.
- Stiglitz (2002): epsilon search costs push competitive markets to monopoly pricing.
- Sraffa (1926): logical inconsistency in competitive supply curve derivation.
- Paris eviction-protection legislation: demonstrated housing shortage from non-competitive regulatory failure.
- Post-war price controls: shortages occurred but are consistent with non-competitive models too.

## Ideological Issues

The competitive model is deployed as a **generic tool** not because it has superior predictive power, but because the story it tells — markets are efficient, government intervention causes shortages and surpluses — is politically useful. Stiglitz: the paradigm survived "partly because the belief in that paradigm, and the policy prescriptions derived from it, has served certain interests."

The choice of minimum wages and rent control as the primary policy applications is not neutral. They are examples where government intervention appears harmful. There are no examples of government intervention that improves outcomes (pollution taxes, natural monopoly regulation) in this section of most textbooks.

## Why This Chapter Matters

The supply-and-demand framework is the foundation of everything that follows. If it is only valid for competitive markets, and competitive markets are rare, then most of what the textbook teaches as universal has very limited applicability — a fact the textbooks hide for as long as possible.

## Possible Weaknesses

- Even if markets are not perfectly competitive, supply and demand can provide useful *directional* predictions in many cases.
- The Blinder et al. finding about sticky prices does not necessarily invalidate all supply-and-demand analysis.
- Multiple equilibria are theoretically possible but may be practically rare.

## Standard vs. Anti-Textbook Contrast

| Standard Textbook | Anti-Textbook |
|---|---|
| Supply and demand is a generic tool | It applies only to perfectly competitive markets |
| Price floors cause surpluses; ceilings cause shortages | These predictions are model-specific and empirically contested |
| Competitive markets are common | Almost all firms are price-makers |
| Tax incidence follows elasticity formula | Supply curves don't exist in most actual markets |
| Markets find equilibrium automatically | No theory of price-setting; equilibrium not guaranteed |

## Memory Hook

**"Supply and demand is the textbook's Swiss Army knife — used for everything even though most jobs require a different tool."**

# CHAPTER 4: PEOPLE AS CONSUMERS

## Standard Textbook View

Consumer theory assumes:
- Preferences are **given** (exogenous) and stable.
- Buyers have **perfect information** about what they purchase.
- Individuals maximise utility, and the utility from consumption is independent of what others consume.
- The demand curve reflects marginal benefit; consumer surplus measures welfare.
- More consumption = more welfare: economic growth increases well-being.

## The Anti-Textbook Critique

### What If Preferences Are Not Given?

The textbook assumes preferences originate within individuals. This assumption:
- Makes advertising a non-issue (it only provides information).
- Ignores that producers invest in changing preferences, and that preferences are partly socially determined.

**Advertising** in the US: about 2.1% of GDP (~$970 per person in 2008). Galbraith (1958): "Advertising expenditures must be integrated with the theory of consumer demand. They are too big to be ignored." Yet the competitive textbook model has no room for advertising.

**The changing-preferences problem**: If your preference for a product exists because you were exposed to marketing, should we measure welfare using your pre-advertising or post-advertising preferences? The textbook ignores this — because acknowledging it would undermine the claim that market outcomes maximise well-being.

**Children and marketing**: Juliet Schor (2004) documents sophisticated marketing targeting children — surveillance of children in stores, Channel One advertising in schools. Quebec's ban on advertising to children under 12 significantly reduced junk food purchases.

### Incomplete and Asymmetric Information

The textbook's perfect-information consumer is a fiction. Real examples:

**Infant formula**: WHO and UNICEF find aggressive marketing of infant formula kills over 1 million infants per year by displacing breastfeeding. UNICEF estimates exclusive breastfeeding would save over 1 million lives annually. US producers distributed free samples in hospitals despite international code prohibiting this. Breastfeeding rates in the US (27%) and UK (21%) are far below Sweden (72%) and Norway (80%) — the difference is policy and cultural norms, not rational consumer choice.

**Prescription drugs**: Prozac and similar antidepressants taken by 40 million people were found to be no more effective than placebos for most users — but companies concealed this. Marketing "disease-mongering" expanded patient populations: shyness became "social anxiety disorder," and statins were recommended for 8-year-old children.

**Food**: Eric Schlosser's *Fast Food Nation* documented what goes into industrial food. Additive-laden food causes allergies, hyperactivity in children, and cancers. Nutritional content of industrially produced food is declining. Consumers often lack information to make informed choices.

**General asymmetric information**: Producers know more about product quality, risks, and content than consumers in almost every market. This is the rule, not the exception.

### Preferences and Relative Position

The textbook assumes utility from consumption is independent of what others have. But evidence strongly suggests **relative position matters**:

**The Easterlin Paradox**:
1. Within countries at any given time: richer people report being happier.
2. Across countries: countries with higher incomes tend to have higher average happiness.
3. Within countries over time: average happiness has *not increased* as average incomes have risen substantially (US happiness flat since 1946 despite enormous income growth).

This paradox is explained by:
- **Social comparison**: people judge well-being relative to others. If everyone gets richer together, relative positions don't change, so happiness doesn't improve.
- **Adaptation**: people adapt to higher incomes, raising their aspirational baseline.
- **Positional arms race** (Robert Frank): inequality raises norms upward because of conspicuous consumption among the wealthy, fuelling a futile status competition and consumer debt explosion.
- **Time scarcity** (Linder): increasing material affluence is associated with increasing time scarcity, reducing satisfaction from possessions.

### Implications

If consumer welfare depends on relative position, market outcomes cannot be simply evaluated using individual demand curves and consumer surplus. The invisible hand does not convert individual consumption choices into social well-being in this context.

If happiness does not increase with income growth in wealthy countries, the textbook's central justification for prioritising efficiency and growth is empirically unsupported.

## Hidden Assumptions

1. Preferences are given and exogenous.
2. Information is complete and symmetric.
3. Utility depends only on one's own consumption, not on others'.
4. More income always increases well-being.
5. Consumer sovereignty: consumers control what is produced through their spending decisions.

## Main Examples and Evidence

- Infant formula marketing and child mortality (WHO, UNICEF data).
- Prozac study: no more effective than placebo; evidence suppressed.
- Easterlin (1974, 1995): happiness paradox — income growth does not raise average happiness.
- Robert Frank (1999): luxury fever and positional arms race.
- Juliet Schor (1992, 1998, 2004): work-and-spend cycle, marketing to children.
- Channel One study: advertising in schools increases materialistic values.
- Baylis and Dhar (2007): Quebec ban on advertising to children reduces junk food spending.
- Happiness data: Figures 4.3, 4.4, 4.5 in original.

## Ideological Issues

The textbook's refusal to question where preferences come from is a value judgement that benefits producers over consumers. "Consumer sovereignty" is a myth when preferences are shaped by marketing and by imperfect information. The focus on growth and efficiency ignores the evidence that growth in wealthy countries does not improve average well-being.

## Why This Chapter Matters

Consumer theory is the microeconomic foundation for demand analysis and for the claim that markets serve consumers. Once preferences are acknowledged to be formed partly by producers, and once information asymmetries are taken seriously, the case for unregulated markets collapses.

## Possible Weaknesses

- The Easterlin Paradox is contested; recent research (Stevenson and Wolfers 2008) finds a positive relationship between income and happiness even over time.
- Advertising provides genuine information in some cases.
- People do have some genuine autonomy over their preferences.

## Standard vs. Anti-Textbook Contrast

| Standard Textbook | Anti-Textbook |
|---|---|
| Preferences are given and stable | Preferences are shaped by advertising, social context, and imperfect information |
| Perfect information | Asymmetric information is pervasive — infant formula, drugs, food |
| More consumption = more welfare | Easterlin paradox: income growth doesn't raise average happiness |
| Consumer sovereignty | Producers shape consumer preferences through marketing |
| Individual utility is independent of others | Relative position matters enormously for well-being |

## Memory Hook

**"The textbook consumer is Robinson Crusoe choosing between fish and coconuts. The real consumer is bombarded with sophisticated marketing, trapped in a positional arms race, and eating food whose contents are unknown."**

# CHAPTER 5: THE FIRM

## Standard Textbook View

The firm is a **profit-maximising** entity. In the short run, output is determined by the law of diminishing marginal returns, producing upward-sloping marginal cost curves. In the long run, U-shaped average cost curves limit firm size, ensuring many small firms in competitive industries. Firms are price-takers in competitive markets; others are price-setters. The principal-agent relationship between shareholders and managers is straightforward; managers implement shareholder interests.

## The Anti-Textbook Critique

### The Cost Curves Are Fictional

The U-shaped cost curves that populate textbooks are constructed to justify perfect competition — not derived from evidence about actual firms.

**Short-run marginal costs**: Blinder et al. (1998) survey 200 representative US firms. "Overwhelmingly bad news": 48% report *constant* marginal costs; 41% report *decreasing* marginal costs; only 11% report increasing marginal costs. The "law" of diminishing marginal returns is largely irrelevant in practice because firms hold idle capacity that they can activate as output rises.

**Long-run average costs**: Edwin Mansfield (1994): "The long-run average cost function in most industries seems to be L-shaped ... not U-shaped. That is, there is no evidence that it turns upward." If average costs don't rise, nothing prevents firms from growing to dominate markets — which is incompatible with perfect competition.

Why do textbooks maintain these fictional cost curves? **Avi Cohen** (1983) explains: without U-shaped costs there is nothing to limit firm size, and "the chaotic indeterminateness of oligopoly" replaces "the elegant determinateness of perfect competition." The cost assumptions are auxiliary assumptions protecting the core competitive paradigm from empirical refutation.

### The Competitive Equilibrium Is Incoherent

If a competitive firm raises its price slightly, it will not lose all its customers — because competitor firms are already producing their profit-maximising output and cannot expand to serve the defectors. Therefore, in the textbook equilibrium, competitive firms would *want to raise their prices*. Dixon (1990: 363): "Firms will want to raise price at the competitive equilibrium." The competitive equilibrium is self-undermining — it is really a disequilibrium.

### Power Outside the Firm

The textbook ignores that firms invest in shaping their external environment:
- **Lobbying**: Washington lobbyists outnumber Congress members 65 to 1; corporate lobbying spends $200 million per month.
- **Healthcare reform (US)**: six lobbyists per member of Congress; insurance companies turned the legislation into "a business bonanza."
- **Tobacco industry**: creates "astroturf" front organisations (Citizens Against Lawsuit Abuse) funded by Philip Morris; spreads "junk science" attacks on health research.
- **Fossil fuel interests**: follow the same playbook against climate science.

**Mancur Olson's** public choice theory explains how concentrated industries (few large firms) can overcome the free-rider problem and organise effectively, while the large majority of citizens (who would benefit from different policies) cannot. "There is a systematic tendency for 'exploitation' of the great by the small."

**Rational ignorance**: Individual citizens gain little from being informed about complex policy issues; the benefit of an informed vote is negligible when one vote makes no difference. Meanwhile, corporations have strong financial incentives to be fully informed and organised. This structural imbalance underlies corporate power over democratic institutions.

### Power Within the Firm

**Principal-agent problem**: Shareholders (principals) appoint managers (agents) who have their own goals. Asymmetric information means managers can pursue growth, perquisites, or short-term performance metrics at shareholders' expense. Bebchuk and Fried show top executive pay rose from 5.2% to 8.1% of net income (1993-2003), costing shareholders $69 billion in excess compensation.

**Workplace democracy**: The capitalist firm is authoritarian as a matter of principle. Alternative forms exist — worker cooperatives (Mondragón in Spain: 80 co-ops, 30,000 members), consumer cooperatives — with evidence of higher productivity. But these are virtually absent from textbooks. John Stuart Mill (1848) predicted worker-managed firms would come to dominate. The textbook presents the autocratic capitalist firm as the only option.

## Hidden Assumptions

1. U-shaped cost curves limit firm size, enabling competitive markets.
2. Firms are passive economic actors; they do not shape public policy.
3. Managers implement shareholder interests.
4. The capitalist firm is the only viable form of economic organisation.
5. Power within firms is irrelevant to economic analysis.

## Main Examples and Evidence

- Blinder et al. (1998): 48% constant, 41% decreasing short-run marginal costs.
- Mansfield (1994): L-shaped long-run average cost curves, not U-shaped.
- Bebchuk and Fried (2004): managerial pay appropriation.
- Olson (1971): logic of collective action; concentrated industries dominate policy.
- Ralph Nader on Washington lobbying.
- Healthcare reform lobbying (US): industry spent hundreds of millions.
- Mondragón cooperative: viable large-scale cooperative enterprise.

## Ideological Issues

The textbook's "black box" firm — passive, profit-maximising, cost-constrained — makes corporate power invisible. By not teaching students about lobbying, corporate capture of government, and the political economy of regulation, textbooks produce citizens who cannot understand how economic policy is actually made.

## Why This Chapter Matters

The theory of the firm is where the textbook's failure to acknowledge power is most glaring. Understanding that firms actively shape the rules of the game is essential for understanding why regulation is weak, why inequality grows, and why externalities persist.

## Standard vs. Anti-Textbook Contrast

| Standard Textbook | Anti-Textbook |
|---|---|
| Diminishing returns limit firm size | Most firms have constant or decreasing costs |
| Firms are passive price-takers or price-setters | Firms actively shape government policy and public opinion |
| Managers serve shareholders | Principal-agent problem: managers serve themselves |
| Capitalist firm is the natural form | Worker cooperatives exist and are productive |
| Power is irrelevant to the theory of the firm | Power is central to understanding how firms and markets actually work |

## Memory Hook

**"The textbook firm is a machine that converts inputs into outputs and profits. The real firm is a political actor that shapes the game it plays in."**

# CHAPTER 6: MARKET STRUCTURE AND EFFICIENCY — OR WHY PERFECT COMPETITION ISN'T SO PERFECT AFTER ALL

## Standard Textbook View

Market structures range from perfect competition (socially optimal) through monopolistic competition and oligopoly (less efficient) to monopoly (most inefficient). Perfect competition:
- Produces at the socially optimal output (P = MC).
- Minimises long-run average costs.
- Distributes output to those who value it most.
- Maximises social welfare (consumer plus producer surplus).

Monopoly creates a deadweight loss. Government can improve outcomes through antitrust policy, price regulation, and publicly provided goods. The competitive model serves as the standard against which all other market structures are evaluated.

## The Anti-Textbook Critique

### Perfect Competition Is Not an Ideal Standard for Three Reasons

**1. Equity**: The efficiency of competitive markets is conditional on the existing distribution of income. If Muriel has $4 of marginal benefit for bread and Virginia has $9, the "efficient" outcome gives the bread to Virginia. But Virginia's higher willingness to pay may simply reflect that she is richer. Efficiency maximises *dollar-weighted* benefits, not human welfare. A "Pareto optimal" outcome could have orphans starving while the rich have three yachts. Perfect competition produces an efficient *given the income distribution*, not an *ideal* outcome.

Most textbooks do not adequately discuss this condition. Efficiency is *not* the same as welfare maximisation unless the income distribution is already just.

**2. Dynamic efficiency**: The textbook focuses on *static* efficiency — producing the optimal amount today with current technology. But the most important driver of long-run prosperity is **innovation** and dynamic efficiency. Schumpeter argued that monopoly profits are the *reward* for innovation — they provide the incentive to invest in R&D. A world of perfect competition, where no one earns above-normal profits, is a world with little incentive to innovate. Innovation is what capitalism does best; perfect competition ignores this completely.

Evidence: Blinder, Baumol, and others show that most innovation in oligopolistic industries has driven dramatic productivity gains. Patent protection — a government-created temporary monopoly — is *necessary* for pharmaceutical innovation.

**3. Static efficiency is contestable**: Even on its own terms, the competitive ideal rests on:
- All externalities internalised (explored in Chapter 7).
- Perfect information (shown to be impossible in Chapter 3).
- All markets existing (including markets for future goods and for pollution).

These conditions are never met in practice.

### The Deadweight Loss Calculation is Exaggerated

The textbook calculates deadweight loss from monopoly as the welfare loss from under-production. But:
- If the monopoly arose from innovation (e.g., a patent), the deadweight loss must be weighed against the dynamic benefit of the innovation.
- **Rent-seeking** (spending to secure monopoly position) is an additional cost — but it is also an input to innovation competition.
- The textbook's deadweight loss triangles are highly sensitive to assumptions about demand elasticity; estimates are often wildly imprecise.

### Financial Markets Are Not Efficient

The textbook treats stock markets as largely efficient (price changes reflecting true value changes). But:
- **Stock market bubbles**: Japanese bubble (1990), dot-com (2001), China (2008), housing (2007-8) represent enormous aggregate price distortions.
- "Smart money" often profits by following rather than correcting irrational momentum (Russell and Thaler 1985).
- Mullainathan and Thaler (2004): "It may often pay 'smart money' to follow 'dumb money' rather than lean against it."
- Closed-end fund anomaly: discounts/premiums to net asset value cannot be explained by rational behaviour.

### Oligopoly and the Limits of Game Theory

The prisoner's dilemma suggests collusion is difficult. But repeated games enable cooperation through "tit-for-tat" strategies. Real oligopolies show evidence of tacit collusion (price leadership, avoiding price wars). The textbook treats this as self-explanatory without examining whether the outcomes are good for consumers or society.

## Hidden Assumptions

1. The income distribution is just, so efficient allocations are optimal.
2. Static efficiency is more important than dynamic efficiency.
3. Perfect competition can serve as a practical policy benchmark.
4. Stock market prices reflect underlying values.
5. Monopoly power is always harmful (not sometimes the reward for innovation).

## Main Examples and Evidence

- Schumpeter on creative destruction and innovation incentives.
- Aggregate stock price bubbles (Figure 6.11).
- Drug patent protection debate: innovation incentives vs. monopoly pricing.
- Blinder et al. on oligopoly as the dominant market structure.
- Tit-for-tat strategy in repeated oligopoly games.

## Ideological Issues

The competitive model as an "ideal benchmark" implies that any deviation is a problem to be corrected toward competition. This frames all regulation and government intervention as second-best correctives rather than as potentially creating better outcomes in their own right. The equity problem with efficiency is systematically ignored in most textbooks.

## Why This Chapter Matters

The efficiency argument for competitive markets is the centrepiece of the textbook's pro-market ideology. Once it is shown that: (a) efficiency depends on the income distribution being just, (b) static efficiency ignores dynamic innovation, and (c) the conditions for competitive efficiency are never met, the whole edifice wobbles.

## Standard vs. Anti-Textbook Contrast

| Standard Textbook | Anti-Textbook |
|---|---|
| Perfect competition = socially optimal | Efficient given existing income distribution, not inherently just |
| Monopoly always creates deadweight loss | Monopoly profits reward innovation — dynamic efficiency matters |
| Competitive market = ideal benchmark | Benchmark rests on conditions never met in reality |
| Stock markets are efficient | Bubbles and irrational dynamics are pervasive |
| Oligopoly is inefficient but manageable | Tacit collusion is common; outcomes uncertain |

## Memory Hook

**"The invisible hand only works if the income distribution is already just — a condition textbooks never check."**

# CHAPTER 7: EXTERNALITIES AND THE UBIQUITY OF MARKET FAILURE

## Standard Textbook View

Externalities are costs or benefits imposed on third parties not reflected in market prices. Negative externalities (pollution) lead to over-production; positive externalities lead to under-production. The textbook presents several corrective mechanisms:
- Pigouvian taxes equalise private and social costs.
- Cap-and-trade systems assign pollution rights.
- Property rights (Coase theorem): if well-defined, bargaining achieves efficiency.

Externalities are treated as exceptions — important, but correctable market failures that do not undermine the basic efficiency story.

## The Anti-Textbook Critique

### Externalities Are Ubiquitous, Not Exceptional

The textbook's placement of externalities *after* the main story of efficient markets is revealing. Hill and Myatt argue externalities are central, not peripheral:

**Climate change**: perhaps the largest market failure in history. Costs borne by future generations who cannot participate in current markets. Most textbooks acknowledge it superficially; few provide data on its magnitude.

**Air pollution**: About 2 million die prematurely each year globally from air pollution (WHO 2008). Canada: 21,000 deaths per year (Canadian Medical Association 2008). The burning of fossil fuels generates carbon monoxide, particulates, nitrogen oxides, and ozone — all killing people at low levels that most textbooks fail to quantify.

**Industrial chemicals**: 85,000 chemicals registered in the US; fewer than 2% tested for carcinogenicity. Polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs), used as flame retardants, are now found in breast milk at levels 40 times higher in North America than Sweden. Cancer rates have been rising for decades across industrialised countries even excluding lung cancer.

**Workplace health and safety**: ILO estimates 1.9-2.3 million occupational deaths per year globally, including 600,000 cancer deaths. The textbook rules this out by assuming perfect information about job risks. **The asbestos holocaust**: 100,000 asbestos-related cancers per year (WHO); potentially 5-10 million total deaths. Industry knew about dangers for decades and suppressed information. The Canadian government fought international asbestos bans while exporting 250,000 metric tonnes annually to developing countries with minimal regulation.

**Fisheries collapse**: 29% of fished species were considered collapsed by 2003 (Worm et al. 2006). The Newfoundland cod fishery closed in 1992 and may never reopen. Classic open-access externality, but government response has consistently favoured commercial fishermen over the public interest.

**Financial externalities**: When a bank makes risky loans, it increases risk for all connected banks — a "systemic externality." Kaufman and Scott (2003) formalise this. Regulation is required but banks resist the costs. The 2008 financial crisis is the clearest illustration of what happens when systemic externalities are ignored (see Postscript).

**Consumption externalities** (Robert Frank, *Luxury Fever*): Visible consumption by the wealthy raises social norms, inducing positional competition throughout society. This is a negative externality that makes *everyone* work longer hours to maintain relative position — a pure welfare loss. The result is too much market consumption and too little leisure, social time, and public goods.

### Imperfect Information Prevents Externality Correction

The textbook's externality corrections work in a world where:
- The external cost can be measured.
- Victims know they are harmed.
- Government responds to evidence in the public interest.

In reality:
- **Employers suppress information** about workplace hazards (asbestos, lead, chemicals).
- **Cognitive dissonance**: workers may choose to believe risks are lower than they are because accepting the truth would be too costly psychologically.
- **Corporate power over regulation**: industries lobby against environmental regulations; sponsor "junk science" to cast doubt on health research; use asymmetric information to capture regulatory agencies.
- **Rational ignorance** among citizens prevents effective political response.

### The Profitability of Harm

The textbook presents externalities as incidental side effects of production. The authors argue that imposing external costs is often *profitable strategy*:
- Tobacco companies marketed to children knowing the product kills, suppressing contrary evidence for decades.
- Asbestos industry suppressed evidence of deaths for a century.
- Pharmaceutical companies concealed evidence of drug side effects.
- Chemical companies deny environmental harms.

Howard Zinn (2002): "the drive of corporations for profit is done at the expense of human beings all over the world." This is not mere moralising — it is a description of rational profit-maximising behaviour in the absence of adequate regulation.

## Hidden Assumptions

1. Externalities are exceptional, not pervasive.
2. External costs can be measured and corrected.
3. Government responds to externalities in the public interest.
4. Workers have perfect information about job risks.
5. Corporate strategy does not include deliberate imposition of external costs.

## Main Examples and Evidence

- WHO air pollution mortality data (2 million deaths/year).
- Cancer incidence data across industrialised countries (Tables 7.1 and 7.2).
- Worm et al. (2006): 29% of fished species collapsed.
- ILO occupational death data.
- Asbestos: 100,000 deaths/year, industry suppression of evidence.
- Financial crisis externalities (Kaufman and Scott 2003).
- Robert Frank's consumption externalities.
- Infant formula marketing deaths (WHO, UNICEF).

## Ideological Issues

Treating externalities as exceptional allows the textbook to maintain its basic message of market efficiency. Acknowledging them as pervasive would require admitting that the entire market economy operates in a regime of systematic market failure that can only be addressed by robust regulation, democratic accountability, and corporate governance reform.

## Why This Chapter Matters

The externality problem is the empirical foundation for understanding why real-world markets produce outcomes so different from the textbook ideal — environmental degradation, occupational illness, financial instability, and public health crises are all externality problems at scale.

## Standard vs. Anti-Textbook Contrast

| Standard Textbook | Anti-Textbook |
|---|---|
| Externalities are exceptions to efficient markets | Externalities are pervasive and central |
| Pigouvian taxes and cap-and-trade solve the problem | Corporate power prevents effective correction |
| Workers are compensated for job risks (compensating differentials) | Workers often lack information; employers suppress it |
| Environmental harms are incidental | Imposing external costs is often deliberate corporate strategy |

## Memory Hook

**"The textbook treats pollution as a footnote. The real world treats it as the price of doing business — paid by the public, not the firm."**

# CHAPTER 8: THE MARGINAL PRODUCTIVITY THEORY OF INCOME DISTRIBUTION — OR YOU'RE WORTH WHAT YOU CAN GET

## Standard Textbook View

Income distribution reflects **marginal productivity**: factors of production are paid according to the value of what they contribute to output. In competitive markets:
- Labour earns its marginal revenue product (MRP).
- Capital earns its marginal product.
- Wage differentials reflect differences in productivity: ability, education (human capital), job characteristics.
- Discrimination is eliminated by competitive forces over time.
- Unions raise wages above equilibrium, causing unemployment.
- The distribution of income is therefore legitimate: you earn what your labour is worth.

## The Anti-Textbook Critique

### Logical Problems: The Adding-Up Problem

**Cambridge Capital Controversy**: If we want to show that capital earns its marginal product, we need to measure the "quantity of capital." But capital is a collection of heterogeneous goods (machines, buildings); to aggregate them we need to value them; to value them we need the rate of return; but the rate of return is what we are trying to determine. The argument is circular.

More technically: paying factors their marginal products only exhausts total output (adds up correctly) if the economy exhibits **constant returns to scale** throughout. This is both a specific technological restriction and something that cannot be independently verified.

**Reswitching**: Joan Robinson and others showed that as the rate of profit changes, a given technique of production can become unprofitable, then profitable again (reswitching). This means there is no monotonic relationship between the supply of capital and the rate of return — the demand curve for capital does not slope downward reliably. The entire neoclassical theory of capital distribution lacks a sound logical foundation.

### Absurd Predictions in Competitive Labour Markets

The textbook competitive labour market predicts:
- There is a **unique market wage** for any given type of worker.
- Wages equal the value of the marginal product.

But empirically: wages for workers of *identical observable quality* vary substantially across firms, industries, and regions — the "no single market wage" finding is robust. The textbook offers subsidiary theories (efficiency wages, compensating differentials, human capital, discrimination) to explain these differentials. But these auxiliary theories undermine the core claim that competitive markets produce wages equal to marginal product.

**Efficiency wages**: Firms pay above-market wages to elicit effort when monitoring is difficult. But this creates unemployment — an inefficiency absent from the textbook's competitive labour market story.

**Monopsony**: A single buyer of labour. Textbooks treat this as rare. But **dynamic monopsony** — where any employer has some short-run market power because workers face costs of changing jobs (search costs, mobility costs) — is pervasive. Evidence:
- Card and Krueger's minimum wage research: if labour markets are monopsonistic, minimum wage increases can *increase* employment by reducing the monopsonist's market power (as shown in Figure 8.4 of the original).
- Manning (2003) argues that monopsony power is the norm in labour markets.
- The textbook prediction that minimum wages *always* reduce employment is specific to competitive labour markets — not monopsonistic ones.

### Unions Get a Bad Press

Textbooks present unions as:
- Raising wages above equilibrium.
- Causing unemployment for non-union workers.
- Being purely self-serving.

Alternative view:
- Unions offset monopsonistic employer power.
- Unions provide voice for workers within firms, reducing turnover and improving information flow.
- Unions enforce occupational safety standards that competitive markets might not produce (because workers lack information and face cognitive dissonance about risks).
- Unions support public goods (universal health, education) that benefit all workers.

### Executive Pay: The Tournament Theory Fails

CEO pay in the US rose from about 30x average wages in 1970 to over 300x by the 2000s (Figure 8.8 in original). Marginal productivity theory predicts this reflects growing executive productivity. Tournament theory (Lazear and Rosen 1981) argues CEO pay is a prize that motivates the whole management team. But:
- CEO pay increased in line with the *stock market* (Figure 8.9), not with firm performance relative to competitors.
- Bebchuk and Fried (2004) show management captures pay through board dominance, not performance.
- CEO pay is strongly correlated with firm size (implying it reflects bargaining power, not productivity).
- Cross-country comparison: CEOs are paid far more in the US than in comparable companies in Europe or Japan, without evidence of correspondingly higher productivity.

The evidence is more consistent with **power** determining CEO pay than with marginal productivity.

### Discrimination: Markets Don't Automatically Correct It

The textbook predicts competitive markets eliminate discrimination because non-discriminating firms hire discriminated workers cheaply, gaining a cost advantage, and eventually dominate. But:
- This requires *non-discriminating* firms to exist in sufficient numbers.
- If customers are themselves prejudiced, discrimination by firms is profit-maximising.
- If workers are trapped by information gaps or mobility constraints, discrimination can persist even without customer prejudice.
- Statistical discrimination: employers discriminate based on group averages even when individual productivity differs, creating self-fulfilling prophecies.

## Hidden Assumptions

1. Capital can be measured independently of the rate of return.
2. A unique market wage exists for any type of labour.
3. Wage differentials reflect productivity differences.
4. Unions are purely negative for economic efficiency.
5. Competitive forces eliminate discrimination.
6. Executive pay reflects executive marginal product.

## Main Examples and Evidence

- Cambridge Capital Controversy: Robinson, Sraffa on reswitching.
- Card and Krueger on minimum wages and monopsony.
- Bebchuk and Fried (2004): executive pay and managerial power.
- Figure 8.8: CEO pay vs. average wages, 1970-2002.
- Manning (2003): pervasive monopsony power.
- Wage variance studies: large wage differences for identical workers.
- ILO data on occupational deaths (Chapter 7 connection).

## Ideological Issues

The marginal productivity theory is ideologically powerful because it makes income distribution appear natural and just: you earn what you produce. This legitimises inequality by claiming it reflects differences in contribution, not differences in power. James K. Galbraith: "Are we prepared to rule the issues of power, monopoly and financial control off the table when we discuss the way incomes are apportioned?"

By locating this discussion near the end of the book — and in many courses never reaching it — the textbook ensures that students absorb the competitive efficiency story without ever seeing its distributional implications questioned.

## Why This Chapter Matters

Income distribution is at the heart of most political debates about the economy. If wages do not reflect marginal productivity — if they reflect power, monopsony, efficiency wages, and historical path dependence — then the market cannot claim to distribute income justly, and the case for redistribution becomes much stronger.

## Standard vs. Anti-Textbook Contrast

| Standard Textbook | Anti-Textbook |
|---|---|
| Wages = marginal revenue product | No logical foundation for capital's marginal product; wages reflect power too |
| Competitive markets eliminate discrimination | Discrimination persists with customer prejudice or information gaps |
| Unions cause unemployment | Unions offset monopsony power; may increase employment |
| CEO pay reflects executive productivity | CEO pay reflects managerial power over boards |
| Monopsony is rare | Dynamic monopsony is pervasive |

## Memory Hook

**"'You're worth what you can get' is the chapter's own subtitle — which says everything the textbook doesn't: wages reflect bargaining power, not just productivity."**

# CHAPTER 9: GOVERNMENT, TAXATION AND THE (RE)DISTRIBUTION OF INCOME — IS A JUST SOCIETY JUST TOO EXPENSIVE?

## Standard Textbook View

Income redistribution involves an **efficiency–equity trade-off** (Okun's leaky bucket): transferring income from rich to poor creates incentive distortions and economic waste. Taxes reduce work incentives and cause deadweight losses. The optimal level of redistribution is a political question; economists can only describe the trade-off. Reducing inequality has costs in terms of reduced GDP growth.

## The Anti-Textbook Critique

### Structural Bias Against Government and Redistribution

The chapter on government and redistribution typically appears near the *end* of the textbook — the 20th chapter of 22, or the 19th of 20, in most surveyed texts. The implicit message: distribution is secondary to efficiency. David George (1990): textbooks consistently frame government as emerging *after* the private sector and "interfering" with it, rather than as constitutive of the market system.

The textbooks use language that encodes a pro-market bias:
- Government "uses up" resources (as if the population gets no benefit).
- Taxes are "imposed" (as if democracy doesn't exist).
- Rent controls "create" shortages; the competitive market only "creates" surpluses through price floors (which are presented as irrational behaviour by suppliers).

### The Equity–Efficiency Trade-off Is Largely a Myth

**Peter Lindert** (2004), in a major empirical study:
- Examined about 20 OECD countries between 1960 and 1995.
- Found **no significant effect** of social transfers on economic growth.
- "It is well-known that higher taxes and transfers reduce productivity. Well known — but unsupported by statistics or history."

**Denmark**: Tax revenues are 50% of GDP (Table 9.1). Denmark has very low income inequality (Table 9.2), almost no child poverty (Table 9.4), and is among the world's happiest countries (Figure 4.4). If the equity–efficiency trade-off were real, Denmark should be economically stagnant. It is not.

**Nordic countries generally**: High-tax, high-redistribution welfare states outperform the textbook prediction. Gunnar Myrdal showed that welfare reforms in Sweden and Scandinavia *enhanced* growth by investing in human capital (housing, nutrition, health, education).

**The real trade-off (Figure 9.4)**: Over the observed range of income inequality in OECD countries, the relationship between growth and equality is essentially flat. The equity–efficiency trade-off is only theoretical; in practice, it appears not to exist within the relevant range.

**New growth theory**: "Endogenous growth" models show that investing in human capital — precisely what welfare states do — raises growth rates. Inequality at the *bottom* of the distribution retards growth by limiting human capital accumulation; only inequality at the top may have ambiguous effects.

### The Pareto Principle Is Not Value-Free

The textbook uses **Pareto improvement** as its criterion for good policy: if some gain and none lose, society is better off. This appears innocuous, but:
- It accepts the existing distribution of income as given and just.
- If people care about relative income (as the evidence in Chapter 4 shows), a policy that raises some incomes while holding others constant may make those "unchanged" people feel *worse off* — and the Pareto principle claims otherwise.
- Martin Feldstein's version: "I am interested only in evaluating changes that increase the incomes of high-income individuals without decreasing the incomes of others. Such a change clearly satisfies the common-sense Pareto principle." He dismisses those who object as "spiteful egalitarians." This value judgement is hidden in what appears to be a technical criterion.
- The compensation principle (potential Pareto improvement): if winners could compensate losers and still be better off, the policy is "good." But actual compensation is almost never paid.

### The Pervasive Costs of Inequality: Health

The textbook is silent on the strongest empirical argument for redistribution: inequality kills people.

**The social gradient of health** (Marmot 2004, Wilkinson and Pickett 2009):
- At any level of income, health declines the lower your social position.
- In the UK Whitehall studies: civil servants in lower grades have dramatically higher rates of coronary heart disease than those one rung above them — *independent* of lifestyle factors.
- Washington DC: 20-year difference in life expectancy between the poorest and wealthiest areas.
- Glasgow: men in affluent areas live to 82; men in deprived areas average 54.
- If African-Americans had the same death rates as whites in the US, there would have been 886,000 fewer deaths between 1991 and 2000.

The World Health Organization's Commission on the Social Determinants of Health (2008): "Social injustice is killing people on a grand scale."

The mechanism: chronic stress from low social status activates the fight-or-flight response continuously, suppressing immune function and cardiovascular health over time.

These findings are entirely ignored in mainstream textbooks. Their inclusion would make the case for redistribution as a public health intervention — an argument with economic force that transcends political philosophy.

## Hidden Assumptions

1. The efficiency–equity trade-off is large and unavoidable.
2. Redistribution necessarily reduces work incentives and growth.
3. The Pareto principle is value-free.
4. Income distribution is a political question outside the scope of economic analysis.
5. Social inequality has no economic costs.

## Main Examples and Evidence

- Lindert (2004): no significant negative effect of social transfers on growth.
- Denmark/Nordic data: high taxation, high equality, high happiness, strong growth.
- Marmot (2004): social gradient of health; WHO Commission report (2008).
- Wilkinson and Pickett (2009): *The Spirit Level* — comprehensive evidence that inequality harms health across multiple domains.
- Glasgow life expectancy data: 54 vs. 82 years.
- Feldstein's explicit statement about Pareto improvements and "spiteful egalitarians."
- Amiel and Cowell (1999): most people don't accept Feldstein's version of the Pareto principle.

## Ideological Issues

The placement of this topic at the back of the book, combined with the uncritical presentation of the equity–efficiency trade-off, functions to marginalise concerns about distribution. The textbook presents what is in fact a substantial empirical and ethical debate as if it were settled: more redistribution means less efficiency. The evidence does not support this.

Hirschman (1991): this follows the "rhetoric of reaction" — opponents of welfare state programmes argue that social reforms have "unacceptable costs," a rhetorical move that has been made against every expansion of social rights for 200 years.

## Why This Chapter Matters

It demonstrates that the textbook's most politically consequential claim — that redistribution is costly — is empirically contestable at best and false at worst within the relevant policy range. The social gradient of health shows that inequality has enormous costs that the textbook entirely ignores.

## Standard vs. Anti-Textbook Contrast

| Standard Textbook | Anti-Textbook |
|---|---|
| Equity–efficiency trade-off is real and significant | Empirically, the trade-off is negligible within observed range |
| Redistribution reduces growth | High-tax welfare states have not underperformed |
| Pareto principle is value-neutral | It encodes acceptance of existing inequality |
| Distribution is a political question, not economic | Inequality kills people — this has economic costs |
| Growth increases well-being | Inequality creates health costs that offset growth benefits |

## Memory Hook

**"Denmark proves it: you can have high taxes, low inequality, high happiness, and strong growth — the textbook's trade-off exists mainly on the chalkboard."**

# CHAPTER 10: TRADE AND GLOBALIZATION WITHOUT THE ROSE-TINTED GLASSES

## Standard Textbook View

Free trade is unambiguously beneficial. Based on **comparative advantage**, specialisation and trade raise total world output. Both countries gain; adjustment costs are transitional. **Tariffs and import protection** create deadweight losses; free trade agreements increase global welfare. **Foreign direct investment (FDI)** brings capital, technology, and jobs to developing countries. The **World Trade Organisation** creates rules that benefit all members.

## The Anti-Textbook Critique

### The Comparative Advantage Model Is Inadequate (Revisiting Chapter 2)

As shown in Chapter 2, the comparative advantage model:
- Is presented without empirical testing.
- Predicts trade between dissimilar countries; but most trade is between *similar* countries (over 70% of industrialised country exports go to other industrialised countries).
- Assumes constant technology (no learning-by-doing, no economies of scale).
- Was relevant in Ricardo's time (1817) when capital flows were negligible; today they dominate exchange rate determination and make the "terms of trade" argument obsolete.
- Does not account for community destruction or the permanence of distributional losses.

Paul Samuelson (2004): "the popular polemical untruth" that free trade must produce net gains for all. Offshoring can permanently lower the real wages of workers in the home country when the foreign country's productivity catches up in the exported sector.

### Tariffs and the Infant Industry Argument

The textbook dismisses protection as always creating deadweight losses. But:
- The US, Canada, Germany, and Japan all industrialised behind high tariff walls.
- "Infant industry" protection with **strategic trade policy** can enable countries to build industries with economies of scale and learning-by-doing effects that create permanent competitive advantages.
- This is not just theory: East Asian development (South Korea, Taiwan) relied heavily on industrial policy and targeted protection.

The textbook's selective historical memory: presenting free trade as unambiguously superior to countries that grew rich precisely by ignoring that advice is hypocritical.

### Trade Produces Distributional Consequences

The textbook uses the **compensation principle**: free trade increases total welfare, so winners could compensate losers and still be ahead. But:
- Compensation is almost never paid.
- Adjustment costs fall disproportionately on specific communities, workers, and industries — permanently, not temporarily.
- The Stolper-Samuelson theorem (standard neoclassical trade theory) predicts that free trade reduces real wages of the scarce factor in each country. For labour-abundant developing countries this is good; for labour in developed countries, free trade with low-wage countries reduces real wages. This is textbook theory that most textbooks downplay.
- Community destruction has no monetary equivalent.

### Power and the Rules of Global Trade

The textbook treats WTO rules as welfare-improving. But:
- **Developed countries** maintain substantial agricultural subsidies while demanding agricultural market access from developing countries — a double standard that has been widely documented.
- **Intellectual property rules** (TRIPS) extend pharmaceutical patents to developing countries at enormous cost in access to medicines.
- **Big country power**: the US can use its market access as leverage to impose trade rules (including investment protection, patent law) that serve American corporate interests.
- Free trade agreements often have little to do with *free trade* and much to do with protecting intellectual property and facilitating capital mobility.

### Multinational Corporations

The textbook virtually ignores multinational corporations (MNCs) despite their central role in global trade and investment. What the textbook omits:

- **FDI serves investor interests, not host country interests** in many cases: the infant formula example in the Philippines (Chapter 4) shows US government pressuring the Philippines Supreme Court to overturn a health regulation at the request of the US Chamber of Commerce — reversed four days later under economic pressure.
- **British imperialism** was essentially a mechanism for transferring income from the British middle class (via taxes for military and administrative overhead) to upper-class investors earning protected returns abroad (Davis and Huttenback 1988).
- **US military interventions** for corporate interests: Iran 1953 (Anglo-American oil companies), Guatemala 1954 (United Fruit Company), Chile 1973 (ITT). General Smedley Butler's famous self-description of US Marines as "high-class muscle men for Big Business."
- **Colombia**: US military aid to the Colombian government that has presided over the killing of 2,000+ trade unionists. The US concluded a free trade agreement with Colombia in this context.

### Environmental Costs of Trade

The textbook ignores the environmental externalities of global trade:
- Transporting goods from one side of the world to the other generates substantial greenhouse gas emissions.
- Global supply chains externalise environmental costs in countries with weak regulation.
- The **"pollution haven" hypothesis**: production migrates to countries with weaker environmental standards, effectively exporting the externality.

### Fair Trade

The textbook does not discuss the **fair trade movement** — the recognition that standard trade terms systematically undervalue the labour and environmental contributions of small producers in developing countries, and that alternative trade relationships can be structured around equity rather than pure price competition.

## Hidden Assumptions

1. Free trade produces net gains that benefit all.
2. Adjustment costs are temporary.
3. The WTO creates neutral, mutually beneficial rules.
4. MNCs are primarily beneficial for host countries.
5. Comparative advantage is empirically valid and policy-relevant.

## Main Examples and Evidence

- Samuelson (2004): offshoring and net losses from trade.
- Historical protectionism in US, Canada, Germany (infant industry success).
- Stolper-Samuelson theorem implications for wages.
- Philippines infant formula case: US government pressure on Supreme Court.
- Davis and Huttenback (1988): British imperial income transfers.
- General Butler's account of US Marine interventions.
- Colombia: trade unionists killed; free trade agreement signed.
- Worm et al. (2006): fisheries collapse (connection to trade in fishing products).

## Ideological Issues

"The case for free international trade is really part of a more general case for free markets" (William Poole). This is why the standard argument for free trade is so resistant to qualification: conceding limits to comparative advantage undermines the broader ideological project of laissez-faire.

## Why This Chapter Matters

Trade and globalisation are central to contemporary politics. Students need an account that includes distributional consequences, power relations, and environmental externalities — not just efficiency gains based on a 1817 model.

# CHAPTER 11: CONCLUSION

## Main Points Gathered Together

The conclusion systematically lists what is unsatisfactory about mainstream introductory microeconomics:

1. **Positive/normative distinction**: Value judgements pervade so-called positive economics from the first definition. The textbook's main normative thrust is an implicit endorsement of laissez-faire.

2. **Rational individual**: Bounded rationality, bounded willpower, bounded selfishness are well established empirically but make minimal impact on textbooks. "Animal spirits" and systematic cognitive biases are real and important, especially in financial markets.

3. **Efficiency over equity**: Equity is always subordinate; the equity–efficiency trade-off is asserted without adequate evidence; the health costs of inequality are ignored entirely.

4. **"More is better"**: The textbook assumes utility increases with consumption, ignoring the Easterlin paradox and the evidence that growth does not increase average happiness in wealthy countries.

5. **Methodological problems**: Hypothesis testing is more difficult than textbooks acknowledge; core propositions are protected by auxiliary assumptions and cannot be empirically refuted.

6. **Overemphasis on perfect competition**: Applied as a generic model to all markets, creating a universal anti-intervention bias.

7. **Ignoring power**: Corporate power over markets, government, and public opinion is invisible. Monopsonistic power in labour markets is acknowledged as rare but is actually pervasive.

8. **Free trade bias**: Textbooks stack the deck in favour of free trade by omitting distributional consequences, historical evidence, and power relations.

9. **Ignoring community**: Markets undermine community; this has real costs that cost-benefit analysis cannot capture. The community is invisible to economists.

10. **Ignoring ecology**: "Empty-world economics" taught in a world where ecological limits are increasingly binding. Nature has been removed from economics since 1880 (McNeil 2000).

### Prasch's Classification (Table 11.2)

The invisible hand works only for "inspection goods" traded in "spot markets." Most real goods are "experience goods" (you only know quality after purchase) or are traded through "relational contracts" (ongoing relationships involving fairness and need). The textbook treats all goods as widgets, ignoring the blank cells where regulation is needed.

### Akerlof and Shiller's Classification (Table 11.1)

Mainstream textbooks occupy only the upper-left cell: economic motives + rational responses. The blank cells — non-economic motives, irrational responses — are where much real behaviour occurs, and where financial crises originate.

**"The textbook lives in one cell of a four-cell table. The other three cells are where most of economic life actually happens."**

# POSTSCRIPT: A CASE STUDY ON THE GLOBAL FINANCIAL MELTDOWN

## The Crisis Illustrates the Anti-Textbook's Themes

The 2008 financial crisis is the book's culminating case study, demonstrating that every blind spot the textbook has produced a real-world disaster:

**Imperfect and asymmetric information**:
- Banks couldn't assess the creditworthiness of borrowers in securitised packages.
- Credit rating agencies had incentives to assign generous ratings (paid per rating).
- Investors couldn't assess the true risk of "alphabet soup" financial instruments (CDOs, SIVs, ABCPs).
- NINJA mortgages (No Income, No Job, No Assets) proliferated because the originating banks sold the loans on immediately.

**Externalities**:
- Bank risk-taking imposes systemic risk on the entire financial system.
- One bank's failure threatens all connected banks — a "systemic externality" (Kaufman and Scott 2003).
- When banks became suspicious of each other, interbank lending froze, creating the credit crunch.

**Limited rationality**:
- Real estate and stock bubbles are driven by overconfidence fuelled by self-reinforcing narratives ("real estate always goes up because land is limited and population grows").
- Irrational pessimism characterises the downswing — fire-sale dynamics that destroy real wealth.

**Principal-agent problems**:
- Bank managers focused on maximising short-term bonus income, not long-term shareholder value.
- CDO structures created incentives for mortgage originators to ignore creditworthiness.
- Mortgage brokers were paid on volume, not quality.

**Deregulation**:
- Glass-Steagall Act (1933) separated commercial and investment banking — repealed in 1999 (Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act).
- Commodity Futures Modernization Act (2000): credit default swaps explicitly excluded from regulation.
- 1980 and 1982: mortgage interest rates and terms deregulated.

**Corporate power over government**:
- Financial industry spent hundreds of millions lobbying against regulation.
- AIG was "too big to fail" — bailed out with $182 billion after its credit default swap exposure became catastrophic.
- Stiglitz (2003): deregulation "made a lot of people very rich" — those who profited invested in lobbying to maintain the environment that produced those profits.

## The Lesson

Rodrik (2009): economists "became over-confident in their preferred models of the moment: markets are efficient, financial innovation transfers risk to those best able to bear it, self-regulation works best." This resulted in policy failures that nearly caused a second Great Depression.

The textbook's world — rational agents, efficient markets, no externalities, minimal government — cannot explain the financial crisis. Understanding it requires precisely the tools the textbooks omit: asymmetric information, externalities, limited rationality, and the analysis of power.

# PART II: SYNTHESIS AND THEMATIC OVERVIEW

## Major Recurring Themes

### 1. Ideology Masquerading as Science

The single most important theme: textbook economics presents a particular ideology as neutral science. The neoclassical paradigm encodes:
- Individualism over community.
- Efficiency over equity.
- Markets over government.
- Growth as the central goal.

These are value judgements, not facts. They align with what Hill and Myatt call "classical liberalism" or conservative political philosophy. Students absorb this framework without being aware it is an ideological choice.

### 2. The Invisible-Hand Story Requires Impossible Conditions

For competitive markets to produce efficient outcomes, you need:
- Perfect competition (rare in practice).
- Perfect information (impossible in most markets).
- No externalities (ubiquitous in practice).
- A just income distribution (not a technical economic result).
- All markets existing (futures markets, environmental markets — most don't).

When any condition fails — and all of them fail constantly — the efficiency story collapses. Yet the textbook presents it as a robust approximation to reality.

### 3. Power Is Absent

The textbook world has no corporate power, no lobbying, no media manipulation, no monopsony in labour markets, no military-industrial complex, no regulatory capture. This is not simplification — it is distortion. Power shapes prices, wages, regulations, and therefore the distribution of income. Without power, you cannot understand capitalism.

### 4. Inequality Has Costs That Are Systematically Ignored

Income inequality is treated as a political problem to be traded off against efficiency. The evidence says otherwise:
- Inequality reduces social trust and growth.
- Inequality kills people through the social gradient of health.
- Inequality fuels positional consumption arms races that reduce welfare.
- Inequality concentrates political power, undermining democracy.

### 5. The Methodological Double Standard

The textbook claims to teach empirical science but:
- Does not test models against evidence.
- Ignores evidence that contradicts core propositions (minimum wage research).
- Applies the competitive model as a generic tool without checking whether it fits.
- Presents consensus where breakdown has occurred.

### 6. Growth Does Not Maximise Well-Being

The Easterlin paradox and the evidence from happiness research: in wealthy countries, more GDP growth does not translate into more happiness. The textbook's central metric (efficiency in maximising output) is thus not the same as the goal it claims to pursue (maximising well-being).

## The Authors' Overall Critique of Introductory Economics Textbooks

Introductory economics textbooks are:
1. **Uniform and ideologically narrow**: they present a single worldview without acknowledging alternatives.
2. **Factually misleading** on key empirical questions (minimum wages, rent control, equity–efficiency trade-off).
3. **Selective in evidence**: they present evidence supporting the competitive model and ignore contrary evidence.
4. **Structurally biased**: topics are ordered to emphasise efficiency and downplay equity; government appears as an afterthought, an "interferer" rather than a constitution of the market system.
5. **Scientifically pretentious**: they claim empirical validation that the evidence does not support.

## The Book's Vision of Markets, Firms, Consumers, Government, Inequality, and Globalization

- **Markets**: often valuable but fundamentally imperfect — riddled with externalities, asymmetric information, and market power. Not a universal organising principle.
- **Firms**: political actors as much as economic ones. Corporate power shapes the rules of the game. The capitalist firm is not the only form; worker cooperatives are viable alternatives.
- **Consumers**: their preferences are partly formed by producers; they operate with imperfect information; their well-being depends on relative position, not just absolute consumption.
- **Government**: not an "interferer" but a constitutive part of the market system. Markets require legal frameworks, regulation, and trust to function. Government provision of public goods, social insurance, and redistribution is consistent with strong economic performance.
- **Inequality**: not just a fairness issue but an efficiency issue. Inequality kills people, reduces trust, reduces growth, and corrupts democracy. The equity–efficiency trade-off is largely imaginary within the policy-relevant range.
- **Globalization**: real gains exist from specialisation and trade, but they are unevenly distributed; adjustment costs are real and persistent; and the rules of global trade reflect power, not just comparative advantage.

# PART III: STUDY TOOLS

## 20 Most Important Ideas in the Whole Book

1. **Economics is not value-free**: every definitional choice, model selection, and topic ordering encodes ideological commitments.

2. **The definition of economics as "unlimited wants + scarce resources"** already frames the discipline toward efficiency rather than equity or need-satisfaction.

3. **Supply and demand = perfect competition**: the generic tool is only valid for a market structure that is almost nonexistent.

4. **Competitive markets require perfect information**: even infinitesimally small search costs destroy competitive pricing (Stiglitz's epsilon argument).

5. **Corporate marginal costs are flat or declining** (Blinder et al.): the law of diminishing returns is empirically irrelevant to most real firms.

6. **Externalities are not exceptions — they are the rule**: pollution, workplace hazards, financial system risk, and consumption spillovers make market failure the normal condition.

7. **The Easterlin Paradox**: income growth in wealthy countries does not raise average happiness. More stuff ≠ more well-being.

8. **The social gradient of health**: inequality kills people through chronic stress. This is ignored entirely by mainstream textbooks.

9. **The equity–efficiency trade-off is largely a myth** (Lindert 2004): welfare states with high taxes and redistribution have not suffered lower growth.

10. **Corporate power shapes public policy** through lobbying, "astroturf" campaigns, rational ignorance of citizens, and media influence (Olson, Galbraith).

11. **Bounded rationality, bounded willpower, bounded selfishness** (Kahneman, Thaler): systematic departures from the rational-choice model undermine the invisible-hand story.

12. **Preferences are partly formed within the economic system** through advertising and social comparison — the textbook's assumption that preferences are exogenous is a political choice.

13. **The Cambridge Capital Controversy**: there is no logical foundation for measuring the "quantity of capital" independently of the distribution of income. Marginal productivity theory of capital distribution is internally inconsistent.

14. **Monopsony power is pervasive in labour markets**: minimum wages can increase employment by offsetting employer market power.

15. **The compensation principle legitimises inequality**: the claim that trade (or any policy) is "good" because winners could compensate losers ignores that compensation is almost never paid.

16. **Financial markets are prone to bubbles and crashes** driven by asymmetric information, systemic externalities, and irrational herding — not efficiently reflecting fundamental values.

17. **Community is invisible to mainstream economics**: markets undermine community ties; this has real costs that are excluded from cost-benefit analysis by definition.

18. **The paradigm determines what can be seen**: economics is taught as a single paradigm (neoclassical) without acknowledging alternatives. Students cannot think outside a box they don't know they're in.

19. **Deregulation caused the 2008 financial crisis**: the repeal of Glass-Steagall and the exclusion of credit default swaps from regulation were products of corporate lobbying enabled by the ideology that markets self-regulate.

20. **The textbook's positive/normative distinction is itself normative**: presenting economics as objective science that avoids value judgements is itself a value judgement — one that serves the status quo.

## 10 Strongest Examples or Empirical Challenges Used by the Authors

1. **Blinder et al. (1998) on firm costs**: 89% of firms have constant or declining short-run marginal costs. This directly falsifies the U-shaped cost curve on which perfect competition rests — and is presented in mainstream textbooks without acknowledgment.

2. **Arnott (1995) on rent control**: Modern second-generation rent controls have "almost imperceptible" effects in most North American cities. The textbook predicts devastating shortages. The empirical reality contradicts the theory.

3. **The minimum wage consensus breakdown**: Surveys show percentage of economists who "generally agree" that minimum wages cause unemployment fell from 68% in 1979 to 46% in 2003. Textbooks continue to claim consensus.

4. **Easterlin Paradox (1974, 1995)**: US average happiness has been flat since 1946 despite enormous income growth. Within countries, richer people are happier; but nationally, growth doesn't improve happiness. The textbook's implicit assumption that growth maximises welfare is empirically false.

5. **The social gradient of health (Marmot 2004)**: Glasgow: men in deprived areas die at 54; men in affluent areas at 82. This 28-year gap is entirely preventable — it results from the social determinants of health, i.e., inequality. Ignored entirely in mainstream texts.

6. **Lindert (2004) on the equity–efficiency trade-off**: 30+ years of OECD data show no significant negative relationship between social transfers and economic growth. The trade-off asserted as central in textbooks is not visible in the data.

7. **Infant formula and child mortality**: WHO and UNICEF estimate aggressive marketing of infant formula causes over 1 million preventable infant deaths per year. This is a direct consequence of the textbook's assumptions about consumer sovereignty and information.

8. **Asbestos: 100,000 cancers per year** despite industry knowing about dangers since the late 19th century. The Canadian government lobbied against international asbestos bans while exporting the product to developing countries with minimal protection.

9. **Card and Krueger (1994) New Jersey/Pennsylvania minimum wage experiment**: No increase in unemployment following a substantial minimum wage increase in New Jersey relative to Pennsylvania. Published in *American Economic Review*; triggered a research programme that broke the supposed consensus.

10. **Samuelson (2004) on offshoring**: A Nobel laureate and primary architect of modern neoclassical theory says the claim that free trade must always produce net gains is a "popular polemical untruth." Textbooks continue to assert it.

## 10 Possible Essay Questions with Model Answer Directions

**1. "Economics is a value-free science." Critically evaluate this claim with reference to Hill and Myatt's analysis.**

*Model direction*: Show how value judgements enter at every stage — the definition of economics, the choice of market structure to emphasise, topic ordering, treatment of equity, and the positive/normative distinction itself. Use McCloskey on rhetoric, Heilbroner on ideology, and specific textbook examples (minimum wage consensus, treatment of rent control). Conclude that acknowledging ideological content is necessary for critical engagement with economic claims.

**2. To what extent is the supply and demand model a useful tool for economic analysis?**

*Model direction*: Explain what supply and demand requires (price-taking behaviour, perfect information, identical products). Show Blinder et al.'s evidence that most firms are price-makers. Discuss Stiglitz's epsilon argument. Acknowledge that the model may give useful directional predictions in some contexts while showing that its universal application creates systematic biases (especially against government intervention). Use rent control and minimum wage examples.

**3. "The equity–efficiency trade-off means that redistribution is always costly." Discuss.**

*Model direction*: Explain Okun's leaky bucket and the standard textbook argument. Present Lindert's empirical finding of no significant trade-off in OECD data. Discuss the Nordic countries as counterexamples. Show how inequality has costs (health, social cohesion, trust, political corruption) through Marmot and Wilkinson/Pickett. Conclude that the trade-off exists in theory but is not observed within the policy-relevant range.

**4. How well does the marginal productivity theory explain income distribution?**

*Model direction*: Explain the theory and its requirements (competitive markets, constant returns to scale, measurable capital). Present the Cambridge Capital Controversy — reswitching shows capital's "marginal product" cannot be determined independently of income distribution. Show empirically that identical workers earn different wages (monopsony, efficiency wages). Discuss CEO pay evidence (Bebchuk and Fried). Conclude that power and bargaining are essential supplements or alternatives to marginal productivity.

**5. "Externalities are exceptional, and market mechanisms can correct them." Critically evaluate.**

*Model direction*: Define externalities. Show that they are pervasive — air pollution, workplace hazards, financial system risk, consumption spillovers. Present evidence (2 million air pollution deaths per year; asbestos; fisheries collapse; financial crisis). Analyse why correction is difficult: corporate suppression of information, lobbying against regulation, regulatory capture, rational ignorance. Conclude that externalities are central to understanding modern capitalism, not peripheral.

**6. Is free trade always beneficial?**

*Model direction*: Explain comparative advantage and the textbook case. Show Samuelson's critique. Discuss the infant industry argument and historical evidence from the US, Germany, South Korea. Present Stolper-Samuelson and distributional consequences. Analyse WTO power dynamics. Show how community destruction is a permanent cost excluded from the welfare calculus. Conclude that free trade has real benefits but also real costs; its effects depend on which rules govern it and who bears the adjustment costs.

**7. How does behavioural economics challenge mainstream economic theory?**

*Model direction*: Explain rational choice theory's core assumptions. Present Kahneman's systematic biases (framing, overconfidence, cognitive errors). Discuss bounded willpower (Save More Tomorrow), bounded selfishness (ultimatum game), and crowding out of altruism. Show textbooks' inadequate response. Discuss implications for consumer sovereignty, financial market efficiency, and government policy (nudging). Conclude that behavioural insights are incompatible with the strong form of rational-choice theory central to the invisible-hand argument.

**8. "Perfect competition is an ideal standard against which other market structures should be assessed." Critically evaluate.**

*Model direction*: Explain the efficiency properties of perfect competition. Show conditions required (perfect information, no externalities, just income distribution, all markets existing). Demonstrate that none are met. Discuss the equity problem: efficiency is relative to income distribution, not absolute. Discuss Schumpeter on dynamic efficiency. Show that the competitive model actually ignores innovation — capitalism's most important feature. Conclude that perfect competition is a useful theoretical benchmark of limited practical relevance.

**9. How does corporate power affect the working of markets and government policy?**

*Model direction*: Use Galbraith on the bipolar economy. Apply Olson's collective action logic — concentrated industries overcome the free-rider problem; dispersed consumers cannot. Present specific examples: healthcare lobby in the US, tobacco and asbestos industries, financial deregulation. Discuss the principal-agent problem and executive pay. Show how rational ignorance among citizens compounds the problem. Conclude that power is a central feature of capitalism that textbook economics systematically ignores.

**10. What is the Easterlin Paradox and why does it matter for economic policy?**

*Model direction*: Explain the paradox: richer people are happier within countries, but average happiness does not rise as countries get richer over time. Discuss explanations: adaptation, social comparison, positional arms race, time scarcity. Show Figure 4.5 (US happiness flat since 1946). Present Robert Frank's luxury fever analysis. Discuss implications: economic growth is not a reliable route to well-being in wealthy countries; redistribution, leisure, and public goods may do more for happiness than further consumption growth.

# FINAL SYNTHESIS: THE BOOK AS A WHOLE

*The Economics Anti-Textbook* is, at its core, a book about **intellectual freedom**. Its argument is that economics students are taught within a narrow ideological framework that presents itself as neutral science. The resulting worldview:

- Treats markets as efficient by default and government as an interferer.
- Treats inequality as a natural outcome of productive differences and redistribution as costly.
- Makes corporate power invisible.
- Defines human welfare in terms of consumption and GDP growth, ignoring evidence that this is a poor measure of well-being.
- Ignores community, ecology, and the historical conditions that have shaped existing market structures.

Hill and Myatt are not anti-economics. They draw on Nobel laureates, mainstream economists, and heterodox scholars alike. Their critique is not that the neoclassical paradigm is entirely wrong, but that:

1. It is **not the only legitimate framework**.
2. It is **presented as fact rather than one perspective among many**.
3. Its **empirical claims are often not supported** by the evidence.
4. Its **policy implications serve particular interests** — those of large corporations, high-income earners, and opponents of redistribution.
5. Its **omissions are as important as its inclusions**: externalities, power, inequality's health costs, behavioural limitations, and community destruction are all systematically excluded.

The book ends with a call for **critical thinking**: the ability to read economic texts with awareness of what they leave out, what assumptions they embed, and whose interests their conclusions serve. This is not cynicism — it is the prerequisite for genuine economic literacy.

As Joan Robinson wrote: "The purpose of studying economics is not to acquire a set of ready-made answers to economic questions, but to learn how to avoid being deceived by economists."
