# High-Density Revision Guide: *The Economics Anti-Textbook* by Rod Hill and Tony Myatt

## How to use this guide

This is a compressed revision guide built from the full study digest. It is designed for fast recall, essay planning, and last-stage exam review. Each section keeps the same three-part logic:

1. **Standard textbook claim**
2. **Anti-textbook critique**
3. **Critical-thinking takeaway**

Each chapter also includes a **memory hook** and **exam use** so that the argument is easier to remember and deploy under time pressure.

---

## Introduction: Our goals, audience and principal themes

### Memory hook
**Textbooks do not only simplify; they also frame.**

### Standard textbook claim
Introductory economics textbooks usually present economics as an objective science. They imply that economists mostly agree on core propositions, that models are tested against evidence, and that markets generally work well unless clear failures appear.

### Anti-textbook critique
Hill and Myatt argue that this picture is too clean. Textbooks select some questions and exclude others. They emphasize scarcity, efficiency, and market coordination while pushing power, inequality, ideology, and institutional conflict toward the edges. They therefore teach more than technique: they also teach a worldview.

The key point is not that all textbook writers are consciously ideological. It is that value judgments can be built into topic choice, language, sequencing, and silence. A book that treats distribution as secondary, market failure as exceptional, and private choice as the basic unit of analysis is already making philosophical commitments.

### Critical-thinking takeaway
Economics textbooks are not neutral mirrors of reality. They are structured arguments. A critical reader should ask:

- Why this definition of economics?
- Why this model first?
- What evidence is left out?
- What does the book treat as "normal"?

### Exam use
- Good opening frame for almost any essay on the book.
- Use it to explain the authors’ broader project: they are criticizing **mainstream textbook economics**, not economics as such.
- Link forward to methodology, market failure, inequality, and globalization.

## Chapter 1: What is economics? Where you start influences where you go

### Memory hook
**How you define economics determines what you think economics is for.**

### Standard textbook claim
Economics is usually introduced as the study of choice under scarcity. Individuals have unlimited wants, resources are scarce, and rational people make decisions by comparing marginal benefits and marginal costs. Markets then coordinate these choices efficiently, while government may step in to correct failures or redistribute.

### Anti-textbook critique
The authors argue that this starting point is not innocent. Defining economics around scarcity and choice privileges some questions over others. It downplays issues such as power, deprivation, needs, and the possibility that resources may be idle rather than scarce in any meaningful short-run sense. It also treats individual preference satisfaction as the basic moral unit, which already pushes economics in a liberal-individualist direction.

The chapter challenges several foundational ideas:

- **Unlimited wants** may not be a universal fact; wants are shaped by culture, advertising, status competition, and institutions.
- **Rational choice** is too narrow to capture fairness, habit, weakness of will, addiction, or framing effects.
- **Efficiency before equity** is a value choice, not a neutral starting point.
- **Community and cooperation** are obscured when economics is centered only on isolated individuals.

Behavioral evidence matters here. People do not always optimize in the neat textbook way. They use rules of thumb, respond to fairness, misjudge risk, procrastinate, and sometimes act cooperatively or altruistically even when self-interest predicts otherwise.

### Critical-thinking takeaway
The first chapter matters because it fixes the moral and analytical vocabulary of the rest of the textbook. If you begin with isolated rational choosers and scarcity, then market allocation will look natural. If you begin with needs, institutions, and unequal power, different questions become central.

### Main concepts
- scarcity
- opportunity cost
- marginal analysis
- rational choice
- behavioral economics
- market efficiency
- equity-efficiency trade-off
- individualism vs community

### Hidden assumptions
- preferences are stable and autonomous
- wants are given rather than socially shaped
- rationality is the normal baseline
- redistribution mainly interferes with efficiency
- the individual is the correct unit of analysis

### Exam use
- Use this chapter to explain why textbook economics is not "just descriptive."
- Strong essay contrast: **choice under scarcity** versus **economy as a system of power, provision, and distribution**.
- Connect directly to Chapters 4, 8, and 9.

## Chapter 2: Introducing economic models

### Memory hook
**Models are not just simplifications; they are selective stories.**

### Standard textbook claim
Textbooks say models simplify reality in order to isolate essentials. They are not judged by realism of assumptions, but by usefulness and predictive success. Early examples often include production possibility frontiers and comparative advantage, which are presented as elegant proofs of the gains from specialization and trade.

### Anti-textbook critique
Hill and Myatt argue that textbook defenses of models are much stronger in theory than in practice. Textbooks often praise testing and prediction, yet they do not consistently compare rival models or show how evidence actually settles disputes. In many controversial cases, evidence is mixed and model choice is more contested than textbooks admit.

The chapter’s main methodological criticism is that economists often present models as if their empirical status were obvious when it is not. The minimum wage debate is central here: textbooks often imply a settled professional consensus that higher minimum wages reduce employment, but empirical work is conflicted. This shows that:

- evidence can be disputed
- econometric results depend on method and framing
- "consensus" can be overstated
- ideology may shape what counts as convincing evidence

The authors also criticize the rhetorical defense of unrealistic assumptions. Saying a model is useful does not prove it is the best model for a problem. A persuasive diagram can function more like a parable than a verified explanation.

Comparative advantage is also treated critically. The core logic is internally neat, but its translation into real-world policy is much less straightforward once history, power, capital mobility, increasing returns, and uneven development are introduced.

### Critical-thinking takeaway
Do not ask only whether a model is elegant. Ask:

- Compared with what alternative?
- Supported by which evidence?
- Under what institutional conditions?
- Used for explanation, prediction, or persuasion?

### Main concepts
- simplification
- assumptions and prediction
- positive vs normative economics
- methodology
- comparative advantage
- minimum wage controversy
- paradigm and rhetoric

### Hidden assumptions
- evidence clearly falsifies bad models
- economists mainly agree because evidence is decisive
- simple trade models apply directly to complex global capitalism
- unrealistic assumptions are harmless if the conclusion is useful

### Exam use
- Strong chapter for essays on methodology.
- Good place to challenge the claim that economics works like a natural science.
- Link to Chapters 3, 8, and 10.

## Chapter 3: How markets work (in an imaginary world)

### Memory hook
**Supply and demand often describe a special world, not the general one.**

### Standard textbook claim
The supply-and-demand model explains market outcomes through the interaction of buyers and sellers. Prices adjust toward equilibrium. Price controls create shortages or surpluses. Minimum wages create unemployment. Tax incidence depends on elasticities. The model appears as a general theory of markets.

### Anti-textbook critique
The authors argue that this is misleading because the standard supply-and-demand framework quietly assumes a highly competitive world. It works most cleanly where firms are price takers, products are standardized, information is good, and adjustment is orderly. But many real markets do not look like this.

Major criticisms:

- the model often depends on **perfect competition**
- many firms are **price setters**, not price takers
- real prices are often **sticky**, administered, or strategic
- information is imperfect
- out-of-equilibrium adjustment is poorly explained
- some textbook applications, such as minimum wage analysis, are empirically weak

The chapter is not saying supply and demand is always useless. It is saying its range of validity is narrower than textbooks imply. When the model is taught as the default description of markets, it predisposes students to distrust regulation and expect simple distortions from intervention.

### Critical-thinking takeaway
Before using a supply-and-demand diagram in policy analysis, ask whether the market is actually close to the model’s assumptions. A neat equilibrium picture may be analytically convenient but socially misleading.

### Main concepts
- equilibrium
- elasticity
- comparative statics
- tax incidence
- price floors and ceilings
- price taking vs price setting
- multiple equilibria
- stability

### Hidden assumptions
- a unique stable equilibrium exists
- prices adjust smoothly
- firms do not strategically set price
- information is sufficient
- institutional context does not matter much

### Exam use
- Essential for essays on minimum wage, rent control, and tax incidence.
- Useful contrast: textbook markets are often imagined as more competitive than they really are.
- Links directly to Chapters 5, 6, and 8.

## Chapter 4: People as consumers

### Memory hook
**Consumer demand is not a pure map of welfare if preferences are shaped.**

### Standard textbook claim
Consumers maximize utility subject to a budget. Demand curves reflect marginal benefit. Consumer surplus measures gains from exchange. If consumers choose freely, markets generally improve welfare because they satisfy preferences.

### Anti-textbook critique
This chapter attacks the hidden premise that preferences are given, stable, informed, and independent. In reality:

- advertising shapes wants
- firms influence taste and identity
- many goods involve strong information asymmetry
- social comparison affects satisfaction
- more consumption does not automatically mean more happiness

This is where the critique of **consumer sovereignty** becomes sharp. If firms spend enormous sums shaping preferences, then observed demand cannot simply be treated as a natural expression of welfare. Similarly, if people consume positional goods to keep up with others, the social result may be wasteful even when each individual choice is "rational" in isolation.

The happiness evidence is important. While richer people within a society may often report greater satisfaction, rising average income over time in rich societies does not necessarily produce corresponding gains in happiness. This weakens the simple equation of market growth with human welfare.

### Critical-thinking takeaway
Demand curves can only be treated as welfare curves if preferences are autonomous and information is adequate. Where persuasion, status competition, and manipulation are central, that identification becomes fragile.

### Main concepts
- utility
- marginal utility
- demand
- consumer surplus
- endogenous preferences
- advertising
- asymmetric information
- positional goods
- happiness research

### Hidden assumptions
- preferences are exogenous
- more consumption means more welfare
- consumers can evaluate quality well
- utility is mostly independent of others’ consumption

### Exam use
- Strong chapter for essays on advertising, welfare economics, and consumer sovereignty.
- Useful for challenging the moral interpretation of market demand.
- Connects closely to Chapters 1, 7, and 9.

## Chapter 5: The firm

### Memory hook
**The firm is not just a production function and a cost curve.**

### Standard textbook claim
The firm combines inputs to produce output and usually aims to maximize profit. In the short run, diminishing marginal returns generate rising marginal cost. In the long run, economies and diseconomies of scale shape cost curves. Firms choose output where marginal revenue equals marginal cost.

### Anti-textbook critique
The authors argue that textbook theory of the firm rests too heavily on stylized cost curves with weak empirical support. Actual firms often do not face the neat upward-sloping marginal costs required by the standard supply story. Costs may remain flat over wide output ranges, and demand may constrain output more than rising costs do.

The critique goes further. Textbook accounts also hide:

- internal power within firms
- separation of ownership and control
- corporate political influence
- alternative forms such as cooperatives
- the role of advertising and strategy

The firm in the textbook is too technical and too apolitical. It looks like a neutral optimizing machine rather than a social institution shaped by hierarchy, conflict, bargaining, and political influence.

### Critical-thinking takeaway
If the standard cost structure is unrealistic, then the textbook derivation of supply and many downstream claims about competition become less convincing. Once the firm is seen as an institution of power as well as production, the whole market story changes.

### Main concepts
- production function
- diminishing marginal returns
- short run and long run
- marginal cost
- average cost
- returns to scale
- profit maximization
- corporate power

### Hidden assumptions
- firms have simple, measurable objectives
- cost curves take the textbook form
- firms are politically passive
- internal conflict is secondary

### Exam use
- Good for essays on realism of cost curves.
- Use to connect firm behavior with market structure in Chapter 6.
- Good bridge from consumer manipulation to corporate power.

## Chapter 6: Market structure and efficiency - or why perfect competition is not so perfect after all

### Memory hook
**Perfect competition is a benchmark with more authority than realism.**

### Standard textbook claim
Perfect competition is efficient because price equals marginal cost, firms earn zero economic profit in the long run, and resources are allocated to their highest-valued uses. Monopoly and other imperfect market structures create deadweight loss and inefficiency relative to this benchmark.

### Anti-textbook critique
Hill and Myatt do not deny that perfect competition is a coherent model. Their point is that textbooks treat it as the normal benchmark in ways that are misleading.

The central criticisms are:

- perfect competition is highly unrealistic
- static efficiency is too narrow a criterion
- distribution affects what counts as efficient
- second-best reasoning weakens simple "more competition = better outcome" conclusions
- dynamic efficiency may sometimes favor concentration or non-price competition
- the standard welfare triangles ignore power, innovation, institutions, and history

The chapter also stresses that **only in very specific conditions does the usual supply curve story apply cleanly**. This matters because textbook welfare economics is built on that framework.

Financial markets extend the critique. Even where many buyers and sellers exist, information problems, speculation, and herd behavior can produce bubbles and instability. Competition alone does not guarantee social efficiency.

### Critical-thinking takeaway
The key issue is not whether perfect competition is elegant. It is whether it deserves to be the moral and analytical yardstick for judging real economies. The authors think textbooks grant it too much authority.

### Main concepts
- perfect competition
- monopoly
- monopolistic competition
- oligopoly
- deadweight loss
- efficiency
- second best
- dynamic vs static efficiency
- efficient market hypothesis

### Hidden assumptions
- the benchmark is normatively appropriate
- distribution is separable from efficiency
- static welfare losses capture the main issue
- market structure can be judged apart from politics and institutions

### Exam use
- One of the most important chapters in the book.
- Central for essays on why textbook economics overstates the case for competitive markets.
- Links strongly to Chapters 3, 5, 7, and Postscript.

## Chapter 7: Externalities and the ubiquity of market failure

### Memory hook
**Externalities are not the exception; they are everywhere.**

### Standard textbook claim
Externalities arise when private actions impose costs or benefits on others. Examples include pollution and public goods. These are treated as special cases where markets fail and government may intervene through taxes, regulation, or property-right solutions.

### Anti-textbook critique
The authors argue that textbook treatment badly understates the scale of the problem. Externalities are not occasional glitches in an otherwise efficient system. They are woven into production, consumption, status competition, environmental damage, and even finance.

Examples include:

- pollution
- climate change
- health harms
- common-resource depletion
- positional competition
- systemic financial risk

This chapter is important because it destabilizes the textbook sequence. If externalities are pervasive, then the earlier story of efficient decentralized markets no longer describes the normal case. Market failure is not a footnote. It may be endemic.

The chapter also points to politics. Knowing that an externality exists does not automatically solve it. Powerful interests, weak information, and collective-action problems can block correction.

### Critical-thinking takeaway
Prices often fail to register real social costs. Any serious evaluation of markets must ask what is omitted from private calculation, and who benefits from that omission.

### Main concepts
- private vs social cost
- Pigouvian taxation
- public goods
- commons
- positional externalities
- climate change
- regulatory failure
- systemic risk

### Hidden assumptions
- externalities are marginal rather than pervasive
- government can costlessly and neutrally correct them
- private and social interests usually coincide

### Exam use
- Essential for essays arguing that market failure is normal rather than exceptional.
- Strong link to climate, inequality, and financial crisis.
- Good pivot chapter from market theory to political economy.

## Chapter 8: The marginal productivity theory of income distribution - or you are worth what you can get

### Memory hook
**Marginal productivity explains some pricing logic; it does not prove desert.**

### Standard textbook claim
In competitive markets, labor and capital are paid according to their marginal productivity. Wages reflect the value of what workers add at the margin. Income differences therefore largely reflect productivity differences, skills, training, and market conditions.

### Anti-textbook critique
This chapter challenges both the explanatory strength and the moral interpretation of the theory.

Key criticisms:

- real labor markets are often not perfectly competitive
- bargaining power and monopsony matter
- fairness norms influence pay
- industry wage differences challenge a pure productivity story
- capital theory is more problematic than textbooks admit
- executive pay is often disconnected from genuine marginal product

The theory slides too easily from a limited analytical claim to a legitimating moral claim: that market incomes are deserved because they reflect contribution. The authors resist that move. Distribution depends not just on productivity, but on ownership, power, institutions, norms, and market structure.

### Critical-thinking takeaway
You should separate three claims that textbooks often blur:

1. factors can be priced using marginal reasoning in some models
2. actual income patterns may diverge from those models
3. even if the model fit were strong, it would still not settle questions of justice

### Main concepts
- marginal revenue product
- labor demand
- union effects
- discrimination
- monopsony
- wage compression
- Cambridge capital controversies
- CEO pay

### Hidden assumptions
- labor markets are close to competitive
- marginal product is measurable
- market outcomes track contribution
- institutional power is secondary

### Exam use
- Strong essay chapter on wages, merit, and inequality.
- Use with Chapter 9 to challenge the idea that redistribution interferes with a basically just market process.
- Also connects to minimum wage debates from Chapters 2 and 3.

## Chapter 9: Government, taxation and the (re)distribution of income: is a just society just too expensive?

### Memory hook
**The bucket may leak, but inequality leaks too.**

### Standard textbook claim
Government redistributes through taxes and spending, but redistribution usually creates distortions. The classic presentation is the equity-efficiency trade-off: more equality can be achieved, but at some cost in incentives, output, or growth.

### Anti-textbook critique
Hill and Myatt argue that this framework is too narrow in both moral and empirical terms.

First, it treats the pre-tax market outcome as if it were a neutral baseline, when in fact that outcome already reflects power, ownership, institutions, and previous policy choices.

Second, it focuses heavily on the costs of redistribution while understating the costs of inequality itself. Inequality affects:

- health
- stress
- life chances
- social cohesion
- political influence
- status competition

Third, the trade-off itself is often exaggerated. Some equal societies perform very well economically. Redistribution can support education, trust, health, and capability development. It is not always a drag on growth.

### Critical-thinking takeaway
The proper question is not simply "How much efficiency do we lose by redistributing?" It is also "How much social damage do we accept by not redistributing?" The trade-off is not one-sided.

### Main concepts
- taxation
- deadweight loss
- Lorenz curve
- Gini coefficient
- poverty
- wealth inequality
- equity-efficiency trade-off
- health gradient

### Hidden assumptions
- market income distribution is the natural baseline
- efficiency can be read off from output alone
- inequality mainly raises moral rather than economic issues
- redistribution is mostly a burden

### Exam use
- Crucial chapter for essays on justice, taxation, and welfare state debates.
- Strong contrast chapter: textbook concern with costs of equality versus anti-textbook concern with costs of inequality.
- Links closely to Chapters 4, 7, and 8.

## Chapter 10: Trade and globalization without the rose-tinted glasses

### Memory hook
**Trade can enlarge the pie, but textbooks too quickly assume the pie story settles the argument.**

### Standard textbook claim
Trade based on comparative advantage benefits all countries. Tariffs create deadweight loss. Globalization increases efficiency, consumer choice, and long-run prosperity. Losses can in principle be compensated by gains elsewhere.

### Anti-textbook critique
The authors do not deny that trade can generate gains. Their critique is that textbooks present the optimistic case too confidently and too abstractly.

Problems with the standard picture:

- adjustment costs are real, not negligible
- gains are unevenly distributed
- compensation is often hypothetical, not actual
- increasing returns and path dependence can matter
- capital mobility changes the story
- environmental externalities are frequently ignored
- trade rules are shaped by power, not just mutual benefit

Globalization is therefore not just an efficiency story. It is also a story about labor standards, bargaining power, environmental damage, global institutions, and the political choices embedded in trade regimes.

### Critical-thinking takeaway
Whenever a trade policy is defended as making "the country" better off, ask:

- Who gains?
- Who loses?
- Are losers compensated?
- What environmental and institutional costs are excluded?
- Whose rules structure the market?

### Main concepts
- comparative advantage
- tariff analysis
- deadweight loss
- compensation principle
- increasing returns
- capital mobility
- multinational firms
- globalization governance

### Hidden assumptions
- transitions are smooth
- trade gains are broadly shared
- compensation is feasible and likely
- no major externalities distort the picture
- international rules are neutral

### Exam use
- Very useful for essays contrasting textbook free-trade optimism with a political-economy view of globalization.
- Link back to methodology in Chapter 2 and inequality in Chapter 9.

## Chapter 11: Conclusion

### Memory hook
**The book’s target is not economics, but a narrow and overconfident way of teaching it.**

### Standard textbook claim being summarized
By the end of a standard course, the student is often left with the impression that markets are usually efficient, that government mainly distorts, that distribution is secondary, and that controversy is limited around the main analytical framework.

### Anti-textbook critique
The conclusion draws together the book’s main line of attack:

- textbook economics is too narrow
- rationality is overstated
- power is downplayed
- efficiency is over-privileged
- externalities are marginalized
- inequality is under-theorized
- evidence is more contested than textbooks admit

The authors want students to see that introductory economics could be taught differently. It could be more plural, more empirically candid, more institutionally grounded, and more open about values.

### Critical-thinking takeaway
The best final reading of the book is that economics is richest when students stop treating introductory diagrams as self-evident truths and start treating them as contestable tools embedded in arguments about society.

### Exam use
- Use this as the concluding frame in almost any essay.
- Good place to summarize the book’s overall critique of textbook economics.

## Postscript: A case study on the global financial meltdown

### Memory hook
**The crisis showed what textbook markets miss: information problems, incentives, bubbles, and systemic risk.**

### Standard textbook claim being confronted
If markets are competitive and prices reflect information, then financial markets should allocate capital efficiently. Problems should be limited and self-correcting unless obvious policy distortions intervene.

### Anti-textbook critique
The financial crisis shows why this confidence is misplaced. The meltdown revealed:

- asymmetric information
- moral hazard
- principal-agent problems
- predatory lending
- opaque securitization
- rating-agency conflicts
- herd behavior and bubbles
- systemic externalities
- deregulatory political economy

This postscript matters because it applies the book’s earlier themes to a dramatic real-world event. The crisis makes the anti-textbook critique concrete. It becomes very difficult to believe in a simple world of informed rational actors, efficient finance, and harmless deregulation after a collapse of this scale.

### Critical-thinking takeaway
Finance is not a clean auction in standardized goods. It is a web of contracts, incentives, expectations, opacity, and public risk. The crisis therefore reinforces the book’s larger message: the textbook market model often leaves out the features that matter most.

### Exam use
- Excellent case-study ending for essays on imperfect information, regulation, and market failure.
- Use to show that the book’s critique is not merely theoretical.

## Major recurring themes of the whole book

### 1. Textbook economics presents a narrow worldview as neutral science
The authors repeatedly argue that selectivity is ideological. Defining economics in terms of scarcity and efficiency already organizes what students learn to care about.

### 2. Rational choice is too limited to carry the full weight textbooks place on it
Behavioral evidence, fairness, addiction, framing, and bounded willpower show that real decision-making is more complex.

### 3. Perfect competition is granted too much authority
It is an elegant model, but textbooks often treat it as if it were normal rather than highly special.

### 4. Efficiency language hides distribution and power
An outcome can be efficient relative to given incomes and yet still be deeply unequal, politically distorted, or socially harmful.

### 5. Market failure is widespread
Externalities, information asymmetries, market power, and instability are not rare exceptions.

### 6. Inequality matters economically, not just morally
It shapes health, status competition, social trust, and political power.

### 7. Government is often presented too negatively and too late
Textbooks highlight tax distortion more readily than public provision, redistribution, or corrective regulation.

### 8. Globalization cannot be evaluated only through gains-from-trade logic
Power, institutions, environment, and unequal adjustment matter.

## Fast synthesis for essays

If you need one paragraph that captures the entire book, use this structure:

1. Standard textbooks present economics as neutral science and markets as generally efficient.
2. Hill and Myatt argue that this presentation is selective and ideological.
3. The main blind spots are rationality limits, imperfect competition, preference shaping, externalities, inequality, power, and globalization’s unequal effects.
4. Their aim is not to reject economics, but to teach students to read its models critically.

## 10 possible essay questions with answer directions

1. **Why do Hill and Myatt argue that introductory economics is not value-free?**  
   Show how value judgments enter through definitions, sequencing, emphasis, and omissions.

2. **How does the book challenge rational choice theory?**  
   Use behavioral evidence, fairness, addiction, framing, and bounded willpower.

3. **Why do the authors think supply and demand is too often treated as a general theory?**  
   Emphasize perfect competition assumptions, price setting, imperfect information, and sticky prices.

4. **Can consumer demand be treated as a measure of welfare?**  
   Discuss exogenous preferences, advertising, information asymmetry, and positional competition.

5. **Why is the textbook theory of the firm incomplete?**  
   Cover cost curves, internal hierarchy, corporate power, and political influence.

6. **Why is perfect competition an inadequate benchmark for real economies?**  
   Explain realism problems, second-best logic, and distributional blindness.

7. **Why do the authors think market failure is ubiquitous?**  
   Use pollution, climate change, positional goods, and finance.

8. **Does marginal productivity theory justify inequality?**  
   Separate analytical pricing logic from moral desert.

9. **How does the book challenge the efficiency-equity trade-off?**  
   Show that inequality itself produces major social and economic harms.

10. **What does the global financial crisis contribute to the authors’ argument?**  
   Explain how it exposes the limits of efficient-market and deregulation optimism.

## Final one-paragraph revision summary

*The Economics Anti-Textbook* argues that introductory microeconomics textbooks present a narrow, market-friendly worldview as if it were neutral science. They rely too heavily on rational choice, supply-and-demand reasoning, perfect competition, and efficiency benchmarks, while underplaying power, inequality, advertising, behavioral limits, imperfect information, externalities, and political institutions. Hill and Myatt do not reject economics itself. Instead, they argue that students should treat textbook models as limited, contested, and value-laden tools. The book’s core lesson is that economics becomes more truthful and more intellectually useful when students ask what assumptions, omissions, and ideological commitments lie behind the diagrams.
