# *23 Things They Don't Tell You About Capitalism*  
## 10-Page High-Density Revision Sheet

### How to use this sheet

This is a compressed revision tool based on the full digest. It is designed for fast recall, essay planning, and chapter comparison. Each section gives you:

- a **memory hook**
- the **mainstream/free-market claim**
- **Chang's central reply**
- **core examples**
- **why the chapter matters**
- **main chapter links**

Read it in two passes:

1. **Vertical pass:** chapter by chapter to understand the whole book.
2. **Horizontal pass:** trace themes such as the state, firms, finance, labour, development, and inequality across chapters.

---

## Master argument of the whole book

Chang's overall claim is not that capitalism should be abolished. His claim is that **free-market capitalism is historically misleading, theoretically shallow, and politically damaging**. Markets do not exist outside politics. Productive development depends on institutions, collective organization, long-term investment, and public action. The best capitalism is therefore **regulated, developmental, socially embedded, and suspicious of short-term finance**.

The chapters move in a clear sequence:

1. **Things 1-6:** demolish basic myths about free markets, merit, selfishness, and macroeconomic orthodoxy.
2. **Things 7-12:** show that development depends on industrial policy, strategic regulation, and state capacity.
3. **Things 13-18:** attack trickle-down economics, inflated executive pay, myths of entrepreneurship, education fetishism, and the idea that firms automatically serve the national good.
4. **Things 19-23:** defend planning, welfare, and financial restraint, then criticize the economics profession that justified neoliberalism.

## Thing 1. There Is No Such Thing as a Free Market

**Memory hook:** *No market without rules.*

### Mainstream claim
Markets work best when left free. Regulation distorts exchange and blocks efficiency.

### Chang's reply
No market is naturally free. Every market is structured by laws and moral decisions about:

- what can be sold,
- who can sell,
- under what conditions exchange occurs,
- what counts as acceptable harm.

So "free market" is not a neutral description. It is a political preference disguised as economics.

### Core examples

- child labour laws once attacked as anti-market
- pollution controls once seen as infringements of liberty
- restrictions on votes, organs, drugs, and professional entry
- immigration controls and central-bank policy shaping wages and interest rates
- 2008 bank rescues showing massive state intervention inside "free enterprise"

### Why it matters
This is the foundation of the whole book. Once markets are seen as politically constructed, later debates on wages, trade, welfare, and finance look different.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 3:** wages are politically structured
- **Thing 7:** free trade is not historically neutral
- **Thing 16:** markets cannot be left alone because humans are limited

## Thing 2. Companies Should Not Be Run in the Interest of Their Owners

**Memory hook:** *Shareholders can exit; long-term stakeholders cannot.*

### Mainstream claim
Shareholders own firms and bear risk, so firms should maximize shareholder value.

### Chang's reply
Modern corporations exist because limited liability reduced shareholder risk. Shareholders, especially mobile ones, are often the least committed long-term participants. Shareholder-value governance pushes:

- cost-cutting,
- job cuts,
- lower investment,
- high dividends,
- share buybacks.

This can weaken both firms and economies.

### Core examples

- limited liability as the institutional basis of modern capitalism
- Adam Smith suspicious of limited liability; Marx more perceptive about it
- rise of professional managers and shareholder-value ideology
- GM as a case of long-term industrial weakening under short-term pressure
- Germany, Sweden, Japan, and France limiting floating-shareholder power

### Why it matters
Explains later chapters on executive pay, financial pressure, and industrial decline.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 14:** executive pay
- **Thing 18:** what helps firms may hurt nations
- **Thing 22:** finance intensifies short-termism

## Thing 3. Most People in Rich Countries Are Paid More Than They Should Be

**Memory hook:** *Wages follow borders, not just talent.*

### Mainstream claim
People are paid according to productivity. Rich-country workers earn more because they are more productive.

### Chang's reply
Huge wage gaps cannot mainly be explained by individual ability. They are sustained by:

- immigration control,
- institutional differences,
- system-level productivity.

Most rich-country workers are not personally dozens of times more productive than poorer-country counterparts doing similar jobs.

### Core examples

- Swedish versus Indian bus drivers
- limited relevance of formal schooling to many jobs
- immigration restrictions as hidden labour-market protectionism
- Warren Buffett on success depending on the system one stands in

### Why it matters
Destroys the moral claim that markets neatly reward merit. Also reframes development: poor countries are poor because of weak productive systems, not because their people are individually inferior.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 1:** political market boundaries
- **Thing 10:** cheap services and living standards
- **Thing 20:** fairness requires more than formal opportunity

## Thing 4. The Washing Machine Has Changed the World More Than the Internet Has

**Memory hook:** *Invisible old technologies can reshape life more than glamorous new ones.*

### Mainstream claim
The internet revolution defines our age, destroys distance, and makes older economic models obsolete.

### Chang's reply
Recent technology is often overrated because it is recent. The telegraph was more revolutionary in communication speed, and household technologies such as washing machines changed social life more deeply by:

- reducing domestic labour,
- enabling women's labour-force participation,
- changing family structure,
- reducing dependence on servants.

### Core examples

- decline of domestic service in rich countries
- washing machine, piped water, electricity, central heating
- women's rising labour-market participation
- telegraph versus internet speed comparison
- misplaced obsession with the digital divide

### Why it matters
Challenges technological determinism and supports Chang's later attack on post-industrial fantasies.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 9:** manufacturing still matters
- **Thing 17:** education and the "knowledge economy"
- **Thing 8:** globalization is shaped by politics, not just technology

## Thing 5. Assume the Worst About People and You Get the Worst

**Memory hook:** *Distrust is costly.*

### Mainstream claim
Because people are selfish, systems must rely on competition and incentives rather than trust.

### Chang's reply
Self-interest matters, but people are also motivated by:

- duty,
- loyalty,
- pride,
- solidarity,
- honesty,
- professionalism.

Large organizations cannot function if everyone behaves like a textbook opportunist.

### Core examples

- Kobe Steel manager on why trust is necessary in complex firms
- work-to-rule slowing output dramatically
- Japanese production systems using worker initiative
- taxi-payment honesty example

### Why it matters
Undermines the anthropology behind free-market theory and supports stronger roles for firms, public institutions, and cooperative organization.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 2:** firms are not just contracts
- **Thing 12:** public-private coordination
- **Thing 16:** rationality is limited

## Thing 6. Greater Macroeconomic Stability Has Not Made the World Economy More Stable

**Memory hook:** *Stable prices, unstable lives.*

### Mainstream claim
Low inflation creates stability, investment, and growth.

### Chang's reply
Moderate inflation is not the same as hyperinflation, and anti-inflation obsession has:

- raised real interest rates,
- weakened productive investment,
- increased unemployment and job insecurity,
- accompanied more financial crises.

Price stability is only one dimension of stability.

### Core examples

- German hyperinflation as real but exceptional
- Brazil and South Korea growing with moderate/high inflation
- banking crises rising in the low-inflation era
- labour insecurity as a form of instability ignored by orthodoxy

### Why it matters
Shows how policy can privilege creditors and asset holders while presenting itself as neutral common sense.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 22:** financial instability
- **Thing 13:** pro-rich policy and weak growth
- **Thing 21:** security matters more than inflation targeting alone

## Thing 7. Free-Market Policies Rarely Make Poor Countries Rich

**Memory hook:** *Rich countries preach free trade after climbing with protection.*

### Mainstream claim
Developing countries failed under protection and state intervention, then improved when they liberalized. Rich countries became rich through markets and openness.

### Chang's reply
Historically, most rich countries used:

- tariffs,
- subsidies,
- state ownership,
- capital controls,
- active industrial policy.

Many developing countries actually grew faster before neoliberal reform than after it.

### Core examples

- China today versus the US in the late nineteenth century
- Hamilton and the infant industry argument
- protectionist traditions of Washington, Lincoln, Grant, and Jackson
- Britain becoming free-trade only after industrial dominance
- Latin American and African slowdown after liberalization

### Why it matters
This is one of Chang's central historical arguments. It attacks the credibility of development orthodoxy at its root.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 11:** Africa and structural adjustment
- **Thing 12:** governments can pick winners
- **Thing 19:** planning remains essential

## Thing 8. Capital Has a Nationality

**Memory hook:** *Multinationals globalize production, not loyalty.*

### Mainstream claim
Capital is now nationless, so governments should not care whether firms are domestically or foreign owned.

### Chang's reply
Most multinationals keep their highest-value activities - especially strategy and top-end R&D - at home. Their nationality still matters for:

- decision-making,
- technological leadership,
- location of high-grade jobs,
- responsiveness to domestic political pressure.

### Core examples

- Carlos Ghosn as exceptional rather than typical
- Chrysler under American, German, then Italian control
- home-country bias in R&D and strategic management
- brownfield investment versus greenfield investment

### Why it matters
Shows that ownership is not an abstract legal issue; it affects who captures long-run development gains.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 9:** de-industrialization
- **Thing 18:** firm and national interests diverge
- **Thing 7:** policy space in development

## Thing 9. We Do Not Live in a Post-Industrial Age

**Memory hook:** *Factories still matter, even when fewer people work in them.*

### Mainstream claim
Rich countries can move beyond manufacturing into services and the knowledge economy. Poor countries might skip industry altogether.

### Chang's reply
De-industrialization is often misread. Manufacturing's GDP share falls largely because manufacturing productivity rises faster, making goods cheaper relative to services. Manufacturing still matters because it drives:

- productivity growth,
- exports,
- technological learning,
- balance-of-payments strength.

Most services are less tradable and often less dynamic.

### Core examples

- Britain as former and then declining "workshop of the world"
- computers versus haircuts
- outsourcing and reclassification effects
- Switzerland and Singapore as industrial, not merely service, success stories

### Why it matters
Directly challenges the claim that rich countries or poor ones can thrive while neglecting industry.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 4:** exaggerated technology stories
- **Thing 17:** education and knowledge-economy myths
- **Thing 18:** industrial decline and firm behaviour

## Thing 10. The US Does Not Have the Highest Living Standard in the World

**Memory hook:** *Average income is not the whole good life.*

### Mainstream claim
The US model delivers the world's highest living standard and proves the superiority of free-market capitalism.

### Chang's reply
Even where US income is very high, living standards must be judged by more than average purchasing power. Chang stresses:

- inequality,
- health outcomes,
- crime,
- leisure time,
- cheap service labour based on insecurity and immigration.

### Core examples

- PPP versus market exchange-rate comparisons
- cheap taxi rides and restaurant meals in the US
- poor US health and crime indicators relative to peers
- longer working hours than many Europeans

### Why it matters
Weakens the prestige of the American model and broadens the meaning of welfare.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 3:** labour-market protection
- **Thing 13:** inequality
- **Thing 21:** welfare and security

## Thing 11. Africa Is Not Destined for Underdevelopment

**Memory hook:** *Recent failure is not permanent fate.*

### Mainstream claim
Africa is poor because of climate, geography, ethnic division, bad institutions, and culture.

### Chang's reply
Africa did grow in the 1960s and 1970s, so recent stagnation cannot simply be the effect of fixed structural handicaps. Many rich countries also developed despite harsh climates, divisions, or resource wealth. The sharper break comes with:

- structural adjustment,
- forced liberalization,
- industrial collapse,
- dependence on commodity exports.

### Core examples

- Sub-Saharan growth before 1980
- IMF/World Bank structural adjustment
- Switzerland and Austria as landlocked success cases
- resource-rich US, Canada, and Australia
- old stereotypes about Germans and Japanese resembling new ones about Africans

### Why it matters
Challenges deterministic explanations of poverty and shifts attention back to policy.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 7:** free-market reform and development
- **Thing 12:** need for active industrial strategy
- **Thing 3:** systems matter more than blaming poor individuals

## Thing 12. Governments Can Pick Winners

**Memory hook:** *The question is not whether states fail, but whether they can learn and discipline.*

### Mainstream claim
Governments lack information and incentives, so industrial policy creates white elephants.

### Chang's reply
Private firms also make mistakes. Governments can gather information, cooperate with business, and back industries with strong future spillovers. They do not need omniscience, only enough institutional capacity to improve the batting average.

### Core examples

- POSCO
- Hyundai shipbuilding
- LG's shift into electronics-related sectors
- US support for semiconductors, aviation, biotech, internet
- Japanese councils and French policy networks
- Concorde as a failure that does not disprove all industrial policy

### Why it matters
This is Chang's strongest constructive chapter on development policy.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 7:** historical development
- **Thing 19:** planning
- **Thing 18:** regulation can help long-run business success

## Thing 13. Making Rich People Richer Doesn't Make the Rest of Us Richer

**Memory hook:** *No automatic trickle-down.*

### Mainstream claim
Giving more income to the rich encourages investment and growth, which later benefits everyone.

### Chang's reply
Since the 1980s, upward redistribution has not produced the promised growth acceleration. Investment often fell even as top incomes rose. And even where gains are created, they do not reliably spread without active redistribution.

### Core examples

- Preobrazhensky and the concentration of surplus
- postwar Golden Age under stronger welfare states
- post-1980 slowdown
- top 1 per cent and top 0.1 per cent income capture in the US

### Why it matters
This chapter attacks the main growth-based defense of inequality.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 14:** executive pay
- **Thing 21:** welfare can support growth
- **Thing 6:** pro-rich macroeconomic policy

## Thing 14. US Managers Are Over-Priced

**Memory hook:** *Elite pay tracks power more than value.*

### Mainstream claim
Very high CEO pay reflects scarce talent and huge responsibility.

### Chang's reply
US CEOs are paid vastly more than earlier generations and far more than peers abroad without clear performance superiority. Boards, shareholder alliances, and ideology allow managers to influence their own rewards.

### Core examples

- rise from roughly 30-40:1 to 300-400:1 CEO-worker pay ratios
- large gaps between US and Japanese/European pay
- stock options inflating total compensation
- golden parachutes and weak punishment for failure

### Why it matters
Concrete illustration of upward redistribution and weak market discipline at the top.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 2:** shareholder-manager alliance
- **Thing 13:** inequality
- **Thing 18:** long-run corporate damage

## Thing 15. People in Poor Countries Are More Entrepreneurial Than People in Rich Countries

**Memory hook:** *Poor countries lack high-productivity organization, not hustle.*

### Mainstream claim
Poor countries are poor because they lack entrepreneurship.

### Chang's reply
Poor countries are full of entrepreneurs, often because survival requires it. The problem is that most activity is low-productivity and weakly scalable. Development requires:

- collective entrepreneurship,
- productive firms,
- cooperatives,
- institutions,
- technology,
- finance that supports upgrading.

### Core examples

- very high self-employment rates in poor countries
- street trading and survival enterprise
- microfinance limits
- Bangladeshi telephone ladies and the fallacy of composition
- European cooperatives and industry associations

### Why it matters
Reframes entrepreneurship from an individual moral story to an organizational and structural one.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 17:** education versus enterprise
- **Thing 12:** industrial policy
- **Thing 5:** richer view of motivation

## Thing 16. We Are Not Smart Enough to Leave Things to the Market

**Memory hook:** *Bounded rationality justifies rules.*

### Mainstream claim
People know their own interests best; markets correct mistakes over time.

### Chang's reply
People are not fully rational. The world is too complex, especially in finance. Regulation can improve outcomes by:

- reducing complexity,
- banning opaque products,
- restricting dangerous choices.

This is not because the state is omniscient, but because everyone is cognitively limited.

### Core examples

- LTCM
- Madoff
- AIG and derivatives
- Herbert Simon on bounded rationality and chess
- approval analogy from drugs and other products

### Why it matters
Provides a deep theoretical basis for Chang's case against deregulation.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 5:** against pure self-seeking rational actors
- **Thing 22:** financial simplification
- **Thing 1:** political design of markets

## Thing 17. More Education in Itself Is Not Going to Make a Country Richer

**Memory hook:** *Education helps people; firms and institutions make countries productive.*

### Mainstream claim
Education, especially in the knowledge economy, is the key driver of national prosperity.

### Chang's reply
Education is valuable, but its relation to national growth is weak and indirect. Much schooling:

- has little direct relevance to productivity,
- acts as sorting rather than skill creation,
- cannot substitute for productive organizations.

### Core examples

- Taiwan versus the Philippines
- Korea versus Argentina
- African literacy improvement without growth
- weak cross-country evidence linking education expansion to growth
- Switzerland's high prosperity with low university enrolment

### Why it matters
Stops students from confusing personal educational advancement with national development strategy.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 15:** enterprise and institutions
- **Thing 9:** manufacturing and structural change
- **Thing 20:** capability and opportunity

## Thing 18. What Is Good for General Motors Is Not Necessarily Good for the United States

**Memory hook:** *Firm success, insider success, and social success can diverge.*

### Mainstream claim
What benefits business benefits the nation.

### Chang's reply
Individual firms may choose strategies that help managers and shareholders in the short run but damage:

- workers,
- suppliers,
- industrial capacity,
- long-run competitiveness,
- taxpayers.

Regulation can therefore be pro-business in a deeper sense.

### Core examples

- GM's shift toward lobbying, finance, and acquisitions rather than making better cars
- GM rescue by US taxpayers
- South Korea growing despite heavy permit requirements
- child-labour, banking, and training regulations as productivity-supporting rules

### Why it matters
Important bridge between corporate governance and national development.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 2:** corporate governance
- **Thing 14:** managerial incentives
- **Thing 21:** adjustment and worker protection

## Thing 19. Despite the Fall of Communism, We Are Still Living in Planned Economies

**Memory hook:** *Capitalism is part market, part organization, part state plan.*

### Mainstream claim
The fall of communism proved that planning does not work.

### Chang's reply
Soviet-style total planning failed, but planning remains everywhere in capitalism:

- inside large firms,
- through state-owned enterprises,
- through industrial policy,
- through infrastructure and R&D planning,
- through indicative national coordination.

### Core examples

- Soviet success in simple industrial coordination, failure in complexity
- French, Japanese, Korean, Taiwanese indicative planning
- US government R&D support
- Herbert Simon's Martian seeing an organizational economy

### Why it matters
Destroys the false opposition between markets and planning.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 12:** industrial policy
- **Thing 2:** firms as hierarchies
- **Thing 23:** economics and intellectual blindness

## Thing 20. Equality of Opportunity May Not Be Fair

**Memory hook:** *Formal opportunity without capability is fake fairness.*

### Mainstream claim
Equality of opportunity is fair; equality of outcome is not.

### Chang's reply
Formal opportunity is not enough when children and adults lack the basic means to use it. Some equalization of outcomes is needed to create:

- nutrition,
- health,
- educational support,
- security,
- genuine mobility.

### Core examples

- Confucian caste restrictions
- apartheid South Africa and the "cappuccino society"
- poor children unable to benefit from school access
- Alejandro Toledo as an exception that proves the rule
- stronger mobility in stronger welfare states

### Why it matters
Connects fairness with capability and social structure, not just legal non-discrimination.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 3:** market rewards are not pure merit
- **Thing 17:** education and capability
- **Thing 21:** welfare and second chances

## Thing 21. Big Government Makes People More Open to Change

**Memory hook:** *Security enables flexibility.*

### Mainstream claim
Big welfare states make people lazy and economies rigid.

### Chang's reply
Without security, people behave conservatively:

- they avoid risky careers,
- resist restructuring,
- demand protectionism,
- cling to declining sectors.

Welfare can make labour markets *more* dynamic by giving workers second chances.

### Core examples

- South Korean students crowding into medicine as insecurity rises
- US workers resisting change more than Europeans because job loss is catastrophic
- welfare state as bankruptcy law for workers
- Scandinavian growth performance

### Why it matters
Shows that efficiency and security are not opposites.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 20:** equal opportunity needs social support
- **Thing 13:** redistribution and growth
- **Thing 18:** restructuring costs

## Thing 22. Financial Markets Need to Become Less, Not More, Efficient

**Memory hook:** *Fast finance can derail slow production.*

### Mainstream claim
Liberalized finance allocates capital efficiently and supports growth.

### Chang's reply
Finance has become too efficient at generating short-term profit. This creates:

- instability,
- short-termism,
- weak patient capital,
- speculative bubbles,
- fragile corporate behaviour.

Finance must be slowed and simplified.

### Core examples

- Iceland's financial boom and collapse
- Ireland, Latvia, Dubai
- financialization of GM, Ford, GE
- MBSs, CDOs, CDSs
- Tobin tax and other friction-creating measures

### Why it matters
Central to Chang's explanation of post-1980 instability and weak long-term growth.

### Chapter links
- **Thing 6:** instability
- **Thing 2:** shareholder impatience
- **Thing 16:** bounded rationality

## Thing 23. Good Economic Policy Does Not Require Good Economists

**Memory hook:** *Good policy needs realism more than orthodoxy.*

### Mainstream claim
Good policy requires expert economists, and simpler free-market policies are safest for states with limited capacity.

### Chang's reply
Many successful developmental states were not run by orthodox economists. Meanwhile, free-market economists often helped justify:

- deregulation,
- weaker growth,
- greater inequality,
- financial instability.

Chang does not reject economics altogether; he calls for plural economics.

### Core examples

- lawyers and engineers in East Asian economic bureaucracies
- weak record of orthodox economists in Latin America
- Queen Elizabeth II's question after the 2008 crisis
- alternative economists: Keynes, Minsky, List, Schumpeter, Hirschman, Simon, Stiglitz, Sen

### Why it matters
This is the book's final meta-critique: bad theory has bad policy consequences.

### Chapter links
- synthesizes **Things 1, 7, 12, 16, 19, 22**

## Major Themes to Revise Across Chapters

### 1. The state
The state is not just a regulator from outside. It creates markets, guides development, funds research, protects workers, and shapes industrial structure.

### 2. Firms
Firms are collective institutions with conflicting stakeholders. Governance matters as much as markets.

### 3. Labour
Labour markets are structured by law, migration control, welfare, and bargaining power. Wages are not pure merit signals.

### 4. Development
Development means building productive capabilities, not just liberalizing trade.

### 5. Education
Education helps people flourish but does not automatically create national wealth.

### 6. Finance
Finance should support real production. Left unconstrained, it becomes destabilizing and extractive.

### 7. Inequality
Inequality is not just a moral issue; it affects growth, mobility, and social cohesion.

### 8. Free-market ideology
It is historically selective, politically loaded, and theoretically overconfident.

## Final One-Paragraph Revision Synthesis

Chang's book argues that capitalism should be understood not as a natural free-market order but as a politically organized system shaped by laws, institutions, firms, finance, and states. Rich countries did not become rich through pure laissez-faire, workers are not paid simply according to merit, shareholders are not natural guardians of firms, finance is not automatically productive, and welfare states need not be enemies of growth. Development depends on building productive capabilities through industry, organization, patient capital, and strategic public action. The central revision lesson is that **good capitalism is governed capitalism**.
