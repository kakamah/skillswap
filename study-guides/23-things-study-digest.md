# Ha-Joon Chang, *23 Things They Don't Tell You About Capitalism*  
## Detailed Study Digest for Fast but Deep Understanding

### Purpose of this digest

This guide is a full study digest, not a quotation bank. It is written to help a student grasp the whole argument of the book quickly and critically. The wording is deliberately paraphrased and synthesized rather than closely following the original text. The goal is to preserve the structure and force of Chang's argument while making it easier to revise, compare, and use in essays.

### How to use this document

You can read it in three ways:

1. **Sequentially**, to see how Chang builds an alternative picture of capitalism from Thing 1 to Thing 23.
2. **Thematically**, by tracking repeated issues such as the state, inequality, labour, finance, industrial policy, and development.
3. **Exam-first**, by using the central thesis, mainstream view, counterargument, examples, weaknesses, chapter links, and memory hook in each section.

### The book's overall intellectual method

Chang does not argue that capitalism should be abolished. He argues that the free-market version of capitalism is historically false, intellectually narrow, and politically dangerous. His method is consistent across the book:

- take a familiar market slogan,
- show the assumptions hidden inside it,
- compare that slogan with actual economic history,
- demonstrate that institutions, politics, and collective organization shape economic outcomes,
- then argue that a better-regulated, more development-oriented capitalism is both possible and preferable.

The chapters are short and polemical in the book, but they are tightly connected. The early chapters attack the myths of market neutrality, merit pay, and shareholder primacy. The middle chapters turn to development, industry, firms, and social organization. The later chapters examine equality, welfare, finance, planning, and economics as a discipline. Read together, they form a systematic criticism of market fundamentalism.

## Thing 1. There Is No Such Thing as a Free Market

**Memory hook:** *Markets always wear rules, even when the rules become invisible.*

### Central thesis

Chang's opening claim is foundational: there is no naturally existing, politically neutral "free market." Every market is structured by law, custom, moral judgment, and state enforcement. What people call a free market is usually just a market whose underlying rules they have become used to.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The standard claim is that markets work best when the state interferes as little as possible. In this view, regulation distorts incentives, prevents resources from flowing to their most efficient uses, and blocks mutually beneficial exchange. Freedom of contract is treated as the baseline, and intervention as an exception.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang argues that this baseline is imaginary. Markets are not natural spaces of unregulated exchange later invaded by politics. They are created and stabilized through political decisions from the beginning. Rules about what may be sold, who may work, what contracts count as legitimate, what counts as acceptable working conditions, and how prices are indirectly shaped are all political choices. The fact that many such rules now look normal does not make them non-political.

His deeper point is methodological. If the boundary between market and non-market activity is historically variable and morally disputed, then economics cannot honestly present itself as a purely technical science that simply discovers the correct amount of market freedom. Free-market economics often hides a political preference inside a language of neutrality.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **Child labour in nineteenth-century Britain:** even mild restrictions on child factory work were denounced as attacks on labour freedom. What is now morally obvious was once described as anti-market interference.
- **Environmental regulation:** pollution controls were also attacked as intrusions on market freedom, but later became normal and broadly accepted.
- **Restrictions on what may be traded:** votes, judges' decisions, many drugs, organs, university places, firearms, and numerous other goods or positions are not left to open buying and selling.
- **Restrictions on who may enter markets:** children, unlicensed doctors, unregulated financial actors, and others are excluded by law.
- **Consumer protection and return policies:** even the right to return unwanted products reflects a moral choice in favour of buyers over sellers.
- **Wages and interest rates:** migration controls and central-bank decisions shape labour prices and credit prices in ways that free-market rhetoric often ignores.
- **The 2008 crisis:** when the US government nationalized or rescued major financial institutions, defenders of free enterprise suddenly accepted massive intervention as "necessary," proving how flexible the supposed market boundary really is.

### Why this chapter matters

Thing 1 sets the terms for the whole book. If markets are politically constituted, then all later debates about trade, wages, firms, welfare, and finance must be re-read. Chang wants the reader to stop asking whether government should interfere with a naturally free market and start asking what kinds of rules should govern economic life, whose interests those rules serve, and how they could be changed.

This matters especially for development and inequality. Once the free market is exposed as a constructed arrangement, it becomes much harder to claim that existing distributions of income or power are simply the fair outcome of impersonal market forces.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- A free-market economist could reply that no serious economist believes in a literally ruleless market; the real question is the *degree* and *type* of intervention, not whether rules exist at all.
- Some may argue that Chang's point, while valid, can become too broad: if every institutional setting counts as political, the distinction between better and worse market design may become blurred.
- Others may say that even if all markets are rule-bound, some institutional frameworks still allow more decentralized coordination and innovation than others; therefore the practical defense of liberalization survives.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It prepares **Thing 3**, where wages are shown to depend heavily on immigration control rather than pure productivity.
- It supports **Thing 7**, which challenges the claim that poor countries became rich through free trade.
- It underpins **Thing 16**, where Chang argues that markets cannot simply be left alone because human rationality is limited.
- It culminates in **Thing 23**, where economics itself is criticized for disguising politics as technical truth.

### Exam angle

Use this chapter whenever you need to challenge the idea that current market outcomes are natural, neutral, or scientifically determined. It is the conceptual doorway into the whole book.

### Transition

Once Chang has dismantled the myth of a naturally free market, he moves to the central institution that supposedly operates inside that market: the modern corporation.

## Thing 2. Companies Should Not Be Run in the Interest of Their Owners

**Memory hook:** *The legal owner is often the least patient stakeholder.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that corporations should not be governed primarily for shareholder value. Shareholders are legally important, but because they are often the most mobile and least committed participants, they are poor guardians of a firm's long-term health.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The standard view is that shareholders own the firm and bear the residual risk. Workers receive wages, lenders receive interest, suppliers receive contracted payments, but shareholders get what is left over after all others are paid. Therefore, if firms are run to maximize shareholder returns, corporate performance and social efficiency are both supposed to improve.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang's response has two layers. First, historically, the modern corporation exists only because limited liability reduced investor risk and separated ownership from management. This means shareholders are not heroic risk-bearers in the old personal sense; modern capitalism deliberately insulated them. Second, because many shareholders can easily sell their shares, they have a strong bias toward short-term profit, high dividends, and rising stock prices rather than patient investment.

The result is what Chang calls an alliance between professional managers and mobile shareholders. Managers are rewarded through stock-linked pay and therefore adopt cost-cutting, downsizing, underinvestment, and buybacks to please shareholders and themselves. This may lift profits temporarily while weakening the technological and organizational base of the firm.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **Limited liability:** Adam Smith distrusted it, while Marx understood that it made modern large-scale capitalism possible. Chang uses this irony to show that corporate form is historically contingent.
- **Managerial capitalism:** as firms grew, professional managers replaced founder-owners, creating a new governance problem.
- **Shareholder value revolution since the 1980s:** stock options, dividend pressure, and share buybacks encouraged short-termism.
- **GM:** instead of sustaining its industrial strength through long-term investment, GM increasingly prioritized financial strategies, buybacks, and managerial rewards, contributing to its decline.
- **Different national models:** Germany, Sweden, France, and Japan limited the power of floating shareholders through state ownership, worker representation, cross-shareholding, or differential voting rights.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter is crucial because it shifts the unit of analysis from market exchange to the internal organization of firms. Free-market ideology often speaks as if firms were simply disciplined by markets. Chang shows that firms are political organizations in their own right, with conflicting stakeholders and governance choices that affect productivity, employment, and innovation.

It also helps explain later chapters on executive pay, finance, and industrial decline. If firms are forced to serve impatient shareholders, then investment falls, workers are squeezed, and national industrial capacity weakens.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Defenders of shareholder primacy may argue that diffuse ownership creates accountability and disciplines wasteful managers.
- Some critics might say Chang romanticizes stakeholder models while understating their own risks: insider protection, weak accountability, managerial complacency, or political capture.
- It is also fair to ask whether cross-shareholding and state influence can reduce short-termism without also reducing flexibility and transparency.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It links directly to **Thing 14**, where excessive executive pay is presented as a result of managerial power and weak accountability.
- It supports **Thing 18**, where what suits a firm's dominant insiders may be harmful to the national economy.
- It connects to **Thing 22**, because financial markets intensify pressure for short-term returns.
- It also foreshadows **Thing 21**, where social protection is shown to support productive risk-taking better than market fear alone.

### Exam angle

Use this chapter for essays on corporate governance, stakeholder capitalism, financialization, or the contrast between Anglo-American and coordinated capitalist models.

### Transition

If shareholder capitalism misdescribes the modern firm, the next common myth Chang tackles is equally basic: the idea that labour markets reward people according to their true productivity.

## Thing 3. Most People in Rich Countries Are Paid More Than They Should Be

**Memory hook:** *Wages follow borders as much as productivity.*

### Central thesis

Chang's argument is not that workers in rich countries are lazy or useless. Rather, he argues that large international wage gaps cannot be explained mainly by differences in individual productivity. They are sustained above all by immigration controls and by the collective productivity of national systems.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

Standard theory says labour is paid according to marginal productivity. If a Swedish bus driver earns many times more than an Indian one, that is assumed to reflect higher skill, better education, or greater economic contribution. Market wages are then presented as both efficient and morally justified.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang replies that this explanation collapses under scrutiny. Many ordinary tasks are not performed dozens of times better by rich-country workers. In fact, workers in poorer countries may often show greater practical skill under harsher conditions. The real reason rich-country wages stay high is that labour mobility is heavily restricted. Rich countries protect their labour markets while pretending to defend free competition.

He then makes a broader claim: even when individuals in rich countries are highly productive, their productivity depends on institutions, infrastructure, technologies, and organizational capabilities accumulated collectively over long periods. So even high earners are not simply being paid for isolated personal merit.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **Swedish and Indian bus drivers:** Chang uses the comparison to show how implausible it is to attribute huge wage differences to individual driving ability.
- **Human capital critique:** extra schooling does not explain wage gaps when the job itself requires little of that educational content.
- **Immigration control as hidden protectionism:** if labour markets were really open, vast numbers of rich-country workers could be replaced by lower-paid workers from poorer countries.
- **Warren Buffett's remark:** he would not earn what he does if placed in a much poorer social setting, because productivity is system-dependent.
- **Rich and poor within poor countries:** the poor are not usually the main drag on national productivity; elites in poor countries often fail to build high-productivity systems.

### Why this chapter matters

Thing 3 attacks a powerful moral belief: that market income accurately reflects personal deservingness. Once that belief weakens, it becomes easier to question both the legitimacy of extreme inequality and the contempt often directed at poorer countries. The chapter also helps students distinguish individual effort from system-level productivity.

It matters for policy because it suggests that development is not mainly about telling poor people to work harder. It is about changing national productive structures and institutions. It also matters for debates on migration, welfare, and meritocracy.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- A critic may answer that Chang underestimates how much productivity differences can arise from capital intensity, work organization, and complementary skills, all of which are relevant even if not strictly "individual."
- Some might say that marginal productivity theory concerns the value of labour in a whole production setting, not pure physical effort, so the theory is less naive than Chang suggests.
- Others may argue that immigration limits are necessary for political and social reasons even if they are economically protectionist; Chang himself partly accepts this.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It extends **Thing 1** by showing that labour markets are politically bounded.
- It connects to **Thing 10**, where American living standards are shown to depend in part on cheap service labour.
- It supports **Thing 20**, because equal opportunity is hollow if starting conditions and systems differ radically.
- It also reinforces **Thing 17**, which questions the simple equation between education and national prosperity.

### Exam angle

This chapter is especially useful in essays on meritocracy, global inequality, migration, labour markets, and the social foundations of productivity.

### Transition

Having challenged the way we think about wages, Chang then widens the lens and asks how we think about progress itself, especially technological progress.

## Thing 4. The Washing Machine Has Changed the World More Than the Internet Has

**Memory hook:** *The biggest technologies are often the least glamorous.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that societies systematically overrate flashy new technologies and underrate older technologies whose effects have become normalized. The washing machine, along with household electrification and related domestic technologies, changed social and economic life more deeply than the internet has so far.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The dominant story presents the internet and information technology as the decisive transformation of our era. Because communications costs have collapsed, distance is said to have died, borders are thought to matter less, and flexibility plus deregulation become the natural policy response.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang does not deny that the internet matters. His point is comparative and historical. First, earlier communications revolutions, especially the telegraph, were far more dramatic in terms of the reduction in transmission time. Second, in terms of social impact, labour-saving domestic technologies transformed gender roles, family life, fertility patterns, and labour-force participation more deeply than the internet has.

The broader lesson is that technological change must be understood in context. Economic importance cannot be measured simply by novelty or visibility. When societies fixate on fashionable technologies, they risk bad policy priorities.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **Domestic servants in rich and poor countries:** Chang shows how common domestic service used to be in rich countries and remains common in poorer ones because labour is relatively cheap there.
- **The washing machine and household appliances:** they massively reduced the time required for laundry, cooking, cleaning, and heating-related work.
- **Women's labour-force participation:** household technologies helped make paid work outside the home much more feasible for women.
- **The telegraph versus the internet:** the telegraph reduced transatlantic communication times by a far larger factor than the internet reduced fax times.
- **The "digital divide":** Chang questions whether poor countries necessarily need computers before they need roads, water systems, electricity, and basic household infrastructure.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter matters because it challenges technological determinism. Chang wants readers to stop assuming that new technology automatically dictates one economic model or one policy package. Politics, institutions, and social priorities still determine how technologies are used and what effects they have.

It also broadens the study of economic change beyond GDP and industrial output. Domestic labour, gender relations, and the social organization of care are shown to be central to economic history. That is one of the book's most valuable hidden lessons.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- One might argue that Chang underestimates the long-run impact of digital networks, software, platform coordination, and AI-related productivity effects that may unfold over decades.
- Others may say he is comparing mature technologies with an immature one, giving an unfair advantage to the older technologies.
- Even so, the force of his argument remains: the newest technology is not automatically the most transformative in the dimensions that matter most.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It leads directly to **Thing 9**, where post-industrial fantasies are criticized.
- It connects to **Thing 17**, because it undermines crude "knowledge economy" arguments about education.
- It also supports **Thing 8**, since globalization depends as much on policy as on technology.

### Exam angle

Use this chapter to argue against technological determinism and in favour of historically grounded analysis of productivity, gender change, and development priorities.

### Transition

From technology, Chang turns to human motivation: if economies are not driven simply by machines and prices, what kind of human beings do market theories assume?

## Thing 5. Assume the Worst About People and You Get the Worst

**Memory hook:** *An economy built on distrust breeds the behaviour it fears.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that free-market economics exaggerates selfishness as the dominant human motive. People are not saints, but they are not pure calculators either. Institutions that assume universal self-seeking may weaken trust, cooperation, and commitment, thereby reducing efficiency rather than increasing it.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The mainstream claim begins from Adam Smith's famous appeal to self-interest. Markets are praised for turning private greed into social benefit. Because people naturally try to cheat, shirk, or maximize private advantage, good systems must rely on competition and monetary incentives rather than moral commitment.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang accepts that self-interest matters, but denies that it is sufficient as a theory of behaviour. Real production and exchange depend on trust, pride, reciprocity, professionalism, solidarity, and internalized moral norms. Without these, the costs of monitoring and enforcement would be overwhelming. Large organizations could barely function.

He therefore rejects the idea that we should design institutions as if everyone were a narrow opportunist. If workers, officials, and managers are treated as untrustworthy by default, they may become less cooperative and more defensive. A productive system should instead recognize mixed motives and cultivate the better ones.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **Kobe Steel manager:** he explains that large organizations cannot function if every proposal is treated as pure self-seeking manipulation.
- **Work-to-rule actions:** production collapses when workers do only what the rulebook literally requires, showing how much output depends on goodwill and initiative beyond contract.
- **Japanese production methods:** these use worker discretion, responsibility, and trust to raise quality and efficiency.
- **Taxi payment example:** people often behave honestly even when escape without punishment seems possible.
- **Invisible sanction critique:** moral behaviour cannot be fully reduced to hidden incentives, because those sanction systems themselves depend on people willing to enforce norms.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter matters because it challenges one of the deepest anthropological assumptions of market ideology. It also offers a bridge between economics and social theory. If motivation is plural rather than single, then the design of firms, public administration, and welfare systems must change.

It also weakens a common free-market move: criticizing government on the ground that public officials are selfish while assuming private actors are adequately disciplined by markets. Chang's point is that all large organizations depend on non-market motives.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Critics may say Chang risks understating the need for incentive structures, particularly in contexts where trust is low and corruption is common.
- Some economists would accept mixed motives but still argue that institutions should be robust to opportunism, since one cannot rely on virtue alone.
- The strongest counterpoint is probably that trust and discipline are complements, not substitutes; Chang sometimes pushes too hard toward the former.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It strengthens **Thing 2**, where firms are shown to be damaged by narrow shareholder incentives.
- It helps explain **Thing 12**, where state capacity and public-private cooperation matter.
- It prepares **Thing 16**, where bounded rationality further undermines the textbook model of the rational actor.

### Exam angle

This is a strong chapter for essays on institutional design, organizational behaviour, corruption, corporate culture, and critiques of homo economicus.

### Transition

If humans are not as rationally self-governing as textbook theory suggests, then macroeconomic management also looks different, especially when economists obsess over one variable at the expense of broader stability.

## Thing 6. Greater Macroeconomic Stability Has Not Made the World Economy More Stable

**Memory hook:** *Low inflation is not the same as a secure economy.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that the anti-inflation consensus since the 1980s mistook one form of stability for stability as such. While inflation fell, economies became more crisis-prone, more financially fragile, and more insecure for ordinary workers.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The mainstream narrative says that high inflation used to poison economies by discouraging saving, investment, and long-term planning. Independent central banks, fiscal restraint, and anti-inflation credibility are therefore treated as the foundation of stable growth.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang replies that moderate inflation is far less harmful than commonly claimed, while the policies used to crush it often damage growth. High real interest rates discourage productive investment, push capital toward speculative finance, and weaken employment. At the same time, liberalization of capital markets and labour markets has generated banking crises and job insecurity.

So even if price levels became more predictable, everyday economic life for many people became more precarious. To lose a job, a home, or savings in a crisis is far more destabilizing than living with 4 per cent inflation instead of 2 per cent.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **German hyperinflation:** Chang accepts that hyperinflation is destructive, but insists that policymakers wrongly generalize from extreme cases.
- **Brazil and South Korea:** both experienced substantial inflation during periods of rapid growth, showing that moderate inflation is not fatal.
- **Financial crisis data:** the era of low inflation coincided with many more banking crises than the earlier postwar era.
- **Job insecurity:** anti-inflationary policy and labour-market flexibility increased insecurity in both rich and poor countries.
- **Financial asset holders:** Chang argues that anti-inflation ideology reflects the interests of creditors and financiers, whose nominal assets are hurt by inflation.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter is central to Chang's criticism of neoliberalism. It shows how a seemingly technical policy goal can embody class priorities and distort macroeconomic thinking. It also teaches an important analytical lesson: macroeconomic stability has multiple dimensions, and what financial elites mean by stability is not necessarily what households experience as stability.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- One could argue that Chang underestimates the damage that inflation expectations can do to credibility and to poorer households with weak indexation.
- Some may object that the post-1980 slowdown had many causes beyond anti-inflation policy alone.
- Yet his broader point remains powerful: low inflation does not prove that an economy is healthy, stable, or socially well organized.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It anticipates **Thing 22** on financial excess and systemic crisis.
- It aligns with **Thing 21**, which argues that social security matters more to lived stability than price orthodoxy.
- It supports **Thing 13**, where pro-rich reforms are shown not to have delivered faster growth.

### Exam angle

Use this chapter in essays on central banking, neoliberal macroeconomics, crisis theory, and the difference between nominal and lived stability.

### Transition

Having challenged macroeconomic orthodoxy, Chang turns to the development question directly: did poor countries become rich by following free-market policies?

## Thing 7. Free-Market Policies Rarely Make Poor Countries Rich

**Memory hook:** *The ladder rich countries climbed is the ladder they later kicked away.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that free trade, deregulation, and minimal government intervention have almost never been the route by which poor countries became rich. Historically, rich countries used protection, subsidies, public investment, and active state guidance during their own development.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The orthodox story says that many developing countries failed because they pursued protectionism, state ownership, import substitution, and industrial planning after decolonization. Growth improved only when they liberalized. Rich countries, according to this account, generally became rich through openness, property rights, and market discipline.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang flips the story. First, he argues that the growth performance of many developing countries was actually better in the period of stronger state intervention than in the neoliberal period after the 1980s. Second, he insists that historical evidence from Britain, the United States, and many others shows that rich countries relied heavily on protectionist and interventionist policies while catching up.

For Chang, development is not about exposing weak firms to full competition immediately. It is about building productive capabilities under shelter and discipline. The free-market recipe is often imposed by countries that did not themselves develop in that way.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **Country A and Country B comparison:** contemporary China and the late nineteenth-century US both look highly "anti-market" by neoliberal standards, yet both were spectacular growth cases.
- **Alexander Hamilton and the infant industry argument:** the US deliberately protected manufacturing early on.
- **US presidents on currency and tariffs:** Washington, Lincoln, Grant, Jackson, and others are used to show how unfree-market the US developmental tradition actually was.
- **Britain:** it became a free-trade preacher only after establishing industrial superiority.
- **Finland, France, Singapore, Taiwan, Japan, Korea:** each used combinations of protection, state ownership, foreign investment controls, or strategic policy.
- **Post-1980 developing world growth slowdown:** Latin America and Sub-Saharan Africa perform especially poorly under neoliberal reform.

### Why this chapter matters

This is one of the book's most important chapters. It provides the historical backbone of Chang's attack on development orthodoxy. For students of political economy, the chapter demonstrates how policy advice is often shaped by power rather than by fair reading of history.

It also reinforces an institutional point: productive capacity is not spontaneously generated by exposure to competition. It often has to be built through long periods of state support, discipline, and strategic sequencing.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Critics may say Chang sometimes groups together very different forms of intervention, some successful and some disastrous.
- Others may argue that earlier protection succeeded only under specific geopolitical and domestic conditions that cannot easily be reproduced.
- It can also be argued that some liberalization was necessary in many overprotected economies even if the full neoliberal package was damaging.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It works with **Thing 12**, which argues that governments can pick winners.
- It connects to **Thing 11**, where Africa's stagnation is linked to structural adjustment rather than fixed destiny.
- It supports **Thing 19**, because development often requires planning and coordination rather than pure market signals.

### Exam angle

Ideal for essays on infant industry protection, development strategy, historical political economy, and the critique of "one-size-fits-all" policy prescriptions.

### Transition

Once development is seen as historically state-shaped, the next issue is foreign capital: if global capital is supposedly nationless, should countries care who owns their firms?

## Thing 8. Capital Has a Nationality

**Memory hook:** *Multinational firms travel widely but keep their brains at home.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that transnational corporations are rarely truly nationless. They may operate globally, but high-value functions such as strategic control, advanced research, and final loyalty remain concentrated in the home country. Therefore the nationality of capital still matters.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The standard view holds that in a globalized era capital goes wherever returns are highest. Since multinational firms are not emotionally attached to any one nation, governments should not discriminate between domestic and foreign capital. Any investment is good if it creates jobs and output.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang accepts that multinational firms are more international than before, but argues that this does not eliminate national bias. Control over top management, advanced research, supplier networks, institutional memory, and political obligations remains strongly home-centred. As a result, the home country captures a disproportionate share of the long-term benefits.

This means policy should not naïvely assume that foreign direct investment automatically generates the same developmental benefits as strong domestic ownership. Brownfield acquisitions may simply transfer control of existing firms rather than create new capacity. Even greenfield investment may lock host countries into lower-value activities.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **Carlos Ghosn:** often treated as a symbol of cosmopolitan management, but Chang uses him as an exceptional case proving the rule.
- **Chrysler:** its control shifted from Americans to Germans and then increasingly to Italians, showing that nationality matters when ownership changes.
- **Home bias in R&D:** advanced research tends to stay in headquarters economies.
- **Brownfield versus greenfield investment:** much FDI consists of acquiring existing firms, not building new productive capacity.
- **Private equity versus industrial investors:** short-term finance may be worse for national development than long-term industrial ownership, whether foreign or domestic.

### Why this chapter matters

The chapter is important because it complicates the usual binary between nationalism and globalization. Chang is not arguing for xenophobia or autarky. He is arguing for strategic realism. If firms keep core capabilities and decision-making power in their home systems, then developing countries must think carefully about ownership, upgrading, and technological learning.

It also broadens the book's notion of power. Markets do not merely allocate resources; they structure where knowledge, control, and future growth possibilities are located.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Some may argue that Chang underestimates the spillovers from foreign ownership, especially in small economies with limited domestic capital.
- Others could respond that domestic capital can be just as predatory as foreign capital and that the quality of regulation matters more than nationality.
- Chang partly concedes this, but still insists nationality remains an important variable. That balanced position is one of the strengths of the chapter.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It follows naturally from **Thing 7**, since policy space includes control over foreign investment.
- It links to **Thing 18**, where corporate and national interests diverge.
- It feeds into **Thing 9**, because de-industrialization is tied to where high-value activities remain located.

### Exam angle

Use this chapter in essays on globalization, foreign direct investment, national champions, industrial upgrading, or strategic sectors.

### Transition

If capital still has a home, then the location of productive activity still matters. Chang therefore turns to one of the biggest myths of recent decades: the claim that manufacturing no longer matters.

## Thing 9. We Do Not Live in a Post-Industrial Age

**Memory hook:** *Services may dominate employment, but manufacturing still anchors prosperity.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that rich countries may look post-industrial because fewer people work in factories, but this does not mean manufacturing has lost economic centrality. Much of the apparent decline of industry reflects relative price changes, outsourcing, and statistical reclassification rather than a simple disappearance of production. Manufacturing remains crucial for productivity growth, exports, and long-run development.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The familiar claim is that advanced economies have moved beyond industry into a service- and knowledge-led era. In this story, manufacturing is old economy activity. Rich countries should specialize in design, finance, information, branding, and other high-value services, while lower-income countries can handle factories. Some even argue that poor countries can skip industrialization and move directly from agriculture into services.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang's answer is that this narrative confuses social change with economic structure. It is true that a smaller share of workers now make physical goods. But the decline of manufacturing's share in GDP is driven largely by the fact that productivity rises faster in manufacturing than in services, which pushes down the relative prices of goods. People can consume more manufactured products while spending a smaller share of income on them.

He further argues that manufacturing matters for three reasons. First, it remains the sector with the strongest potential for sustained productivity growth. Second, many advanced services depend on industrial capabilities. Third, manufacturing is much easier to export than most services, making it vital for balance-of-payments stability and technological upgrading.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **"Made in China" and Britain's earlier role as the "workshop of the world":** Chang uses Britain and China to show that industrial leadership matters historically and internationally.
- **Computers versus haircuts:** when computer prices fall because productivity rises, spending shifts toward services not necessarily because people want vastly more services, but because services become relatively expensive.
- **Outsourcing and reclassification:** some apparent de-industrialization happens because service tasks once counted inside manufacturing firms are moved outside them.
- **Britain and the US:** both are used as examples of countries that tolerated industrial weakening by trusting in finance and knowledge services.
- **Switzerland and Singapore:** often described as service-based success stories, but in reality both remain highly industrialized in per capita terms.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter is one of the book's strongest attacks on lazy economic storytelling. It matters because post-industrial language has justified policy neglect of manufacturing, especially in the UK and US. Chang wants students to see that "high value" cannot simply be inferred from whether an activity is a service or a good. A strong industrial base supports learning, exports, supplier networks, and technical capabilities that diffuse across the whole economy.

For developing countries, the chapter is even more important. If they accept the fantasy that they can leap straight into service-based prosperity, they may lock themselves into low-productivity, weakly tradable sectors and give up the most reliable route to catch-up growth.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Critics may argue that Chang is too skeptical about future productivity growth in digital services, software, data processing, and platform-based coordination.
- Some may say that advanced manufacturing itself increasingly merges with services, making the boundary less useful than Chang suggests.
- Others may note that some small economies can rely heavily on services, though Chang replies that such cases are narrow exceptions and usually depend on special advantages.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It builds on **Thing 4**, which criticized exaggerated belief in recent technologies.
- It links to **Thing 8**, because foreign ownership affects where high-value industrial functions remain.
- It supports **Thing 17**, where education is shown not to generate prosperity automatically unless linked to productive organizations.
- It feeds into **Thing 18**, where industrial decline and corporate strategy are shown to harm national interest.

### Exam angle

Use this chapter in essays on de-industrialization, the knowledge economy, structural change, and why manufacturing still matters for development.

### Transition

If rich countries are not as post-industrial as they claim, then their alleged superiority also needs a second look. Chang next challenges the prestige of the American model itself.

## Thing 10. The US Does Not Have the Highest Living Standard in the World

**Memory hook:** *High average income is not the same as the best life.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that even if the United States ranks extremely highly in income per head, it does not clearly offer the world's highest living standard. Average purchasing power conceals inequality, poor social outcomes, long working hours, and reliance on cheap labour in low-wage services.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The orthodox claim is that the US sits at or near the top of world living standards, especially once purchasing power is considered. This is often treated as evidence that a lightly regulated, high-inequality, high-flexibility economy rewards talent, innovation, and enterprise better than European social models.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang does not deny that average US consumption power is high. His point is that average income is an imperfect proxy for how people actually live. The US is much more unequal than most rich countries, so the average reflects elite incomes more than broad well-being. In addition, the relatively low cost of many services in the US depends on insecure, poorly paid labour and substantial immigration. Finally, Americans work longer hours than citizens in many comparable societies, so their output per hour and leisure-adjusted living standard look less exceptional.

His deeper claim is normative as well as empirical: a good life cannot be measured only by the quantity of goods and services one can buy. Security, health, leisure, social cohesion, and dignity matter too.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **Market exchange rates versus purchasing power parity:** Chang explains why income rankings change depending on method.
- **Taxi rides and restaurant meals in the US versus Europe:** these show how cheap services in the US raise purchasing power figures.
- **Health and crime indicators:** the US performs poorly on measures like life expectancy, infant mortality, imprisonment, and violence relative to many other rich countries.
- **Working hours:** Americans work notably longer than many Europeans and some others with similar or higher hourly productivity.
- **Italian migrants' disappointment:** the image of America as a paradise is historically powerful but often exaggerated.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter matters because the American model functions symbolically in modern capitalism. If the US is presented as proof that freer markets generate the best life, then weakening that claim weakens a large portion of neoliberal rhetoric. Chang is not just ranking countries; he is challenging the criteria used to judge success.

It also broadens the student's understanding of welfare. Living standards should include distribution, social outcomes, time, and public provision, not just consumption.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Some may say Chang mixes living standards with broader quality-of-life measures and that these should be analytically separated.
- Others might argue that many people rationally choose the US trade-off of more income for less leisure.
- A further objection is that cross-country health and crime outcomes have many causes beyond the economic model alone.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It builds on **Thing 3**, since cheap service labour is tied to migration and labour-market structure.
- It anticipates **Thing 20** and **Thing 21**, which emphasize capability, welfare, and social protection.
- It supports **Thing 13**, because inequality does not simply raise everybody's well-being.

### Exam angle

This chapter is useful in essays comparing capitalist models, questioning GDP per capita as a welfare metric, or assessing the US versus Europe.

### Transition

After revising our view of the richest capitalist country, Chang turns to a region usually treated as the opposite extreme: Africa. His aim is the same in both cases - to show that conventional explanations rest on weak assumptions.

## Thing 11. Africa Is Not Destined for Underdevelopment

**Memory hook:** *Geography matters, but policy decides whether it becomes fate.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that Africa's recent stagnation should not be explained as the inevitable result of geography, climate, ethnic diversity, culture, or colonial inheritance. These factors matter, but they do not doom the continent. The more convincing explanation for the growth collapse from the 1980s is policy failure, especially the imposition of free-market structural adjustment.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The standard story says Africa is held back by structural handicaps: tropical disease, landlocked geography, poor neighbours, resource curses, ethnic fragmentation, weak institutions, and anti-development culture. Since these conditions are deeply embedded, the continent is often seen as requiring outside aid rather than self-sustaining development.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang makes two main moves. First, he points out that Africa did grow at a respectable rate in the 1960s and 1970s. If structural handicaps were decisive, growth should never have happened. Second, he shows that most rich countries themselves developed while facing at least some of the same problems: harsh climates, ethnic divisions, weak institutions, difficult geography, or abundant natural resources.

His conclusion is not that structural conditions are meaningless, but that their effects depend on technology, institutions, and political choices. African stagnation became severe when structural adjustment programs pushed rapid liberalization, crushed fragile industry, cut public investment, and forced countries back into dependence on volatile primary commodities.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **Sub-Saharan African growth before 1980:** Chang stresses that the "tragedy" is historically recent rather than permanent.
- **Structural Adjustment Programs:** imposed by the IMF and World Bank, these are treated as the key turning point.
- **Climate comparisons:** tropical burdens exist, but so do severe cold-climate burdens in rich countries.
- **Landlocked states:** Switzerland and Austria show that geography alone is not destiny.
- **Resource-rich successes:** the US, Canada, and Australia show that natural wealth need not be a curse.
- **Ethnic diversity:** many successful countries are historically divided; nation-building and institutions matter.
- **Culture:** stereotypes about Africans resemble older stereotypes once applied to Germans, Japanese, and others.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter matters intellectually and morally. It resists deterministic accounts that blame Africans for conditions beyond their control while protecting global policy orthodoxy from criticism. It also teaches a major methodological lesson: if a theory attributes outcomes to fixed conditions, but those outcomes change sharply over time, the theory is incomplete.

For students, the chapter is useful because it combines growth analysis, historical comparison, and ideological critique. It shows how explanations of poverty can function as political excuses.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Critics may say Chang underplays internal African political failures, predatory states, and armed conflict.
- Others may argue that structural adjustment often responded to pre-existing macroeconomic crises, not just ideological zeal.
- It is also true that structural and policy explanations need not be mutually exclusive; the interaction may be more complex than Chang's polemical format allows.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It extends **Thing 7** by showing the costs of neoliberal reform in a specific region.
- It links to **Thing 3**, because poverty reflects systemic productivity rather than the failings of poor individuals.
- It supports **Thing 12**, since building productive sectors may require active policy, not passive liberalization.

### Exam angle

Use this chapter for essays on African development, structural versus policy explanations, postcolonial political economy, or critiques of economic determinism.

### Transition

If Africa's failure is not predetermined, then development policy matters. Chang therefore moves to a question at the heart of development debate: can governments successfully guide industrial change?

## Thing 12. Governments Can Pick Winners

**Memory hook:** *States do not need perfect foresight; they need decent judgment and learning mechanisms.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that governments can successfully promote strategic industries and firms. They do not do so infallibly, but neither do private investors. The real issue is not whether winner-picking is possible, but how public institutions can improve the odds.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The orthodox view says governments lack the information, incentives, and competence needed to allocate resources well. Firms closest to the market know their own capabilities best. Bureaucrats, by contrast, tend to chase prestige, political gain, or white-elephant projects. Therefore industrial policy supposedly produces losers rather than winners.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang responds that this comparison is unfairly stacked. Private firms also make bad decisions all the time. Proximity to a problem does not guarantee wisdom. Governments can gather information through state-owned enterprises, reporting requirements, formal consultation bodies, and long-term relationships with industry. In addition, what is rational for an individual firm is not necessarily best for the national economy. States may push firms into industries with stronger spillovers, higher learning potential, or better long-run strategic value.

He emphasizes that successful industrial policy usually involves discipline and reciprocity, not blind subsidy. The state should support firms, but under conditions that encourage performance.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **POSCO in South Korea:** a steel project that looked absurd by conventional theory but became globally successful.
- **LG being steered away from textiles and toward cables/electronics:** a case where the state pushed a firm toward a more strategic path.
- **Hyundai and shipbuilding:** state pressure helped create a globally important industry.
- **Concorde:** used as a reminder that governments do sometimes back failures.
- **US government support for semiconductors, aviation, the internet, biotech, and computing:** state backing exists even in countries that deny practicing industrial policy.
- **Japanese deliberation councils and French elite networks:** ways of improving state-business information exchange.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter is central to Chang's positive alternative. Many earlier chapters dismantle market myths; this one begins to reconstruct a different model. It matters because it presents economic development as a process of strategic capability building rather than passive adaptation to comparative advantage.

It also restores a more realistic understanding of uncertainty. Productive transformation always involves error. The existence of failed projects does not prove that public leadership is impossible any more than failed private ventures prove capitalism impossible.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Industrial policy can become politicized, corrupt, or captured by incumbents.
- Successful cases may depend on state capacity that is hard to reproduce.
- Chang is strongest when arguing that governments *can* pick winners; he is less precise on the institutional conditions that separate success from waste.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It develops the historical argument of **Thing 7**.
- It depends on **Thing 5**, because trust and commitment matter in public-private cooperation.
- It anticipates **Thing 19**, where planning in capitalism is defended.
- It complements **Thing 18**, which argues that business freedom alone does not guarantee good national outcomes.

### Exam angle

This chapter is ideal for essays on industrial policy, development strategy, comparative advantage, and state capacity.

### Transition

Once Chang has defended strategic state action, he returns to distribution. The next question is whether making elites richer is really the path to general prosperity.

## Thing 13. Making Rich People Richer Doesn't Make the Rest of Us Richer

**Memory hook:** *If gains stay at the top and investment does not rise, "trickle-down" is a slogan, not a theory.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that upward redistribution of income has not produced faster growth and does not reliably benefit the rest of society. The rich may receive a larger share of national income, but unless that larger share is translated into productive investment and then broadly shared, the expected benefits do not materialize.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The familiar claim is that first one must create wealth before sharing it. Because the rich invest and create jobs, policies that lower their taxes, weaken labour, and allow larger rewards at the top are said to expand the economic pie. The poor may receive relatively less at first, but eventually everyone becomes richer through growth.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang attacks both steps in this logic. First, he argues that pro-rich reforms since the 1980s did not accelerate growth. Second, even if the rich do become richer, there is no automatic mechanism ensuring that the gains diffuse widely. In practice, much of the extra income stays concentrated at the top, especially in weak-welfare systems.

He also makes a historically interesting point by comparing modern trickle-down reasoning with older debates about whether capital accumulation requires concentrating surplus in the hands of capitalists. The claim is not new; it has simply been repackaged.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **Preobrazhensky and Soviet accumulation:** Chang uses the forced concentration of surplus in the Soviet system as an extreme comparison to modern pro-rich logic.
- **Classical political economy:** Ricardo and others believed workers consumed while capitalists invested, so income needed to be concentrated upward.
- **Postwar Golden Age:** rich capitalist countries grew rapidly during decades of stronger welfare states, higher taxation, and lower inequality.
- **Post-1980 slowdown:** world growth per capita fell relative to the decades before neoliberal reform.
- **US inequality:** the top 1 per cent and especially the top 0.1 per cent captured enormous shares of income growth.
- **Investment weakness:** despite upward redistribution, investment ratios fell in major economies.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter is a direct challenge to one of the core moral justifications for inequality in modern capitalism. Chang is not just saying inequality is unpleasant. He is saying the usual growth defense of inequality is empirically weak. That is a major claim.

It also reorients thinking about redistribution. Redistribution is not necessarily a drag on prosperity; under some conditions it can support demand, social peace, and human development.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Critics could argue that innovation incentives and capital formation are sensitive to taxation in ways Chang understates.
- Some may say the slowdown after the 1970s had many structural causes not reducible to redistribution upward.
- Still, Chang's narrow point remains hard to dismiss: the promised pay-off from pro-rich reform has not been convincingly delivered.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It works with **Thing 14**, where top managerial pay is shown to be disconnected from merit.
- It supports **Thing 21**, where welfare and security are linked to dynamism rather than stagnation.
- It links to **Thing 6**, because pro-rich macro and financial policy often privilege asset owners.

### Exam angle

Use this chapter in essays on inequality, taxation, social democracy, neoliberal reform, and the empirical critique of trickle-down economics.

### Transition

Chang then sharpens the distributional issue by focusing on one especially visible symbol of upward redistribution: the pay of top corporate managers.

## Thing 14. US Managers Are Over-Priced

**Memory hook:** *Top pay reflects power more than pure productivity.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that the extraordinary pay of US executives cannot be justified by market merit. American CEOs are paid far more than their predecessors and far more than comparable managers in other rich countries without corresponding evidence of superior performance.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The orthodox argument says that top managers operate at the summit of huge organizations, so small differences in talent can produce enormous gains. Therefore very high compensation is efficient and necessary to attract and retain the best people.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang disputes both the scale and the supposed market neutrality of executive pay. If American CEOs are suddenly worth ten times more relative to workers than earlier generations of CEOs, we would need evidence of equally dramatic productivity improvement. That evidence is lacking. Comparative evidence also weakens the case: executives in Japan and Europe run similarly large firms for far less money.

He argues that executive compensation reflects class power. Managers influence boards, shape the information directors receive, ally with shareholders through dividend-friendly policies, and benefit from an ideology that treats existing rewards as automatically efficient.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **CEO-to-worker pay ratios:** rising from roughly 30-40:1 in earlier decades to around 300-400:1 in recent decades in the US.
- **International comparisons:** Swiss, German, Dutch, Swedish, and especially Japanese CEOs are paid much less than US peers.
- **Stock options:** these magnify compensation gaps even more than salary figures suggest.
- **Failure without punishment:** severance packages and pensions for failed executives show how weak discipline can be.
- **Financial crisis bailouts:** bankers and executives often retained huge rewards even after destroying shareholder value and requiring public rescue.

### Why this chapter matters

The chapter matters because it challenges the idea that labour markets at the top are uniquely efficient and meritocratic. It also highlights a general theme in Chang's book: when elites become powerful enough, market outcomes start to reflect their institutional leverage rather than neutral competition.

It is also a bridge chapter between corporate governance and inequality. Executive pay is not just an issue of optics. It shapes investment, labour relations, and national income distribution.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Critics may argue that executive labour markets are global and winner-take-most, which can justify high pay even with noisy performance data.
- Others may say Chang underestimates the role of firm size and capital-market pressure in driving compensation.
- Even so, the cross-country evidence remains hard to ignore: if market necessity alone determined pay, the international gaps should be much smaller.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It directly extends **Thing 2** on shareholder capitalism and managerial alliance.
- It supports **Thing 13** on upward redistribution.
- It feeds into **Thing 18**, where management decisions weaken firms and harm national economies.

### Exam angle

This chapter is useful for essays on executive compensation, meritocracy, agency theory, corporate power, and inequality at the top.

### Transition

From elite over-reward, Chang turns to a claim often made about poor countries: that they are poor because they lack entrepreneurial spirit. He argues almost the opposite.

## Thing 15. People in Poor Countries Are More Entrepreneurial Than People in Rich Countries

**Memory hook:** *Poor countries have plenty of hustle; what they lack is productive organization.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that poor countries do not suffer from a shortage of entrepreneurial energy. On the contrary, survival in poor economies often requires constant improvisation, self-employment, and small-scale initiative. The real deficiency lies in productive technology, organizational capacity, and collective institutions.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The usual claim is that development requires entrepreneurs who see opportunities, take risks, and build businesses. Poor countries are therefore often portrayed as stagnant because their people are insufficiently enterprising or because they rely too much on the state rather than individual initiative.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang responds that poor countries are crowded with small entrepreneurs: traders, hawkers, repairers, queue-sellers, transport improvisers, and micro-vendors. The problem is not the absence of entrepreneurial action but the low productivity and limited scalability of the activities available. Modern economic progress depends less on isolated heroic individuals than on collective entrepreneurship embedded in firms, cooperatives, supply chains, finance, and public institutions.

Microfinance is important in his argument because it reveals the limits of romanticizing tiny enterprise. Small loans may help short-run coping, but they rarely transform economic structure.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **Self-employment data:** developing countries often have much higher rates of self-employment than rich countries.
- **Street-level entrepreneurship:** poor-country economies teem with small improvised businesses.
- **Microfinance and Grameen-style lending:** Chang questions the celebratory narrative around tiny credit.
- **Telephone ladies in Bangladesh:** an initially profitable microcredit idea becomes overcrowded and less rewarding over time.
- **Fallacy of composition:** what works for a few individuals cannot necessarily scale to millions.
- **Cooperatives in Europe:** dairy and small-industry success in countries like Denmark, Germany, and the Netherlands depended on collective organization.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter reframes entrepreneurship from an individual moral virtue into a structural capacity. It matters because development discourse often praises grit while ignoring organization. Chang wants students to see that productivity depends on firms, networks, training, standards, technology, and institutional support - not just on the willingness of poor people to start yet another tiny business.

It also enriches the book's broader criticism of individualism. In a modern economy, innovation is usually social and collective before it is personal.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- A defender of microfinance might say Chang is too dismissive of its social benefits, including resilience, female empowerment, and local autonomy.
- Some may argue that self-employment is a weak proxy for entrepreneurship in the Schumpeterian sense of innovation and transformation.
- Chang would likely accept that distinction, but he uses it precisely to show how misleading popular rhetoric can be.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It links strongly to **Thing 17**, because education alone does not create productive organizations.
- It builds on **Thing 5**, where motivation is shown to be richer than the market model assumes.
- It supports **Thing 12**, which stresses strategic industrial and organizational development.

### Exam angle

Use this chapter in essays on entrepreneurship, microfinance, informal economies, collective action, and why development requires more than small business creation.

### Transition

If individual entrepreneurship is not enough, the next question is whether markets can still be trusted to coordinate everything rationally. Chang now deepens his critique by attacking the assumption of full rationality itself.

## Thing 16. We Are Not Smart Enough to Leave Things to the Market

**Memory hook:** *Regulation is needed not because the state is omniscient, but because humans are limited.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that market systems cannot be safely left alone because the standard assumption of rational, fully informed actors is false. Human beings have bounded rationality. In a complex world, they rely on routines, heuristics, and restrictive rules. Regulation is therefore often useful precisely because it simplifies choices and limits systemic danger.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The standard claim is that individuals and firms know their own interests best. Even if some mistakes occur, markets punish error and reward sound decisions. Government intervention is then suspect because officials know less than the actors they regulate.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang's key move is subtle. He does not merely say markets can fail. He says the actors inside markets are not fully rational to begin with. That changes the whole debate. Regulation can help not because bureaucrats possess total knowledge, but because complexity itself overwhelms private decision-makers. Limiting available choices may improve outcomes.

He relies especially on Herbert Simon's concept of bounded rationality. Individuals and organizations need routines to cope with uncertainty. In finance, where complexity exploded before 2008, neither market participants nor regulators understood the full implications of innovation. This is an argument for simpler, more heavily constrained systems.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **Long-Term Capital Management:** even Nobel-linked financial models failed spectacularly.
- **The Madoff scandal:** elite investors and institutions proved unable to detect fraud.
- **AIG and credit default swaps:** decision-makers dramatically underestimated risk.
- **Herbert Simon's chess example:** no human can calculate every possible move, so rules of thumb are necessary.
- **Donald Rumsfeld's "known unknowns" and "unknown unknowns":** Chang uses the phrase to explain uncertainty.
- **Drug approval analogy:** society already accepts ex ante testing and prohibition for dangerous products; finance should not be exempt.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter gives Chang's critique of markets a deeper philosophical basis. It is not only that incentives can misfire or institutions can be badly designed. It is that the mental model behind laissez-faire is wrong. Once rationality is bounded, deregulation loses its automatic appeal.

The chapter is especially important for understanding his stance on finance. Complex financial markets cannot be justified by the claim that sophisticated actors can protect themselves.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Critics may reply that bounded rationality applies to regulators too, so intervention may simply shift error rather than reduce it.
- Others may say market experimentation allows learning, and overregulation can freeze useful innovation.
- Chang partially anticipates this by arguing for restrictions especially where products are opaque and systemic.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It extends **Thing 5** by showing that people are not textbook agents.
- It supports **Thing 22**, where financial markets are said to be too efficient and too fast.
- It circles back to **Thing 1**, because if markets are politically designed anyway, design should reflect real human limits.

### Exam angle

Use this chapter in essays on behavioural economics, regulation, financial innovation, and critiques of rational-choice models.

### Transition

Having challenged the assumptions of rationality, Chang next questions another modern faith: the idea that more schooling by itself guarantees national prosperity.

## Thing 17. More Education in Itself Is Not Going to Make a Country Richer

**Memory hook:** *Schooling matters for life, but not automatically for national productivity.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that more education does not straightforwardly produce greater national wealth. Education is valuable, but much of its value is cultural, civic, and personal rather than directly productive. Economic success depends more on how societies organize production than on how many years of schooling individuals accumulate.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The standard claim is that human capital is the key to growth. East Asia is often cited as proof: disciplined students, strong educational achievement, and economic miracle are presented as parts of the same causal story. In the so-called knowledge economy, this argument becomes even stronger. If ideas are now the main source of wealth, then education supposedly becomes the master variable.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang offers a layered rebuttal. First, some countries with impressive educational achievement have performed poorly economically, while others with weaker starting educational indicators have grown rapidly. Second, much education has little direct effect on most jobs. Third, even higher education often performs a sorting function rather than a productivity-raising one: it tells employers who is disciplined or socially advantaged, not necessarily who possesses useful knowledge.

He also rejects the novelty of the "knowledge economy." Knowledge has always mattered. What changes prosperity is not simply the stock of formally educated individuals, but whether a country has institutions and firms capable of using knowledge productively.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **Taiwan versus the Philippines; Korea versus Argentina:** better-educated countries did not necessarily grow faster.
- **Rising African literacy with poor growth performance:** more schooling did not automatically generate development.
- **Cross-country research by Lant Pritchett:** finds weak evidence of a simple positive relationship between education expansion and growth.
- **Maths and science testing:** high educational scores do not line up neatly with national prosperity.
- **Switzerland:** one of the richest countries despite unusually low university enrolment by rich-country standards.
- **Degree inflation:** as more people go to university, higher education increasingly functions as a status filter rather than a direct source of productivity.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter is especially useful because it separates two debates that are often blurred. One debate is whether education is intrinsically valuable for freedom, citizenship, and personal development. Chang says yes. The other is whether simply expanding formal education will make an economy richer. On that question, he is skeptical.

The chapter matters for development strategy. Poor countries may waste scarce resources if they assume schooling alone can substitute for industrial policy, infrastructure, enterprise development, and institutional reform.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Some readers may think Chang swings too far against education after years of overpraise, especially given the importance of literacy, numeracy, and technical competence.
- Others may argue that educational effects are indirect, long-term, and hard to capture in simple cross-country data.
- These objections are reasonable, but Chang's main point survives: education is not a magic growth switch.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It supports **Thing 15**, because entrepreneurship needs institutions, not just energetic individuals.
- It fits with **Thing 9**, where manufacturing and productive structure matter more than post-industrial rhetoric.
- It links to **Thing 20**, because equal opportunity in education is meaningless without capability and support.

### Exam angle

Use this chapter in essays on human capital, the knowledge economy, development strategy, and the limits of educational determinism.

### Transition

If schooling alone does not guarantee prosperity, then national success must depend more on how firms, industries, and states are organized. Chang therefore returns to the relation between corporate strategy and the national economy.

## Thing 18. What Is Good for General Motors Is Not Necessarily Good for the United States

**Memory hook:** *Corporate success, managerial success, and national success are not the same thing.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that the interests of individual firms do not automatically coincide with the interests of the national economy. Even what appears rational for a firm in the short run may be damaging for workers, suppliers, technological capacity, and the firm's own long-run future.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The standard business-friendly position is that firms create jobs, innovation, and tax revenue, so what benefits business benefits the country. Regulation that constrains firms is therefore often portrayed as anti-growth and anti-national prosperity.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang rejects this simple alignment. Firms contain multiple stakeholders with conflicting interests, and dominant groups inside firms - especially managers and short-term shareholders - may pursue strategies that raise immediate profits while weakening national productive capacity. Regulation can therefore be pro-business in a broader, long-term sense if it prevents firms from degrading shared resources or underinvesting in capabilities.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **General Motors as postwar giant:** once central to American industrial power and war production.
- **GM's long decline:** rather than restoring competitiveness through better cars and deeper investment, it relied on protectionist lobbying, foreign acquisitions, and finance through GMAC.
- **GM's bankruptcy and public rescue:** taxpayers bore the cost of strategies that enriched insiders while eroding industrial strength.
- **South Korea's heavy licensing system:** despite needing hundreds of permits, the economy grew rapidly, showing that regulatory burden alone does not predict stagnation.
- **Examples of useful regulation:** training requirements, child labour bans, and banking restrictions can support long-run productivity.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter corrects a common simplification in policy debate. "Business" is not a unitary national hero. It consists of firms with different time horizons, ownership structures, and incentive systems. A country can have very successful firms in a narrow profit sense while undermining long-run productive development.

The chapter is also important because it reframes regulation. The question is not how much regulation exists in total, but whether regulation raises or lowers collective productive capacity over time.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- A critic may argue that Chang risks judging firms too much from a national perspective in a globally integrated economy.
- Others may say some of GM's decline reflected deeper structural competition rather than simple managerial short-termism.
- Yet his central point still stands: firm-level rationality and national rationality often diverge.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It builds directly on **Thing 2** and **Thing 14**, where shareholder value and executive incentives distort firm behaviour.
- It links to **Thing 8**, because ownership affects where strategic functions remain.
- It supports **Thing 21**, since industrial restructuring is easier when workers are protected.

### Exam angle

Excellent for essays on industrial decline, corporate governance, regulation, national competitiveness, and why "business-friendly" policy can still be economically destructive.

### Transition

Once firm strategy is shown to be only partly aligned with the public good, Chang moves to an even larger scale: whole economies. Are capitalist systems really unplanned, as free-market rhetoric suggests?

## Thing 19. Despite the Fall of Communism, We Are Still Living in Planned Economies

**Memory hook:** *The real choice is not planning or no planning, but what kind of planning.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that planning did not disappear with communism. Modern capitalist economies are deeply planned through states, corporations, infrastructure systems, R&D support, sectoral policy, and internal firm organization. The meaningful question is not whether planning exists, but where and how it should operate.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The common claim is that the collapse of communist central planning proved that modern economies are too complex to be planned. Markets, not plans, are treated as the only workable coordinating mechanism for large-scale societies.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang accepts that Soviet-style comprehensive central planning failed under growing complexity. But he argues that this proves only the limits of one extreme form of planning. Capitalist economies still rely heavily on planning inside firms and through government. Large corporations plan investments, production, supply chains, and cross-subsidies internally. States plan infrastructure, R&D priorities, the activities of state-owned enterprises, and the future of strategic sectors.

So the image of capitalism as pure spontaneous order is false. Capitalism works through a hybrid of planning and markets.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **The Soviet Union as "Upper Volta with rockets":** successful in some coordinated goals, poor at complex consumer allocation.
- **Indicative planning in France, Japan, Korea, Taiwan, Finland, Norway, and Austria:** examples of capitalist planning without full central command.
- **State-owned enterprises:** governments directly plan sizable parts of many capitalist economies.
- **Government-funded R&D in the US:** one of Chang's strongest pieces of evidence that even the archetypal market economy is deeply planned.
- **Herbert Simon's Martian thought experiment:** a visitor would see large organizational islands linked by markets, not a pure market society.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter is conceptually important because it breaks the false opposition between market and plan. It also helps students understand why Chang repeatedly emphasizes firms, industrial policy, and public coordination. Once planning is recognized as normal rather than exceptional, economic debate shifts from ideology toward institutional design.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Free-market defenders may say Chang stretches the term "planning" too broadly, making ordinary management sound equivalent to public economic control.
- Some may argue that internal corporate planning still depends on market competition externally, which is what keeps it disciplined.
- Chang would likely agree, but that only reinforces his hybrid view.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It supports **Thing 12**, where public coordination helps build industries.
- It fits with **Thing 2**, since firms are not mere market points but organized hierarchies.
- It complements **Thing 23**, which criticizes the economics profession for presenting capitalist systems too narrowly.

### Exam angle

Use this chapter for essays on planning, mixed economies, industrial policy, corporate hierarchy, and the institutional structure of capitalism.

### Transition

Once the myth of a purely market society has been dismantled, Chang turns to a moral-political issue that sits at the center of modern liberal thought: is equal opportunity enough to make capitalism fair?

## Thing 20. Equality of Opportunity May Not Be Fair

**Memory hook:** *A race is not fair when some runners start with weights on their legs.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that equal opportunity, while essential, is not sufficient for fairness. Without some equalization of outcomes - especially those affecting children's basic capabilities - formal opportunity remains hollow.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The conventional view is that equality of outcome is dangerous because it weakens incentives, while equality of opportunity is just and efficient. As long as individuals are not legally blocked by race, caste, sex, or birth, differences in outcome can be accepted as the result of effort and talent.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang agrees that a society should reject rigid caste-like restrictions and direct discrimination. But he argues that opportunity cannot be meaningful unless people have the capability to use it. Hungry children, badly housed families, underfunded schools, lack of healthcare, and long-term unemployment all undermine fair competition before any contest even begins.

He therefore defends a limited but real equalization of outcomes through welfare, educational support, public health, and income guarantees. Without this, meritocracy becomes an illusion.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **Confucian Korea and caste restrictions:** birth once tightly constrained advancement.
- **Apartheid South Africa:** formal access to institutions after apartheid did not erase unequal preparation.
- **The "cappuccino society":** a vivid image of racialized class hierarchy after legal equality.
- **Poor children and schooling:** hunger, illness, family burdens, and lack of support make equal school access insufficient.
- **Alejandro Toledo:** one exceptional rise from poverty does not prove equal opportunity exists for millions.
- **Cross-country social mobility:** stronger welfare states are associated with greater mobility.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter is crucial because it joins Chang's moral and economic arguments. It speaks to justice, but also to efficiency. A society that wastes talent because children lack food, healthcare, and educational support is not meritocratic and is not using its human resources well.

It also connects distribution to capability rather than charity. Redistribution is not only a transfer after the fact; it shapes who can compete before the fact.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Some critics may worry that Chang leaves the boundary of "necessary equalization" underdefined.
- Others may argue that family differences and cultural variation will always limit equal opportunity, so public policy cannot fully solve the problem.
- Even so, his main claim is strong: formal equality alone is inadequate.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It builds on **Thing 3**, where incomes are shown not to reflect pure merit.
- It links to **Thing 17**, because educational opportunity depends on broader support.
- It prepares **Thing 21**, where welfare is defended not only morally but dynamically.

### Exam angle

Use this chapter in essays on meritocracy, equality, social mobility, distributive justice, and capability theory.

### Transition

If equal opportunity requires some social protection and capability support, the usual attack on the welfare state becomes harder to sustain. Chang addresses that issue directly next.

## Thing 21. Big Government Makes People More Open to Change

**Memory hook:** *People take risks more readily when they know failure will not destroy them.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that a well-designed welfare state can increase, rather than reduce, economic dynamism. Security can make workers and households more willing to accept restructuring, retraining, and occupational change.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The standard claim is that large welfare states breed laziness, weaken incentives, burden the rich with taxes, and make labour markets rigid. Europe is often contrasted unfavourably with the supposedly flexible and dynamic United States.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang turns the argument around. Extreme insecurity may push people to work harder, but in the wrong ways. They cling to declining industries, avoid risky but productive careers, and demand protectionism. When people have unemployment benefits, retraining support, healthcare, and income protection, they can adapt more easily.

His analogy is memorable: the welfare state is like bankruptcy law for workers. Capitalism encourages entrepreneurial risk-taking by giving firms limited liability and the possibility of debt discharge. Workers need analogous second chances if labour markets are to be both humane and dynamic.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **South Korea after the 1997 crisis:** heightened insecurity pushed top students toward medicine and law because licensed professions looked safer than engineering.
- **US versus Europe on trade protection:** Americans with weak safety nets resist restructuring more fiercely because job loss is catastrophic.
- **Bankruptcy law analogy:** capitalism already accepts second chances for capital; Chang asks why labour should not receive them too.
- **Scandinavian welfare states:** Finland, Norway, and Sweden show that large welfare systems can coexist with strong growth.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter is one of Chang's most politically important. It rejects the idea that efficiency and security are simple opposites. Social protection can be productivity-enhancing by reducing fear, improving matching, and encouraging adaptation.

It also reveals a recurring asymmetry in capitalist ideology: risk-sharing for capital is accepted as necessary, while risk-sharing for labour is treated as moral weakness.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Poorly designed welfare systems can indeed discourage some forms of work or create dependency.
- Institutional transfer is difficult: Scandinavian-style success may depend on high trust, administrative capacity, and universal design.
- Chang recognizes some downsides, but insists the blanket anti-welfare argument is false.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It extends **Thing 20**, since real opportunity requires security.
- It supports **Thing 13**, because redistribution can aid growth under some conditions.
- It complements **Thing 18**, where industrial change harms workers unless adjustment systems exist.

### Exam angle

Excellent for essays on welfare states, flexibility versus security, labour-market reform, and social democracy.

### Transition

With security, investment, and adaptation in view, Chang turns to the sector that most dramatically upset the balance of capitalism in recent decades: finance.

## Thing 22. Financial Markets Need to Become Less, Not More, Efficient

**Memory hook:** *When finance moves too fast, the rest of the economy cannot keep up.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that modern financial markets have become too efficient in the narrow sense of allowing rapid buying, selling, and profit extraction. This speed and liquidity serve short-term financial gain but destabilize the wider economy and starve productive sectors of patient capital.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The orthodox view is that deep, liberalized, innovative financial markets allocate capital efficiently, respond quickly to new opportunities, and therefore support growth. Financial development is often treated as a hallmark of modern prosperity.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang's answer is one of the sharpest in the book: the problem is not that financial markets are too rigid, but that they are too fluid. Finance is far more liquid than productive investment. Because capital can be moved so quickly, shareholders and financiers demand rapid returns. Firms then cut investment, labour, and long-term capability building to satisfy short-term expectations.

At the same time, financial innovation creates layers of claims far removed from real productive assets. That makes valuation more uncertain and crises more likely. To align finance with development, financial systems must be deliberately slowed down and simplified.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **Iceland:** privatization and financial liberalization created a spectacular but fragile boom followed by collapse.
- **Ireland, Latvia, and Dubai:** other finance-led models that ended badly.
- **Financialization of firms like GM, Ford, and GE:** manufacturing firms increasingly made profits through finance.
- **Mortgage-backed securities, CDOs, and CDSs:** layers of claims multiplying on top of the same real assets.
- **Warren Buffett's phrase "weapons of financial mass destruction":** used to underscore derivative danger.
- **James Tobin's "throw sand in the wheels":** the argument for slowing finance through taxes and controls.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter is essential because finance is where many of Chang's other themes converge: bounded rationality, shareholder pressure, inequality, instability, and weak productive investment. It also shows why growth statistics from finance-led booms can be deceptive.

For students, the central lesson is that financial sophistication is not equivalent to economic health. A healthy financial system is one that supports production, innovation, and resilience - not one that maximizes transaction speed.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Financial defenders may argue that too much restriction can choke off innovation and credit.
- Some may say Chang underestimates the informational value of liquid markets and the difficulty of identifying "harmful" instruments in advance.
- Yet the 2008 crisis gives his skepticism strong empirical force.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It builds on **Thing 6** about instability.
- It supports **Thing 2** by explaining shareholder impatience.
- It relies on **Thing 16**, since complexity overwhelms rational actors.
- It feeds into **Thing 23**, where free-market economics is criticized for legitimizing deregulation.

### Exam angle

Use this chapter in essays on financialization, crisis, short-termism, patient capital, and post-2008 reform.

### Transition

Chang ends the 23 Things by turning the critique back on the discipline that has so often justified these outcomes. The final chapter asks not just what policies are wrong, but what kinds of economists and economics have made them look right.

## Thing 23. Good Economic Policy Does Not Require Good Economists

**Memory hook:** *What matters most is not technical orthodoxy but a realistic view of how development works.*

### Central thesis

Chang argues that successful economic policy does not depend on large numbers of highly orthodox economists. In fact, free-market economics has often made policy worse. What matters more is practical intelligence, institutional understanding, and economic ideas suited to structural transformation rather than abstract market purity.

### What mainstream/free-market economists usually claim

The standard view says that interventionist policy is dangerous because it requires sophisticated state judgment, while free-market policy is simpler and therefore safer. Developing countries with weak bureaucracies are told to avoid ambitious state action and follow liberal rules instead.

### Chang's counterargument

Chang points out that many of the most successful developmental states were led not by orthodox economists but by lawyers, engineers, or administrators with a practical grasp of industry and transformation. He then makes a harsher claim: in the last few decades economics, as dominated by free-market theory, has not merely failed to help. It has actively justified slower growth, higher inequality, greater instability, and destructive deregulation.

He does not conclude that economics is useless in every form. Rather, he distinguishes between market-fundamentalist economics and richer traditions - Keynesian, institutionalist, developmentalist, Schumpeterian, Marxian, Simonian, and welfare-based approaches.

### Key examples and illustrations

- **East Asian economic bureaucracies:** often staffed by lawyers or engineers rather than conventional economists.
- **Latin America's highly trained orthodox economists:** their expertise did not deliver East Asian-style success.
- **Queen Elizabeth II's question after the 2008 crisis:** "How come nobody could foresee it?"
- **British Academy response:** the profession's explanation appeared vague and evasive.
- **Alternative economists:** Keynes, Minsky, Kindleberger, List, Schumpeter, Hirschman, Simon, Pigou, Sen, Baumol, Stiglitz.

### Why this chapter matters

This chapter ties the book together. Chang is not merely debating policy details; he is criticizing the intellectual monopoly of one style of economics. The chapter matters because it teaches students to treat economics as a contested field rather than a settled technical science.

It also gives a positive methodological lesson: good policy requires historical knowledge, institutional realism, and awareness of uncertainty.

### Possible weaknesses, limitations, or counterarguments

- Some may say Chang caricatures mainstream economics and underplays the diversity within it.
- Others might object that economists did in fact warn about financial fragility, even if they were not the dominant voices.
- These are fair points, but Chang's broader criticism of the profession's public influence remains persuasive.

### How this chapter connects to other chapters

- It synthesizes **Thing 1** on the political nature of markets.
- It reinforces **Thing 16** and **Thing 22** on rationality and finance.
- It gathers the developmental themes of **Things 7, 11, 12, 17, and 19** into a critique of orthodox expertise.

### Exam angle

Use this chapter in essays on the role of economics as a discipline, policy expertise, pluralism in economic thought, and the intellectual roots of neoliberalism.

### Transition to the full-book synthesis

With the 23 Things complete, Chang's larger picture comes into focus. The book is not just a collection of provocative claims. It is a coherent argument about how capitalism actually works and how it should be governed.

## Major Recurring Themes Across the Whole Book

### 1. Markets are politically made, not naturally given

This is the master theme. Markets require laws, enforcement, moral boundaries, and institutional design. Because they are politically constituted, they can be politically redesigned.

### 2. Capitalism has many varieties

Chang repeatedly resists the claim that there is one best model. American, Scandinavian, German, Japanese, Korean, and other forms of capitalism differ significantly in corporate governance, labour relations, welfare systems, finance, and industrial policy.

### 3. Productive capacity matters more than static efficiency

The free-market view often asks whether current resources are allocated efficiently. Chang asks a different question: which institutions build future capabilities? This is why he stresses manufacturing, industrial policy, long-term investment, and technological learning.

### 4. History refutes neoliberal mythmaking

Many of the policies condemned today - tariffs, subsidies, state ownership, planning, capital controls - were used by today's rich countries while they were developing. Historical memory is politically selective.

### 5. Firms are social organizations, not just profit machines

Corporations have stakeholders, internal hierarchies, routines, and governance systems. Their behaviour cannot be understood purely through market prices. Shareholder value ideology often harms both firms and societies.

### 6. Human beings are neither pure egoists nor perfect rational calculators

Trust, loyalty, pride, and moral commitment matter. So do cognitive limits. Both facts undermine the simplistic anthropology of free-market economics.

### 7. Distribution is not separate from growth

Inequality is not merely an ethical side issue. Distribution affects demand, investment, political stability, capability formation, and social mobility. Growth and fairness interact.

### 8. Finance must serve production, not dominate it

The book repeatedly returns to the danger of short-term capital. Finance is necessary, but its tempo and incentives must be aligned with real investment.

### 9. Development requires collective organization

Poor countries are not poor because individuals lack spirit. They are poor because they lack the institutions, productive structures, and organizational capabilities needed for sustained upgrading.

### 10. Economics should be plural, historical, and institutional

Chang's target is not all economics. It is a specific style that naturalizes markets, ignores history, and strips politics and institutions out of analysis.

---

## Chang's Overall View of Capitalism

Chang is not anti-capitalist in the simple sense. He does not call for abolishing markets, private ownership, or profits. He accepts capitalism as a powerful engine of innovation and wealth creation. However, he insists that capitalism only works tolerably well when it is regulated, socially embedded, and institutionally shaped.

His preferred system is a disciplined, developmental, and socially constrained capitalism - one that supports long-term investment, accepts a significant state role, protects workers from catastrophic insecurity, and refuses to treat financial gain as the supreme social value.

He therefore rejects both extremes:

- he rejects Soviet-style comprehensive central planning,
- and he rejects Anglo-American market fundamentalism.

What he wants instead is a mixed political economy with active public institutions, patient finance, productive industry, and a realistic understanding of human motivation and social justice.

---

## Chang's Criticism of Free-Market Ideology

Chang's criticism operates on several levels.

### 1. Historical criticism

Free-market ideology misdescribes how rich countries actually developed. It universalizes a late-stage policy posture - openness after industrial success - and presents it as the path that created success in the first place.

### 2. Theoretical criticism

It relies on implausible assumptions:

- markets can be clearly separated from politics,
- people are rational maximizers,
- wages reflect merit,
- shareholders are natural guardians of firms,
- finance rewards efficient use of capital,
- low inflation equals stability,
- equality of opportunity alone ensures fairness.

Chang disputes each of these.

### 3. Moral criticism

Free-market ideology often justifies outcomes that are socially harmful by calling them efficient. It naturalizes inequality, insecurity, and underdevelopment.

### 4. Political criticism

It disguises elite interests as neutral expertise. In Chang's telling, anti-inflation orthodoxy, shareholder capitalism, financial liberalization, and anti-state rhetoric all serve particular groups more than society as a whole.

## Chang's Views on Key Topics

### The state

The state is not simply a constraint on markets. It is a market-maker, rule-setter, investor, coordinator, disciplinarian, insurer, and sometimes entrepreneur. A capable state is central to development and to a civilized capitalism.

### Firms

Firms are collective organizations with long-term capabilities. They should not be governed solely for short-term shareholders. Governance arrangements should protect productive investment, workers, and strategic learning.

### Labour

Labour markets are politically structured. Wages often reflect bargaining systems, migration control, and institutional context more than pure merit. Workers need security and second chances to adapt productively.

### Development

Development is a process of building productive capabilities. It requires structural transformation, industrial policy, strategic protection, institutional innovation, and often temporary shelter from full competition.

### Education

Education is socially valuable, but it is not a simple engine of growth. Productive organization matters more than aggregate years of schooling.

### Finance

Finance should provide liquidity and patient capital. When it becomes hyper-mobile and innovation-driven for its own sake, it destabilizes the whole economy.

### Inequality

High inequality is not justified merely because markets produce it. Outcomes depend on rules, power, and social background. Excessive inequality can weaken growth and undermine fairness.

## Final Synthesis: The Whole Book as a Revision Framework

If you need to revise the entire book quickly, the simplest way is to treat it as an argument in five moves.

### Move 1: Destroy the myth of neutral markets

Things 1, 3, 5, and 16 show that markets are political constructions populated by morally mixed and cognitively limited human beings. Therefore market outcomes are not automatically natural, fair, or efficient.

### Move 2: Rethink the firm and the economy

Things 2, 14, 18, and 19 show that corporations are governed organizations, not pure market actors, and that capitalist economies contain a great deal of planning. Shareholder value and executive power often damage long-term productivity.

### Move 3: Reject neoliberal development stories

Things 7, 8, 9, 11, 12, 15, and 17 argue that development depends on building industrial and organizational capabilities, not simply opening markets, expanding schooling, inviting foreign capital, or celebrating entrepreneurship.

### Move 4: Challenge inequality and insecurity

Things 10, 13, 20, and 21 show that high inequality is not a necessary or reliable route to growth, and that welfare systems can promote rather than obstruct dynamism by giving people genuine capabilities and second chances.

### Move 5: Bring finance and economics back into their proper place

Things 6, 22, and 23 argue that anti-inflation obsession, financial deregulation, and free-market economics as a dominant doctrine have made capitalism less stable, less equitable, and less productive.

From these five moves, Chang reaches one broad conclusion: capitalism should be judged not by how closely it approximates an imaginary free market, but by how well it builds productive capabilities, shares security, disciplines finance, and improves human lives.

## 10 Possible Essay Questions with Model Answer Directions

### 1. "There is no such thing as a free market." Discuss.
**Answer direction:** Define Chang's claim carefully. Explain market boundaries as politically chosen. Use child labour, environmental regulation, immigration control, and crisis bailouts. Conclude by showing that the issue is not market versus politics, but which political rules shape markets.

### 2. Does Chang successfully refute shareholder value maximization?
**Answer direction:** Explain the orthodox ownership-risk argument, then develop Chang's critique through limited liability, shareholder exit, managerial-stock alliances, dividends, and buybacks. Use GM and cross-national corporate governance comparisons.

### 3. Are people in rich countries paid according to their productivity?
**Answer direction:** Present the marginal productivity view, then counter with migration controls, system-level productivity, and the bus-driver example. Link to institutions and collective productivity.

### 4. Has the world become more stable under neoliberal macroeconomic policy?
**Answer direction:** Contrast inflation stability with financial instability and labour insecurity. Use moderate inflation cases, banking crises, and anti-inflation policy effects on investment.

### 5. Did today's rich countries become rich through free-market policies?
**Answer direction:** Use Hamilton, protectionist Britain, US tariff history, and East Asian development. Contrast pre-1980 developmental performance with post-1980 neoliberal slowdown in developing regions.

### 6. Can governments pick winners?
**Answer direction:** Reject both naive optimism and absolute skepticism. Use POSCO, Hyundai, US R&D, Japanese councils, and the existence of public failures like Concorde. Emphasize state capacity and learning mechanisms.

### 7. Why does Chang think de-industrialization is a serious problem?
**Answer direction:** Explain the price effect, productivity gap, tradability, and balance-of-payments constraint. Use Britain, Switzerland, Singapore, and the critique of post-industrial fantasy.

### 8. Is inequality necessary for growth?
**Answer direction:** Set out trickle-down logic, then use Golden Age evidence, post-1980 slowdown, falling investment ratios, and the role of welfare in diffusing gains. Bring in CEO pay and top-income concentration.

### 9. Does a large welfare state reduce economic dynamism?
**Answer direction:** Present the orthodox disincentive argument, then answer with worker risk, second chances, Korean medicine example, US versus Europe on adjustment, and Scandinavian growth evidence.

### 10. What is Chang's main criticism of modern economics?
**Answer direction:** Show that his target is not all economics but free-market orthodoxy. Use failure to foresee crisis, misleading assumptions about rationality and markets, and alternative traditions such as Keynes, Minsky, Hirschman, List, and Simon.
