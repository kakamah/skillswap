## One-line map of the whole book

Chang's overall claim: **capitalism works better when markets are politically shaped to support long-term production, social protection, and disciplined finance, rather than being left to free-market ideology.**

## The 23 Things: claim -> example -> link

### 1. No such thing as a free market*
- **Claim:** All markets are rule-bound; "free" markets are just markets whose rules we take for granted.
- **Example:** Child labour was once defended as market freedom.
- **Link:** Foundation for 3, 7, 16, 23.

### 2. Firms should not be run only for owners*
- **Claim:** Shareholders are often too mobile and short-termist to govern firms well.
- **Example:** Shareholder value, dividends, and buybacks weaken long-term investment.
- **Link:** Leads to 14, 18, 22.

### 3. Rich-country workers are often paid above their true market worth*
- **Claim:** Huge wage gaps reflect immigration control and national systems more than individual productivity.
- **Example:** Swedish bus driver vs Indian bus driver.
- **Link:** Connects to 1, 10, 20.

### 4. Washing machine > internet
- **Claim:** Older technologies may transform life more deeply than newer glamorous ones.
- **Example:** Household appliances helped move women into paid work.
- **Link:** Supports 9 and 17.

### 5. Assume selfishness and you may produce worse behaviour
- **Claim:** People are motivated by trust, pride, duty, and cooperation as well as self-interest.
- **Example:** Work-to-rule shows firms depend on goodwill beyond contracts.
- **Link:** Supports 12 and 16.

### 6. Low inflation does not equal real stability
- **Claim:** Anti-inflation policy reduced one kind of instability while increasing financial crises and insecurity.
- **Example:** Post-1980 low inflation coexisted with repeated banking crises.
- **Link:** Feeds into 13 and 22.

### 7. Free-market policies rarely make poor countries rich*
- **Claim:** Rich countries developed through protection, subsidies, and intervention, not pure free trade.
- **Example:** Hamiltonian US and protectionist Britain.
- **Link:** Key base for 11, 12, 19, 23.

### 8. Capital has a nationality
- **Claim:** Multinationals remain nationally rooted in management, R&D, and strategic loyalty.
- **Example:** Chrysler changes nationality as ownership changes.
- **Link:** Supports 9 and 18.

### 9. We do not live in a post-industrial age*
- **Claim:** Manufacturing still matters for productivity, exports, and development.
- **Example:** Computers get cheaper while haircuts get dearer, making manufacturing look smaller than it is.
- **Link:** Connects to 4, 17, 18.

### 10. The US does not clearly have the best living standard
- **Claim:** High average income hides inequality, weak social outcomes, cheap labour, and long hours.
- **Example:** US looks rich partly because services are cheap and workers are insecure.
- **Link:** Connects to 3, 13, 20, 21.

### 11. Africa is not doomed
- **Claim:** Africa's weak growth is not destiny; policy failure matters more than fixed geography or culture.
- **Example:** Africa grew in the 1960s-70s, then stagnated under structural adjustment.
- **Link:** Supports 7 and 12.

### 12. Governments can pick winners*
- **Claim:** States can successfully promote industries; the real issue is institutional quality, not possibility.
- **Example:** POSCO in South Korea.
- **Link:** Builds from 7 and leads to 19.

### 13. Making the rich richer does not automatically enrich everyone*
- **Claim:** Trickle-down economics failed both in growth and in distribution.
- **Example:** Since the 1980s inequality rose but growth slowed.
- **Link:** Connects to 14, 21, 22.

### 14. US managers are over-priced
- **Claim:** Executive pay reflects power, ideology, and weak accountability more than true productivity.
- **Example:** CEO-worker pay ratios in the US far exceed other rich countries.
- **Link:** Reinforces 2 and 13.

### 15. Poor countries are full of entrepreneurs
- **Claim:** Poverty comes less from lack of effort than from lack of productive organization and scalable firms.
- **Example:** Microcredit often produces overcrowded tiny businesses, not transformation.
- **Link:** Connects to 12 and 17.

### 16. We are not smart enough to leave everything to the market
- **Claim:** Human beings have bounded rationality; regulation often helps by reducing complexity.
- **Example:** Even elite financiers did not understand the instruments they were trading.
- **Link:** Supports 1, 5, 22, 23.

### 17. More education alone does not make a country richer
- **Claim:** Education matters, but productive organization matters more for national prosperity.
- **Example:** Switzerland is rich with relatively low university enrolment.
- **Link:** Connects to 9, 15, 20.

### 18. What helps firms may not help nations
- **Claim:** Corporate and national interests can diverge sharply.
- **Example:** GM protected profits and managers for years while weakening US industrial capacity.
- **Link:** Builds on 2, 8, 14.

### 19. Capitalist economies are still planned
- **Claim:** The real issue is not planning vs market, but the right mix of state planning, corporate planning, and market coordination.
- **Example:** Indicative planning and state-funded R&D in capitalist countries.
- **Link:** Connects to 12 and 23.

### 20. Equality of opportunity is not enough
- **Claim:** Formal opportunity is hollow if people lack nutrition, health, schooling quality, and security.
- **Example:** Post-apartheid South Africa still reproduces inequality despite legal equality.
- **Link:** Leads to 21 and links back to 3.

### 21. Big government can make people more open to change*
- **Claim:** Welfare states can increase dynamism by giving workers second chances.
- **Example:** Scandinavian welfare versus US insecurity; Korean students flee into safe professions when security falls.
- **Link:** Supports 13 and 20.

### 22. Finance should become less "efficient"*
- **Claim:** Hyper-liquid finance destabilizes the economy and undermines long-term investment.
- **Example:** Iceland's finance-led boom and collapse; layered derivatives on the same assets.
- **Link:** Integrates 2, 6, 16.

### 23. Good policy does not require orthodox economists*
- **Claim:** Free-market economics has often misled policy; practical developmental success often came from broader, more realistic traditions.
- **Example:** East Asian policy elites were often lawyers and engineers, not neoliberal economists.
- **Link:** Synthesizes the whole book.

## The book's strongest recurring arguments

### A. Anti-free-market argument
- Markets are always politically designed.
- Therefore market outcomes are never purely natural.
- Therefore "less state" is never a neutral benchmark.

### B. Anti-meritocracy argument
- Wages and rewards are shaped by power, borders, institutions, and inherited systems.
- Therefore income is not a clean measure of desert.

### C. Anti-neoliberal development argument
- Rich countries used protection and intervention while developing.
- Poor countries were often forced into openness too early.
- Therefore free-market advice is historically hypocritical and often harmful.

### D. Anti-financialization argument
- Finance should support production, not dominate it.
- Short-term liquidity harms long-term capability building.

### E. Pro-state but non-socialist argument
- Chang is not anti-market.
- He wants regulated, developmental capitalism, not full central planning.

## 5-sentence final synthesis

1. Chang argues that capitalism is not self-regulating but institutionally constructed.  
2. Rich countries became rich through strategic state action, not by following pure free-market doctrine.  
3. Firms, labour markets, and financial markets all work badly when short-term incentives dominate long-term capability building.  
4. Equality, welfare, and industrial policy are not enemies of growth; they can be conditions for better growth.  
5. The central enemy in the book is not capitalism itself but free-market ideology and the narrow economics that defends it.
