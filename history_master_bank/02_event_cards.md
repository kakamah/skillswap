# Event cards: Cambridge AS Level International History 1870–1945

Dense revision notes. Each card is structured for quick recall and essay linkage.

---

## 1. Berlin Conference (1884–85)

**Date:** Nov 1884–Feb 1885 (Berlin).

**Background / causes:** Late-19th-century “scramble for Africa” intensifying colonial claims and friction; Bismarck—initially a reluctant overseas imperialist—seeks a diplomatic framework to manage competition and stabilise relations among European powers rather than leave disputes to unilateral annexation.

**What happened:** Representatives of **13 European states** (no African participation) negotiated ground rules for claiming territory in Africa. Outcome codified chiefly in the **General Act of the Berlin Conference** (often loosely called the **Treaty of Berlin** framework).

**Immediate consequences:** **“Effective occupation”** as a legitimising standard—powers expected to demonstrate administrative control, not just paper claims. **Commercial access:** principle of **free navigation** on the **Congo** and **Niger** (with qualifications in practice). **Anti-slavery language** on paper (humanitarian veneer); **Congo** recognised as **Leopold II’s** personal domain (**Congo Free State**) with catastrophic later results.

**Wider significance:** **Accelerated** the scramble: European governments rushed to plant flags, treaties, and administrators to meet the new “effective occupation” test. **Institutionalised** African exclusion from decisions about Africa’s partition.

**Arguments this helps prove:** **European racial/civilisational arrogance** and diplomatic **exclusion** of colonised peoples; **imperialism driven by interstate rivalry and security** (managing Europe) **not only** narrow economic profit; **“international law”** as a tool of imperial order.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Bismarck**; **Leopold II**; **General Act of Berlin (1885)**; intensified **scramble** and **“effective occupation”** races; link forward to **Fashoda**, **Boer War** context, and **Versailles** debates about self-determination (ironic contrast).

## 2. Fashoda Incident (1898)

**Date:** Sept 1898 (Fashoda, **Sudan**).

**Background / causes:** **Anglo-Egyptian** consolidation southward after Sudanese conflicts; **French** expansion east from **West Africa** (**French Sudan**) under officers such as **Marchand**, seeking a **trans-African** belt of influence and prestige.

**What happened:** **Jean-Baptiste Marchand**’s French expedition and **Herbert Kitchener**’s Anglo-Egyptian forces confronted at **Fashoda**; both asserted sovereignty; crisis escalated to near-war levels in Europe.

**Immediate consequences:** **War threat**; naval/military signalling; **diplomatic compromise:** France backed down in the Nile valley; **Britain** entrenched primacy in **Egypt** and the **Anglo-Egyptian Sudan**; **France** secured future **diplomatic room** in **Morocco** (context for later **Entente** diplomacy and **Moroccan crises**).

**Wider significance:** Demonstrates imperial friction could reach **brinkmanship** yet be **resolved** through great-power bargaining—**imperialism produced crises**, but **not every crisis** meant **European war**.

**Arguments this helps prove:** **Imperial rivalry was real and dangerous**; **compromise was possible** when domestic/strategic costs outweighed gains; **Africa** as chessboard for **European** priorities.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Marchand**; **Kitchener**; **Britain–France** imperial competition → later **Entente Cordiale (1904)** as a wider **colonial swap** of tensions; foreshadows **Moroccan crises (1905, 1911)**.

## 3. Boxer Rebellion (1898–1901)

**Date:** 1898–1901 (peak violence **1900**; **Boxer Protocol** **1901**).

**Background / causes:** **Semi-colonial** penetration of **China** after **Opium Wars** and **unequal treaties**; **spheres of influence**; missionary presence; economic resentment; **Qing** manoeuvring between reform and reaction; **Yihetuan (“Boxers”)** anti-foreign, anti-Christian violence.

**What happened:** Uprising targets **foreigners** and **Chinese Christians**; **Empress Dowager Cixi** eventually backs anti-foreign elements; **Eight-Nation Alliance** intervenes; **relief expeditions**; occupation/punitive operations; diplomatic settlement imposed.

**Immediate consequences:** **Heavy indemnity** (often cited in textbooks around **450 million taels** with interest—huge long-term drain); foreign military presence and strengthened **treaty port** control; further erosion of **Qing** authority; intensified Chinese humiliation narrative.

**Wider significance:** **China’s** subordination **entrenched**; shows **imperial powers could cooperate militarily** against a shared “threat” despite their own rivalries.

**Arguments this helps prove:** **Western/Japanese imperial coordination** when interests aligned; **Chinese weakness** as structural; **nationalist anger** as a long fuse for **20th-century** revolution.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Eight-Nation Alliance**; **Boxer Protocol (1901)**; link to **Russo-Japanese War** competition in **Manchuria**; foreshadows **May Fourth (1919)** and **Warlord Era**; Japanese expansionism in China accelerates contextually.

## 4. Russo-Japanese War (1904–05)

**Date:** Feb **1904**–Sept **1905**; **Treaty of Portsmouth** Sept **1905**.

**Background / causes:** Rivalry over **Manchuria** and **Korea**; **Triple Intervention (1895)** returned **Liaodong/Port Arthur** to China then **Russia** leased/developed **Port Arthur**; **Japan** saw **Russian** delays/denials on withdrawal/recognition as intolerable; failed diplomacy.

**What happened:** **Japan** surprise-attacks **Russian** assets (**Port Arthur** area, **Feb 1904**); **Japanese** naval superiority in theatre; land campaigns including **Mukden**; **Tsushima (May 1905)**—**Admiral Togo** annihilates **Baltic Fleet** sent the long way; **USA** mediates **Portsmouth**.

**Immediate consequences:** **Russia** loses major **Far East** leverage; **Japan** recognised as dominant over **Korea** (annexation **1910** follows logically); **US** rises as Pacific broker (**Roosevelt**).

**Wider significance:** First major modern war where an **Asian industrialised power** defeats a **European great power**; exposes **Russian** military/administrative weakness—feeds **1905 Revolution** pressures.

**Arguments this helps prove:** **Japanese modernisation (Meiji)** translated into **great-power** military capability; **race** hierarchies of empire were **strategically false**; **US** interests in **Pacific equilibrium**.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Togo**; **Roosevelt**; **Treaty of Portsmouth (1905)**; **Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902)** context; link to **WWI** Far East and **21 Demands (1915)**.

---

## 5. Spanish-American War (1898)

**Date:** April–Aug **1898**; **Treaty of Paris** Dec **1898**.

**Background / causes:** **Cuban** independence struggle against **Spanish** rule; **USS Maine** explosion (**Feb 1898**) blamed on Spain in US public opinion; **yellow press** mobilisation; **US** economic/strategic interest in **Caribbean** markets and a **canal** route.

**What happened:** **US** naval victories—**Manila Bay (Dewey)** and **Santiago** campaign; rapid Spanish collapse; US acquires overseas territories via settlement.

**Immediate consequences:** **Treaty of Paris:** **USA** gains **Philippines**, **Puerto Rico**, **Guam**; **Cuba** nominally independent but **Platt Amendment (1901)** entrenches US intervention rights and naval base access (**Guantánamo**).

**Wider significance:** Marks **USA** as an **overseas imperial power** with **Pacific** bases—transforms global strategy and foreshadows **20th-century** superpower posture.

**Arguments this helps prove:** **Isolationism** was not a fixed law—**economic + naval + popular** pressures produced **expansion**; imperialism was **American** as well as European.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **McKinley**; **Theodore Roosevelt** (Rough Riders / naval politics); **Treaty of Paris (1898)**; **Platt Amendment**; **Philippine** insurgency; link to **Open Door** and **Pacific War** long arc.

## 6. Paris Peace Conference (1919)

**Date:** Jan **1919** onwards; treaties signed **1919–1920**.

**Background / causes:** **WWI** devastation; collapse of **German**, **Ottoman**, **Austro-Hungarian**, **Russian** (Bolshevik) old orders; incompatible aims—**Wilson**’s **Fourteen Points**/self-determination vs **Clemenceau**’s French security/revenge vs **Lloyd George**’s electoral pressures and imperial maintenance.

**What happened:** **Big Three** dominated; defeated powers largely **excluded** from negotiation; output split into **five major treaties** (e.g. **Versailles** with Germany, plus **St Germain**, **Trianon**, **Neuilly**, **Sèvres**/later revision).

**Immediate consequences:** **League of Nations** Covenant; **German** territorial losses, **disarmament**, **Article 231** “war guilt,” **reparations** machinery; **Italian** grievances (“**mutilated victory**”); **US Senate** rejection of **Versailles/League**; new states in Eastern Europe with **minority** problems.

**Wider significance:** Settlement **encoded instability**: resentments in Germany/Italy/Japan; incomplete self-determination; **structural** flaws contributed to **1930s** breakdown (debated—**intentionalist** vs **structural** essays).

**Arguments this helps prove:** **Peacemaking** satisfied **no coalition member fully**; **compromise** produced **universal resentment**; **idealism** collided with **realpolitik** and **imperial** continuity.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Wilson**, **Clemenceau**, **Lloyd George**; **Treaty of Versailles (1919)**; **League Covenant**; **Mandate system**; leads to **Ruhr 1923**, **Locarno**, **Depression**, **rise of Hitler**.

## 7. French occupation of the Ruhr (1923)

**Date:** Jan **1923**–**1925** (occupation phased/resolved diplomatically via **Dawes** dynamics).

**Background / causes:** **Reparations** crisis under **Versailles**; **Germany** defaults/delays (complex chain including **passive resistance** politics); **France** (with **Belgium**) insists on **coercion** to extract payment/resources.

**What happened:** **French/Belgian** troops occupy the **Ruhr**—Germany’s industrial heartland; **German** government encourages **passive resistance** (non-cooperation).

**Immediate consequences:** Industrial **paralysis**; **government** financing of resistance fuels **monetary collapse**—**hyperinflation** peaks **late 1923**; Franco-British **friction** (Britain generally opposed the occupation strategy); political radicalisation inside Germany.

**Wider significance:** Demonstrates **French security-through-coercion** could be **self-defeating**; crisis pipeline to **Dawes Plan (1924)** and temporary stabilisation.

**Arguments this helps prove:** **Reparations** were **central** to **1920s** European politics; **economic interdependence** made **punitive extraction** explosive; **Weimar** instability had **international** drivers.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Poincaré**; **Stresemann** (policy shift afterwards); **Dawes Plan**; **Young Plan** later; link to **Locarno** psychology.

---

## 8. Locarno Conference (1925)

**Date:** Oct **1925**; agreements signed **Locarno**, **Dec 1925** in **London**.

**Background / causes:** **Dawes** eased immediate reparations/reconstruction; **Stresemann** pursues **rapprochement** to end Germany’s pariah status and unlock economic recovery; **Allies** want western security reassurance.

**What happened:** **Rhine Pact** core: **Germany/France/Belgium** mutually guarantee **western frontiers** (including **demilitarised Rhineland**); **Britain** and **Italy** as **guarantors** of that western settlement.

**Immediate consequences:** Germany seen to **accept** **Versailles** **western** territorial/demilitarised provisions diplomatically; **Germany** admitted to **League (1926)**; **“Spirit of Locarno”** optimism.

**Wider significance:** **Reconciliation partial by design**—**no parallel guarantees** for **Germany’s eastern borders** with **Poland/Czechoslovakia**; leaves **eastern Europe** as a future flashpoint.

**Arguments this helps prove:** **1920s stability** was **real but shallow**; **security guarantees** followed **power politics**, not universal justice; **appeasement’s later** failures partly rooted in **asymmetric** commitments.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Stresemann**, **Aristide Briand**; **Locarno Treaties (1925)**; prelude to **Kellogg-Briand (1928)**; contrast with **Hitler**’s **1936** Rhineland breach.

## 9. Wall Street Crash (1929)

**Date:** **Oct 1929** (panic from **24 Oct—Black Thursday**; worst single session **29 Oct—Black Tuesday**).

**Background / causes:** **US** boom built on **credit**, **margin buying**, and **overvaluation**; agricultural/global **overproduction**; **protectionism** (**Fordney-McCumber** era context; **Smoot-Hawley** follows **1930**) shrank trade; fragile **international lending** chains (notably to **Germany**).

**What happened:** **Equity** market collapse triggers **bank failures**, **deflationary spiral**, mass **unemployment**; confidence collapses across **banking** and **industry**.

**Immediate consequences:** **Great Depression**; **US loans** to **Germany** dry up → **Weimar** economic shock → political radicalisation; global **unemployment** and **trade** contraction.

**Wider significance:** Undermined the **economic base** of **1920s** diplomatic “normality”; helped enable **Nazi** electoral breakthrough and **Japanese** militarist expansion as states sought autarkic/imperial “fixes.”

**Arguments this helps prove:** **Interwar peace** rested on **fragile credit** (**US lending**); **economic crisis** translated into **international** instability; **isolationist** **USA** could still **destabilise** Europe by **withdrawing capital**.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Hoover**; **Smoot-Hawley Tariff (1930)**; **Lausanne** reparations debates; link to **Banking Crisis**, **Nazi rise**, **Manchuria 1931**.

## 10. Japan invades Manchuria (1931)

**Date:** **Sept 1931** (**Mukden Incident**); **Manchukuo** declared **1932**.

**Background / causes:** **Kwantung Army** **autonomy** and expansionist ideology; **Chinese** weakness (**Nanjing** government limited control; disasters/civil conflict); strategic/economic value of **Manchuria**.

**What happened:** Staged incident → rapid military occupation; puppet state **Manchukuo** created with **Puyi** as figurehead; **League** dispatches **Lytton Commission**.

**Immediate consequences:** **Lytton Report** recommends non-recognition and restoration principles; **Assembly** vote (**42–1**) but **no effective enforcement**; **Japan** rejects outcome and **withdraws from League (1933)**.

**Wider significance:** First major **League failure** against **great-power aggression**; reveals **Britain/France** unwilling to risk **Far East war** for **collective security**.

**Arguments this helps prove:** **Collective security** without **great-power** will = **paper**; **League** could **investigate** but not **compel**; **precedent** for **1930s** aggression.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Kwantung Army**; **Lytton Commission**; **Stimson Doctrine** (non-recognition—limited effect); connects to **Second Sino-Japanese War** escalation and **Pacific War** origins.

## 11. Italy invades Abyssinia (1935–36)

**Date:** **Oct 1935** invasion; war concludes **1936**.

**Background / causes:** **Mussolini** seeks **prestige** and empire; **Depression** pressures; admiration/competition with **Hitler**’s boldness; **Abyssinia (Ethiopia)** as target (prior **Adwa 1896** humiliation memory).

**What happened:** **Italian** invasion from **Eritrea/Somalia**; **Haile Selassie** appeals to **League**; **League** condemns and imposes **sanctions**—**not** on **oil/coal/steel** (critical gaps).

**Immediate consequences:** **Sanctions fail**; **Italy** conquers **Abyssinia**; **Hoare–Laval** secret partition plan leaks → resignations; **Italy** leaves **League**; **Rome–Berlin Axis** tightens.

**Wider significance:** **A. J. P. Taylor**-style verdict: fatally damaged **League** moral authority—**aggression paid**.

**Arguments this helps prove:** **Britain/France** prioritised **imperial/Mediterranean** calculations over **League** principle; **sanctions** without **strategic** commitment are **theatre**; **fascist** powers **learned** the system was **soft**.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Mussolini**; **Haile Selassie**; **Hoare–Laval Pact**; link to **Spanish Civil War** non-intervention; **Rome-Berlin Axis**; **Stresa Front** collapse narrative.

## 12. Remilitarisation of the Rhineland (1936)

**Date:** **7 Mar 1936**.

**Background / causes:** **Versailles** demilitarised **Rhineland**; **Locarno** reaffirmed; **Hitler** uses **Franco-Soviet Treaty** as **pretext** claiming encirclement; **Wehrmacht** still relatively weak early on.

**What happened:** **German troops** re-enter **Rhineland**; **Hitler** privately prepared withdrawal if **France** militarily intervened—**intervention did not occur**.

**Immediate consequences:** No credible **Franco-British** counterstroke; **Hitler** reads **weakness/appeasement**; **Rhineland** fortification later alters **French** strategic calculus.

**Wider significance:** **Cheap** point to stop aggression—lost; emboldens further **revisionism** (**Anschluss**, **Sudetenland**).

**Arguments this helps prove:** **Appeasement** was not only moral cowardice—also **miscalculation** and **post-WWI** war-weariness; **Hitler**’s early moves included **bluff**; **French** political/strategic caution enabled **Nazi** consolidation.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Hitler**; **Locarno** guarantees tested; leads toward **Anschluss 1938**, **Munich 1938**; compare **Schuschnigg** crisis.

## 13. Spanish Civil War (1936–39)

**Date:** **July 1936**–**Apr 1939**.

**Background / causes:** **Spanish** polarisation—**Republic** vs **Nationalist** right; collapse of **Primo de Rivera** legacy; **CEDA**/**Popular Front** cycles; army revolt led by **Franco**.

**What happened:** **Coup** becomes war; **Germany** (**Condor Legion**) and **Italy** send **men/material**; **USSR** aids **Republicans** (via **Comintern** channels); **Britain/France** **Non-Intervention Committee**—**observed in the breach**.

**Immediate consequences:** **Franco** victory; **authoritarian** state; brutal post-war repression; **Republic** destroyed.

**Wider significance:** **Ideological** proxy war—**fascism** vs **communism** with **liberal** democracies paralysed; **Luftwaffe** tests **tactics** (**Guernica**).

**Arguments this helps prove:** **League** irrelevant; **appeasement** extended to **civil conflict**; **intervention** shaped outcome; **Axis** cooperation deepened.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Franco**; **Hitler**; **Mussolini**; **Stalin**; **International Brigades**; link to **Munich** mindset and **outbreak of WWII** radicalisation.

## 14. Anschluss (March 1938)

**Date:** **12–13 Mar 1938**.

**Background / causes:** **Versailles**/**St Germain** forbade union; **1934** coup attempt failed partly because **Mussolini** guaranteed **Austrian** independence; by **1938** **Rome–Berlin** alignment removes that brake.

**What happened:** **Austrian** **Nazi** pressure; **Schuschnigg** plebiscite gambit fails; **German** entry; **Anschluss** declared—**Austria** absorbed.

**Immediate consequences:** Germany gains **population**, **troops**, **resources** (**iron ore**), strategic depth; **Czechoslovakia** **surrounded**; major **Versailles** violation **unresisted**.

**Wider significance:** Shows **fascist diplomacy** shifting—**Mussolini** now **enabler** not blocker; **Western** response feeble.

**Arguments this helps prove:** **Appeasement** failing in sequence; **French** political turmoil reduced decisiveness; **Chamberlain** Britain unwilling to risk war for **Austria**.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Hitler**; **Schuschnigg**; **Mussolini**; leads directly to **Sudeten crisis** → **Munich**; compare **Hossbach**/**Sudeten** planning context.

## 15. Munich Conference (Sept 1938)

**Date:** **29–30 Sept 1938**.

**Background / causes:** **Hitler** demands **Sudetenland** (**~3.5m** German speakers); **Czechoslovakia**’s **border fortifications** and industry made cession strategically fatal; **war scare** summer **1938**.

**What happened:** **Hitler**, **Chamberlain**, **Daladier**, **Mussolini**; **Czechoslovakia** **excluded**; **USSR** excluded; agreement cedes **Sudetenland** to Germany.

**Immediate consequences:** Czechoslovakia loses **~70%+** heavy industry and key defences; left **defenceless**; **Chamberlain** claims **“peace for our time”**; domestic UK vote **369–150** backing government policy.

**Wider significance:** **Textbook appeasement**—handed initiative to **Hitler**; **Churchill**’s **“total…defeat”** framing for essay counterpoints.

**Arguments this helps prove:** **Appeasement** had **rational** drivers (**WWI trauma**, **rearmament lag**, empire overstretch) yet **strategic** outcome disastrous; **self-determination** rhetoric **weaponised** by dictator.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Chamberlain**; **Daladier**; **Beneš**; **Munich Agreement**; leads to **Prague 1939** end of Czech independence; pathway to **Nazi-Soviet Pact** and **Poland**.

## 16. Nazi–Soviet Pact (August 1939)

**Date:** **23 Aug 1939** (Molotov–Ribbentrop).

**Background / causes:** **Stalin** sought **collective security** with West; **Munich** and **slow** Anglo-French talks bred distrust; fear of **two-front** war vs **Germany** and **Japan**; **Hitler** needs **clear eastern flank** for **Poland**.

**What happened:** **Non-aggression** pact; **secret protocol** spheres: German freedom to act in **western Poland**; Soviet gains in **eastern Poland**, **Baltic states**, **Finnish**/**Romanian** adjustments (details staged).

**Immediate consequences:** Removes **last deterrent** calculus blocking **1 Sept** invasion of **Poland**; **shock** worldwide—ideological enemies as **deal partners**.

**Wider significance:** **Realpolitik** supreme; **imperial** partition logic returns in **Eastern Europe**.

**Arguments this helps prove:** **Stalin** prioritised **state survival/expansion** over **ideology**; **Western** failure to secure **Soviet** alliance had **strategic** costs; **interwar** diplomacy **cynical** at great-power level.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Molotov**; **Ribbentrop**; **Stalin**; **Hitler**; invasion of **Poland**; **Winter War**; **Katyn** context; **Barbarossa 1941** breaks pact.

## 17. Pearl Harbor (7 December 1941)

**Date:** **7 Dec 1941** (Hawaii); US enters war **8 Dec** (Japan); **Germany** declares war on **USA** **11 Dec**.

**Background / causes:** **Japanese** continental war in **China**; **US** diplomatic pressure, asset freezes, escalating **embargo** especially **oil**; failed negotiations (**Hull note** context); **Japanese** decision for **southern** expansion + preventive strike.

**What happened:** Carrier air strike on **Pearl Harbor**; battleship-centric damage; airfield losses; **~2402** military killed (common textbook figure); **8** battleships damaged/sunk (many salvaged); **~188** aircraft destroyed.

**Immediate consequences:** **US** **isolationism** collapses; **global war** alignment: **Germany** brings **USA** into **European** war; **unconditional** mobilisation begins.

**Wider significance:** WWII becomes truly **global**; Pacific strategy reshapes **decolonisation** timelines indirectly via power shift.

**Arguments this helps prove:** **Japanese** **gamble**—tactical surprise, **strategic** misjudgement: **carriers** absent/saved; **oil** onshore not eliminated; shallow-water salvage lesson—**decisive battle** thinking vs **industrial** USA.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Yamamoto**; **Tojo**; **Roosevelt**; leads to **Pacific War** campaigns; **Midway 1942**; **atomic** decision context.

## 18. Long March (1934–35)

**Date:** **Oct 1934**–**Oct 1935** (Yenan arrival).

**Background / causes:** **Chiang Kai-shek**’s **Fifth Encirclement Campaign** with **German**-influenced doctrine; **CCP** base areas compressed; **CCP** leadership crises.

**What happened:** **~86,000** begin breakout; **~9,000 km** traverse; massive attrition—**~8,000** reach **Shaanxi**; **Mao Zedong** consolidates leadership narrative and practice en route (**Zunyi** politico-military turning point **Jan 1935**—often examined with the March).

**Immediate consequences:** **CCP** survives with **Yan’an** (**Yenan**) base; reorganised **Red Army** mythos.

**Wider significance:** Military retreat reframed as **moral/political victory**; cadre survival seeds **1949** revolution elite.

**Arguments this helps prove:** **CCP resilience** under extinction pressure; **Mao**’s **party-army** model; **propaganda** converting defeat into legitimacy.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Mao**; **Chiang**; **Zunyi Conference**; connects to **Xi’an Incident** and **Second United Front** against Japan.

---

## 19. Xi’an Incident (December 1936)

**Date:** **Dec 1936** (kidnapping **12–25 Dec**).

**Background / causes:** **Zhang Xueliang** and allies resent **Chiang**’s priority on anti-CCP **extermination** campaigns while **Japan** occupies **Manchuria**; nationalist pressure for **anti-Japanese** unity.

**What happened:** **Zhang** detains **Chiang** in **Xi’an**; forced negotiations involving **CCP** representatives (e.g. **Zhou Enlai** channel); **Chiang** released after commitments.

**Immediate consequences:** **Second United Front**—**KMT–CCP** truce/cooperation against **Japan** (uneasy, contingent).

**Wider significance:** Pauses **civil war** at critical moment; gives **CCP** legitimacy and breathing space—long-run beneficiary.

**Arguments this helps prove:** **Chinese nationalism** could **temporarily** override **ideological** civil war; **warlord** politics shaped **national** strategy.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Chiang Kai-shek**; **Zhang Xueliang**; **Zhou Enlai**; prelude to **Marco Polo Bridge 1937** and full **Second Sino-Japanese War**.

---

## 20. May Fourth Movement (1919)

**Date:** **4 May 1919** protests; movement ripples through **1919–20s**.

**Background / causes:** **Versailles** decision to transfer **German** **Shandong** rights to **Japan** rather than restore full Chinese sovereignty—perceived betrayal of **China**’s WWI contribution hopes.

**What happened:** **3000+** **Peking/Beijing** students demonstrate; strikes and boycotts spread nationally; intellectual ferment.

**Immediate consequences:** Government refusal to sign **Versailles** (Chinese delegation); surge in **nationalist** organisations; radical intellectual currents—**Marxism** gains traction among elites.

**Wider significance:** watershed in **modern Chinese** political consciousness; organisational prelude to **CCP founding (1921)**.

**Arguments this helps prove:** **Versailles** generated **non-European** resentment; **imperialism** shaped **Chinese** revolution; **intellectual** mobilisation matters as much as armies.

**Related actors / treaties / later developments:** **Versailles Treaty** (Shandong clauses); **Twenty-One Demands** memory; **CCP (1921)**; **Sun Yat-sen**/KMT revival context; connects to **Northern Expedition** and **Nanjing decade**.
