# Cambridge AS Level International History (1870–1945): Actor Profiles

Dense reference profiles for major leaders, states, and blocs.

---

## Leaders

### 1. Otto von Bismarck (German Chancellor)

| Field | Content |
|--------|---------|
| **Name** | Otto von Bismarck, architect of German unification; Chancellor of the German Empire. |
| **Aims** | Secure the new Reich through a stable European balance; limit Franco-Russian alignment; preserve conservative domestic order. |
| **Fears** | Encirclement (especially France + Russia); war on two fronts; domestic radicals unsettling the state. |
| **Constraints** | New great power surrounded by rivals; need to reassure other powers while satisfying German nationalism; Kaiser and Reichstag politics. |
| **Actions** | **Berlin Conference (1884–85)** — framed “civilising” rules for African partition while limiting direct Franco-British clashes. Initially **resisted colonial expansion**; later yielded under pressure. Built the **alliance system** (Dual Alliance with Austria-Hungary, Dreikaiserbund manoeuvres, **Reinsurance Treaty** with Russia until dropped). |
| **Key events** | Unification wars’ legacy; Berlin Conference; shift from continental diplomacy toward colonial concessions; succession by Wilhelm II (1890) ending his system. |
| **Changed over time** | From **continental realpolitik** and alliance juggling to **greater colonial involvement** as domestic/industrial lobbies demanded empire; dismissed 1890 — policies partly reversed by successors. |
| **Why they matter** | His **caution and alliance craft** aimed to manage risk; contrasts sharply with **Wilhelm II’s assertive Weltpolitik**, a standard textbook contrast explaining how German diplomacy moved from restraint to provocation. |

### 2. Kaiser Wilhelm II (German Emperor from 1888)

| **Name** | Wilhelm II, German Emperor and King of Prussia (1888–1918). |
| **Aims** | **Weltpolitik** — global prestige and a “place in the sun”; **naval expansion** to rival Britain; colonial and commercial assertiveness. |
| **Fears** | Encirclement by **France, Russia, and Britain**; diplomatic isolation; domestic challenge to monarchical authority. |
| **Constraints** | Inherited **alliance commitments**; fiscal and parliamentary limits; risk that naval and colonial policy would **antagonise Britain** while France–Russia tightened. |
| **Actions** | **Kruger Telegram** (1896) — congratulated Boers, offending Britain. **Tangier speech** (1905) — challenged French influence in Morocco, triggering **First Moroccan Crisis**. **Agadir crisis** (1911) — gunboat *Panther*, heightening Anglo-German tension. **Naval laws** — Anglo-German naval race. **Abdication** (November 1918) after military collapse and revolution. |
| **Key events** | Dismissal of Bismarck; Moroccan crises; naval race; July Crisis and **WWI**; German defeat and abdication. |
| **Changed over time** | From symbolic **new emperor** to driver of **aggressive world policy**; by 1918 from war leader to **exile** as the Hohenzollern order collapsed. |
| **Why they matter** | Personal and systemic **aggression** and **naval rivalry** helped **reverse Bismarck’s caution**, deepen **ententes**, and raise the probability of **general European war**. |

### 3. Cecil Rhodes (British imperialist)

| **Name** | Cecil Rhodes, businessman, Cape politician, architect of **British South Africa Company** expansion. |
| **Aims** | **Cape-to-Cairo** corridor; **British supremacy** in southern/central Africa; mineral wealth and strategic control. |
| **Fears** | Afrikaner or rival European powers blocking northward expansion; limited chartered company resources versus vast claims. |
| **Constraints** | Company rule, not full Crown control; London’s intermittent reluctance; local African polities’ resistance. |
| **Actions** | **Jameson Raid** (1895–96) — unauthorised raid into Transvaal from Rhodesia; discredited Rhodes politically, worsened **Uitlander**/Boer tensions, prelude to **South African War**. |
| **Key events** | Chartered **Rhodesia**; Jameson Raid; legacy of mineral imperialism and white settler rule. |
| **Changed over time** | From dominant **Cape–Rhodesia** operator to **resigned Cape premiership** after Raid; influence continued indirectly in mining and territory. |
| **Why they matter** | Embodies **aggressive British imperialism** and the **privatised violence** of chartered companies; Raid illustrates how **elite adventurism** could **destabilise** regions and **strain** British politics. |

### 4. King Leopold II of Belgium

| **Name** | Leopold II, King of the Belgians; personal ruler of the **Congo Free State** (1885–1908). |
| **Aims** | **Personal fortune** from **rubber and ivory**; colonial prestige masked as philanthropy (“civilising mission”). |
| **Fears** | Exposure of atrocities; loss of personal control; international pressure from missionaries, journalists, and rival powers. |
| **Constraints** | Need to maintain façade of reform; Belgian and international opinion; eventual **transfer to Belgian state** rule. |
| **Actions** | **Forced labour** quotas on villages; **violence and mutilation** as punishment; concession companies as extractive machines. **1908** — compelled to **cede Congo to the Belgian government**. |
| **Key events** | Berlin Conference recognition of personal rule; **Congo reform** campaigns; **Casement**-era reporting; annexation by Belgium. |
| **Changed over time** | From **unaccountable personal fief** to **formal colony** under Belgian administration — exploitation continued but under a different legal shell. |
| **Why they matter** | **Extreme case** of **colonial extraction** and **state-sponsored terror** in the **Scramble for Africa**; central to debates on **humanitarianism vs. empire**. |

### 5. Theodore Roosevelt (US President 1901–09)

| **Name** | Theodore Roosevelt, 26th US President; former Assistant Secretary of the Navy, Rough Rider, “big stick” practitioner. |
| **Aims** | **US great-power status**; **strong navy**; hemispheric dominance; Pacific leverage; mediation as proof of weight. |
| **Fears** | European re-entanglement in Americas; instability in Caribbean defaulting on debts; strategic weakness versus other navies. |
| **Constraints** | Congress, public opinion, and need to justify intervention as order-building not pure annexation. |
| **Actions** | **Roosevelt Corollary** (1903) to **Monroe Doctrine** — US right to intervene in chronic wrongdoing. **Panama Canal** — support for Panamanian separation and canal zone control. **Naval expansion** (“Great White Fleet”). **Treaty of Portsmouth** (1905) — ended Russo-Japanese War; **Nobel Peace Prize**. |
| **Key events** | Spanish-American War legacy; Panama; Corollary; naval display; East Asia balance-of-power diplomacy. |
| **Changed over time** | From **regional reformer** within Republican progressive politics to **global strategist** using navy and arbitration. |
| **Why they matter** | Marks the shift from **isolationist myth** to **active imperial and naval power** in **Latin America** and **Pacific** baselines for 20th-century US policy. |

### 6. Woodrow Wilson (US President 1912–21)

| **Name** | Woodrow Wilson, 28th US President; academic turned wartime leader and peace architect. |
| **Aims** | **“Peace without victory”** (ideal); **Fourteen Points**; **League of Nations**; **self-determination** (selectively applied); open diplomacy and arms control as norms. |
| **Fears** | **Secret diplomacy** and **arms races** repeating 1914; Bolshevik contagion; permanent European rivalry. |
| **Constraints** | **Republican Senate** and **Article X** controversy; **European allies** demanding **security, territory, and reparations**; US domestic **isolationist** sentiment; **stroke (1919)** reducing his political leverage. |
| **Actions** | **Neutrality** to 1917; **unrestricted submarine warfare** and **Zimmermann Telegram** context for **war entry**; led US at **Paris Peace Conference**; failed to secure **Treaty of Versailles** ratification with League. |
| **Key events** | US entry into **WWI**; **Armistice**; **Versailles**; **League Covenant**; **Treaty rejection** by Senate. |
| **Changed over time** | **Neutral mediator** → **war president** → **idealist peacemaker** hobbled at home — vision **partially realised abroad**, **rejected at home**. |
| **Why they matter** | **Language of self-determination** and **international organisation** shaped the **post-1918 order**; US **non-membership** in the League epitomises **American ambivalence** and **limits of Wilsonianism**. |

### 7. Georges Clemenceau (French PM 1917–20)

| **Name** | Georges Clemenceau (“The Tiger”), French Prime Minister during final war phase and Paris peace. |
| **Aims** | **Cripple Germany** militarily and economically; **avenge 1871** and **Alsace-Lorraine** return; **permanent French security** via frontier and Rhineland solutions. |
| **Fears** | **German recovery** and **revanche**; Anglo-American unwillingness to enforce harsh terms; Bolshevik spillover. |
| **Constraints** | Need for **British and US** consent; **war weariness**; limits of how much Germany could pay without chaos. |
| **Actions** | Pushed **harsh Versailles** clauses; wanted **independent Rhineland** and deeper **dismemberment** of Germany; emphasised **reparations** and **guilt** (**Article 231**). |
| **Key events** | French war leadership 1917–18; **Versailles**; **occupation** issues; postwar security politics. |
| **Changed over time** | From **wartime unifier** to **hard-line peacemaker**; some demands **diluted** by allies but **security obsession** defined French interwar stance. |
| **Why they matter** | **French security anxieties** at Paris **drove punitive elements** of Versailles, feeding **German resentment** and **interwar tension** — textbook link between **victors’ fear** and **unstable peace**. |

---

### 8. David Lloyd George (British PM 1916–22)

| **Name** | David Lloyd George, British Liberal Prime Minister; architect of coalition war leadership and Paris bargaining. |
| **Aims** | **Punish Germany** enough to satisfy voters and **strategic needs**, but **preserve European recovery** so Britain could **trade** and avoid continental chaos. |
| **Fears** | **Bolshevik revolution** spreading; **French overreach** breaking balance; **imperial overstretch**; Irish and colonial troubles. |
| **Constraints** | **1918 “coupon” election** — mandate to **“make Germany pay”**; **anti-German public** mood; dependence on **Dominions** and Conservatives in coalition. |
| **Actions** | **Moderating voice** at Paris versus Clemenceau’s extremes; still signed **Versailles**; later **Genoa Conference (1922)** — engagement with **economic diplomacy** and **Soviet trade** discussions (context of postwar reconstruction). |
| **Key events** | War cabinet; **Versailles**; Irish Treaty; **Chanak** crisis context; Genoa. |
| **Changed over time** | From **dynamic war leader** to **postwar negotiator** caught between **public vengeance** and **realist recovery**; coalition and imperial issues eroded his base. |
| **Why they matter** | Embodies **British middle position**: **less idealist than Wilson**, **less punitive than Clemenceau** — explains **compromise treaty** that satisfied nobody fully. |

### 9. John Maynard Keynes (British economist)

| **Name** | John Maynard Keynes, Treasury adviser at Paris; critic of reparations design. |
| **Aims** | **Stable European economy**; **moderate** peace terms to avoid **deflationary spiral** and political radicalisation. |
| **Fears** | **Unpayable reparations** destroying **German production** and **creditor recovery**; **contagion** to wider trade. |
| **Constraints** | Political triumph of **“make Germany pay”**; limited influence on final treaty text. |
| **Actions** | Resigned from delegation in protest; published ***The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919)** — argued treaty would cause **catastrophe**. |
| **Key events** | Paris Peace Conference (adviser); 1919 book; interwar vindication in **German default**, **hyperinflation**, and **debt crises**. |
| **Changed over time** | From **insider critic** to **public intellectual** defining **economic revisionism** about Versailles. |
| **Why they matter** | His **warnings** **proved broadly correct** in textbook narratives linking **harsh economics** to **Weimar instability** and **revisionist politics** — foundation for later **Keynesian** statecraft. |

### 10. Gustav Stresemann (German Foreign Minister 1923–29)

| **Name** | Gustav Stresemann, Weimar statesman; Chancellor briefly, mainly **Foreign Minister**. |
| **Aims** | **Restore German sovereignty and prestige** by **peaceful revision** of Versailles; **end isolation**; secure **economic stability** (Dawes/Young context). |
| **Fears** | **Renewed Ruhr-style coercion**; **political collapse**; **permanent subordination**. |
| **Constraints** | **Allied control**; **nationalist** domestic right attacking “compliance”; **Reichstag** fragmentation. |
| **Actions** | **Locarno Treaties (1925)** — Rhineland guarantees, arbitration, Franco-German normalisation; **Germany joins League (1926)**; negotiation of **reparations** frameworks. |
| **Key events** | **Ruhr crisis** aftermath; **Locarno**; **League entry**; **Young Plan** era; his **death (1929)** as **Great Depression** begins. |
| **Changed over time** | From **nationalist** in early Weimar to **architect of reconciliation** — **accepted losses** in practice to **win concessions** on occupation and status. |
| **Why they matter** | **Symbol of 1920s détente**; shows **revisionism via diplomacy** as alternative to Hitler’s **violent revision** — his death removes a **key stabiliser**. |

---

### 11. Aristide Briand (French Foreign Minister)

| **Name** | Aristide Briand, French Foreign Minister (multiple terms); Nobel Peace Prize (with Stresemann, 1926). |
| **Aims** | **French security** through **legal guarantees** and **diplomatic embedding** of Germany, not only military coercion. |
| **Fears** | **German resurgence**; **Anglo-American disengagement** from European security. |
| **Constraints** | **French electorate** and military’s **scepticism**; need to pair conciliation with **visible safeguards**. |
| **Actions** | **Locarno** partnership with **Stresemann**; **Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928)** — “outlawry” of war (weak enforcement, high symbolism). |
| **Key events** | Locarno era; Kellogg-Briand; **League** diplomacy; contrast with later **Maginot–entente** hardening. |
| **Changed over time** | From **wartime/reconstruction politics** to **face of French diplomatic compromise** in **late 1920s**; influence **fades** with **Depression** and **German revisionism**. |
| **Why they matter** | Shows **French willingness to compromise** when **Germany** appeared **anchored** in **legal order** — a **temporary** structural condition, not a permanent French shift. |

### 12. Benito Mussolini (Italian dictator 1922–43)

| **Name** | Benito Mussolini, **Duce**; Fascist ruler of Italy. |
| **Aims** | Make Italy **“great, respected, feared”**; **imperial expansion** in Balkans, Africa, Mediterranean; domestic **totalitarian** consolidation. |
| **Fears** | Initially **German power** and **Anschluss** altering balance (blocked **1934**); later subordination to Hitler if Italy appeared weak. |
| **Constraints** | **Weak industrial base**; **military underperformance**; **monarchy** and **Church** as partial checks; **League** and **Great Power** reactions. |
| **Actions** | **Corfu incident (1923)**; **Fiume settlement** context (1924 **Treaty of Rome**); **Abyssinia invasion (1935)** — **League sanctions**, **Hoare-Laval** fiasco; **Rome-Berlin Axis (1936)**; **Anti-Comintern Pact (1937)**; **Pact of Steel (1939)**. |
| **Key events** | March on Rome; **Abyssinia** and **League crisis**; **Spanish Civil War** intervention; **Axis** alignment; **WWII** entry; **fall 1943**. |
| **Changed over time** | From **cautious balancer vs. Germany** to **Hitler’s junior partner** — **opposition to Anschluss** **replaced** by **expansionist alliance**. |
| **Why they matter** | **Fascist aggression** **exposed League weakness**; **Axis** **tightened** the **encirclement logic** of **democracies** and **made wider war more probable**. |

### 13. Adolf Hitler (German dictator 1933–45)

| **Name** | Adolf Hitler, Führer; NSDAP leader; combined **chancellor** and **presidential** powers. |
| **Aims** | **Destroy Versailles**; **unite all German speakers**; secure **Lebensraum** in the **East**; **mass rearmament**; racial empire. |
| **Fears** | Early **two-front war**; **premature** conflict before **rearmament**; **economic** and **elite** constraints until **Gleichschaltung** secured. |
| **Constraints** | **Treaty limits** (evaded); **officer corps** and **industrial** bottlenecks; need to **manage** domestic **opinion** and **foreign** timing. |
| **Actions** | **Withdrawal from League**, **conscription**, **Rhineland remilitarisation (1936)**; **Anschluss (1938)**; **Sudetenland** and **dismemberment of Czechoslovakia (1938–39)**; **Poland invasion (1939)** triggering **WWII**. **Method**: **threats**, **bluff**, **timed conciliation**, exploitation of **Anglo-French** **risk aversion**. |
| **Key events** | **Reichstag Fire**/**Enabling Act**; **Night of Long Knives**; **Olympics 1936**; **Munich**; **Molotov-Ribbentrop** slice of Poland. |
| **Changed over time** | From **chancellor needing coalition** to **war lord of continental empire**; **strategy** shifts from **piecemeal revision** to **total war** in **East** after **Barbarossa**. |
| **Why they matter** | **Primary agency** in **violent breakdown** of interwar order; **ideological** and **strategic** driver of **WWII** and **genocide**. |

### 14. Neville Chamberlain (British PM 1937–40)

| **Name** | Neville Chamberlain, Conservative Prime Minister; Chancellor turned premier. |
| **Aims** | **Avoid war** through **negotiated** adjustments; **buy time** for rearmament; preserve **empire** and **economy**. |
| **Fears** | **Another Somme**; **strategic overstretch**; **domestic** anti-war mood. |
| **Constraints** | **Military unpreparedness** relative to worst-case **continental** commitment; **economic** limits; **public** **war-weariness**; **Dominion** caution. |
| **Actions** | **Munich Agreement (1938)** — **Sudetenland** ceded; **“peace for our time”**; later **Polish guarantee (1939)** after **Prague** showed **appeasement** failed to **satiate** Hitler. |
| **Key events** | **Anschluss** response; **Munich**; **occupation of Prague**; **invasion of Poland**; **declaration of war**; replaced by **Churchill (1940)**. |
| **Changed over time** | From **arch-appeaser** to **war leader** — **same person**, **revised** assessment of **Hitler’s** **intent**. |
| **Why they matter** | **Appeasement debate** — **pragmatic delay** vs **moral/strategic failure** — is **central** to **AS** interpretations of **1930s** and **origins of WWII**. |

### 15. Winston Churchill (British politician; PM from 1940)

| **Name** | Winston Churchill, long-serving minister and MP; Prime Minister 1940–45 (first ministry). |
| **Aims** | **Resist aggression** early; **mobilise** Britain (and Empire) for **total war**; **Anglo-American** **alliance**. |
| **Fears** | **Fascist** domination of **Europe**; **British** **weakness** if **demoralised**. |
| **Constraints** | **Marginalised** in **1930s** cabinets; **financial** dependence on **US**; **imperial** overstretch. |
| **Actions** | **Sustained criticism** of **appeasement**; **Munich** speech — **“total and unmitigated defeat”**; **First Lord**/**PM** war leadership; **1940** succession. |
| **Key events** | **Gallipoli** legacy (earlier); **1930s** warnings; **Battle of Britain**/**Blitz** rhetoric; **Atlantic Charter** context; **Big Three** diplomacy. |
| **Changed over time** | From **backbencher Cassandra** to **national symbol** of **defiance**; post-1945 **election loss** but **historical vindication** on **1930s** judgement. |
| **Why they matter** | **Leading anti-appeasement voice**; **personifies** the **lesson** that **credibility** and **early deterrence** matter — textbook contrast with **Chamberlain**. |

### 16. Joseph Stalin (Soviet dictator from late 1920s)

| **Name** | Joseph Stalin, General Secretary / undisputed Soviet leader after **power struggles**. |
| **Aims** | **Socialism in one country** (doctrine); **state security** against **capitalist encirclement**; **industrial/military** modernisation. |
| **Fears** | **German** attack; **Japanese** threat in **Far East**; **capitalist** **coalitions** against USSR. |
| **Constraints** | **Weak Red Army** post-purge (late 1930s); **ideological** suspicion of **West**; need for **time**. |
| **Actions** | **Collectivisation** and **Five-Year Plans**; **Great Purge**; **Litvinov** **collective security** diplomacy; **USSR joins League (1934)**; **Spanish Civil War** aid; **Nazi-Soviet Pact (1939)** — **secret protocols**, **partition** spheres; **1941** **Barbarossa**. |
| **Key events** | **Purges**; **Munich** (USSR excluded — deep mistrust); **Pact**; **invasion of Poland** from east; **Winter War**; **Great Patriotic War**. |
| **Changed over time** | From **seeking Western** **anti-fascist** alignment (rebuffed at **Munich** and after) to **deal with Hitler** to **gain space/time** and **territory** — **reversed** again **1941** with **Grand Alliance**. |
| **Why they matter** | **Nazi-Soviet Pact** **removed** **two-front** **constraint** on **Germany** in **1939–41** and **reshaped** **Eastern Europe** — **enabling** **WWII**’s **eastern** **opening** in standard narratives. |

### 17. Sun Yat-sen (“Father of the Chinese Nation”)

| **Name** | Sun Yat-sen, revolutionary leader and **KMT** founder; provisional **Republic** president (brief). |
| **Aims** | **Three Principles**: **nationalism** (end foreign domination), **democracy**, **people’s livelihood** (economic justice). |
| **Fears** | **Warlord** fragmentation; **foreign** **imperialism**; **dynastic** restoration. |
| **Constraints** | **Military weakness**; **regional** power bases; **foreign** **treaty** structures. |
| **Actions** | **Founded KMT**; **1911** revolution and **Republic**; **reorganisation** with **Comintern** help; **alliance** with **CCP** (First United Front). **Died 1925**. |
| **Key events** | **Xinhai** legacy; **May Fourth** context; **Whampoa** / **United Front** setup; **successor struggle**. |
| **Changed over time** | From **anti-Qing** **revolutionary** to **state-builder** leaning on **Soviet** **organisational** aid while **ideologically** **ambiguous**. |
| **Why they matter** | **Sets the agenda** for **20th-century Chinese politics** — **national unification** + **modernisation** + **anti-imperialism** — inherited by **KMT** and **CCP** in **rival** forms. |

### 18. Chiang Kai-shek (KMT leader from 1925)

| **Name** | Chiang Kai-shek, **KMT** military leader and **Nationalist** government head. |
| **Aims** | **Unify China**; **modernise** state and army; **destroy CCP**; **resist Japan** (unevenly prioritised vs. **anti-communism**). |
| **Fears** | **Japanese expansion**; **Soviet-backed** communists; **warlord** **defection** and **regionalism**. |
| **Constraints** | **Warlords**; **Japanese invasion**; **corruption** and **fiscal** weakness; **foreign** **concessions**; **CCP** **rural** base. |
| **Actions** | **Northern Expedition**; **Shanghai Massacre (1927)** — **purge** of communists, **end** First United Front; **Encirclement Campaigns**; **Nanking Decade** reforms; **Xi’an Incident (1936)** — **forced** **United Front** vs. Japan. |
| **Key events** | **Nanjing** as capital; **Long March** pursuit; **Marco Polo Bridge** and **war**; **Chongqing** wartime capital; **post-1945** **civil war** defeat. |
| **Changed over time** | Increasingly **authoritarian** and **militarised**; **anti-communist** fixation **shaped** **strategic** **errors** vs. Japan and **peasant** politics. |
| **Why they matter** | **Failure** of **land/social reform** and **repressive** **modernisation** **drove** **peasant** **support** to **Mao** — textbook **causal** link to **CCP victory**. |

### 19. Mao Zedong (CCP leader)

| **Name** | Mao Zedong, **CCP** chairman; strategist of **rural** revolution. |
| **Aims** | **Peasant-based** revolution; **land redistribution**; **CCP** **monopoly** of power; **national liberation** framed in **Marxist-Leninist** terms. |
| **Fears** | **KMT extermination** campaigns; **urban**-centric **Comintern** dogma; **Japanese** occupation disrupting **base areas**. |
| **Constraints** | **Guerrilla** logistics; **factional** struggles; **Japanese** and **KMT** **pressure**. |
| **Actions** | **Jiangxi Soviet**; **Long March (1934–35)**; **Yenan** base; **Rectification Movement** — **party discipline** and **Maoist** supremacy; **mass line** propaganda; **“Eight Points for Attention”** (discipline toward civilians). |
| **Key events** | **Zunyi** (leadership shift narrative); **Yenan**; **Second United Front**; wartime **base expansion**. |
| **Changed over time** | From **marginal** **rural** **strategist** to **undisputed** **CCP** **leader** with **distinct** **Chinese** **Marxism**. |
| **Why they matter** | **Adapted Marxism** to **peasant China**; built **mass support** where **KMT** **urban** **elite** state **failed** — **foundational** for **1949** and **PRC** **legitimacy** narratives. |

### 20. Emperor Hirohito (Japan from 1926)

| **Name** | Hirohito, **Showa** Emperor; **constitutional monarch** under **Meiji** system. |
| **Aims** | Preserve **imperial institution** prestige and **national** **unity** symbolically. |
| **Fears** | **Army** **insubordination** or **coups** **damaging** throne; **loss of face** if seen to **block** **nationalist** **consensus**. |
| **Constraints** | **Limited** **direct** **political** role by **custom** and **law**; **genro**/court–**military** **politics**. |
| **Actions** | **Did not** **order withdrawal** from **Manchuria** after **incidents** escalated; **appointed** **military-heavy** cabinets; **sanctioned** **war** **escalation** in **practice** through **non-veto** and **appointments**; **1945** **surrender rescript**. |
| **Key events** | **Manchuria** crisis; **February 26** era; **China War**; **Pacific War**; **defeat** and **occupation**. |
| **Changed over time** | From **symbol** of **Taishō democracy**’s **survival** to **figure** above **ascendant** **militarists**; **post-1945** **redefinition** as **pacifist** **symbol** under **SCAP**. |
| **Why they matter** | **Personifies** how **weak civilian control** + **sacred monarchy** could **accommodate** **slide** into **military dictatorship** and **aggressive war**. |

### 21. General Tojo (Japanese PM 1941–44)

| **Name** | Hideki Tojo, general and **Prime Minister**; war cabinet leader. |
| **Aims** | **Coercive expansion** — **Greater East Asia** **sphere**; **resource** **autarky**; **alignment** with **Axis** **anti-Western** **order** (admired **Hitler’s** **gambits**). |
| **Fears** | **ABCD encirclement**; **oil/strategic** **strangulation**; **strategic** **vulnerability** if **China** **quagmire** persisted without **southern** **resources**. |
| **Constraints** | **US** **economic** **pressure**; **navy–army** **tensions**; **overextension**. |
| **Actions** | **Decision for war** against **US/UK**; **Pearl Harbor** attack (December 1941); **Pacific** **offensives**; **fall** from power **1944**; **executed** **1948** (**Class-A** **trials**). |
| **Key events** | **Pearl Harbor**; **Southeast Asia** **conquests**; **Midway**/attrition; **defeat** and **occupation**. |
| **Changed over time** | From **army** **hardliner** to **scapegoat** **figure** of **failed** **Pacific** **strategy**. |
| **Why they matter** | Embodies **military** **dictatorship** **leading** Japan into **strategic** **catastrophe**; **Pearl Harbor** **globalises** the war. |

## States and blocs

### Britain

| Field | Content |
|--------|---------|
| **Name** | United Kingdom and **British Empire** — formal and informal **imperial** network. |
| **Aims** | **Global trade**; **naval supremacy**; **imperial defence**; **balance of power** in Europe without **permanent** **large** **continental** **army**. |
| **Fears** | **Peer naval rival** (Germany); **imperial** **overstretch**; **Dominion** **disunity**; **radical** **movements** at home and abroad. |
| **Constraints** | **Two-power standard** **strain**; **public** **anti-war** **pressure**; **fiscal** limits; **dependence** on **sea lines**. |
| **Actions** | **“Splendid isolation”** → **Entente** diplomacy (**French** **settlement**, **Russian** **understandings**); **naval race** **management**; **WWI** **blockade**; **Versailles** **middle** **course**; **interwar** **appeasement** then **guarantees**; **WWII** **survival** **strategy**. |
