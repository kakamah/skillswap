# 06 — Turning Points

## Format
For each: **What changed** | **Why it mattered** | **Before** | **After** | **Why this > surrounding events**

---

### 1. Berlin Conference (1884-85)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | European powers agreed rules for claiming African territory: effective occupation required; free trade on Niger and Congo; free navigation on major rivers. |
| **Before** | Unregulated scramble; overlapping claims; risk of European war over colonial disputes. |
| **After** | Rules-based but massively accelerated colonisation. Within 20 years, ~90% of Africa under European control. |
| **Why it mattered** | Codified European arrogance: no African leaders consulted. Set precedent that European interests override indigenous sovereignty. Created arbitrary borders ignoring ethnic/linguistic realities—consequences persist today. |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | Individual colonial ventures (e.g., Leopold's Congo) were significant, but Berlin Conference systematised the process and made the scramble a collective European project rather than individual adventures. |

---

### 2. Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | Britain signed formal alliance with Japan—first military alliance between a European and Asian power on equal terms. |
| **Before** | Japan isolated after Triple Intervention (1895); Britain pursuing "splendid isolation." |
| **After** | Japan had great-power backing; could confront Russia without fearing Franco-Russian intervention. Britain gained Far Eastern ally to protect interests without deploying own fleet. |
| **Why it mattered** | First time an Asian nation recognised as diplomatic equal by leading European power. Ended British splendid isolation. Enabled Russo-Japanese War. |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | Other diplomatic shifts (e.g., Entente Cordiale 1904) were important, but the Anglo-Japanese Alliance was revolutionary in breaking the European monopoly on great-power status. |

---

### 3. Treaty of Portsmouth (1905)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | Japan defeated Russia—first time an Asian power defeated a European power in modern warfare. Treaty mediated by US (Theodore Roosevelt). |
| **Before** | European assumption of racial/military superiority unchallenged. Russia considered major land power. |
| **After** | Japan = confirmed world power. Russia humiliated → 1905 Revolution. Balance of power in Far East permanently altered. |
| **Why it mattered** | Shattered the myth of European invincibility. Inspired nationalist movements across Asia. Demonstrated that modernisation could create military parity within one generation. |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | The war itself (1904-05) was the military event; Portsmouth was the diplomatic confirmation that changed Japan's international status permanently. |

---

### 4. US Entry into WWI (April 1917)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | USA abandoned neutrality; declared war on Germany. Eventually deployed 2 million troops to Western Front. |
| **Before** | USA neutral since 1914; profiting from war loans and trade. Stalemate on Western Front. |
| **After** | Fresh American troops tipped balance. Germany's spring 1918 offensive failed. Armistice November 1918. USA emerged as world's greatest creditor nation. |
| **Why it mattered** | Ended European self-sufficiency in great-power conflict. Established USA as decisive factor in global politics. Wilson's Fourteen Points shaped peace settlement. |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | Zimmerman Telegram (January 1917) and unrestricted submarine warfare were triggers, but US entry itself was the transformative moment—it determined the war's outcome and America's future role. |

---

### 5. Treaty of Versailles (June 28, 1919)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | Germany forced to accept: War Guilt Clause (Art. 231); £6.6B reparations; loss of 13% territory + all colonies; army limited to 100,000; Rhineland demilitarised; Anschluss forbidden. |
| **Before** | German Empire: great-power status; large military; colonial possessions; industrial dominance of Central Europe. |
| **After** | Weimar Republic burdened with guilt, debt, territorial loss. "November criminals" narrative. Democratic government associated with national humiliation. |
| **Why it mattered** | Created structural instability: Germany strong enough to recover but resentful enough to want revenge. Hitler's entire programme was built on Versailles destruction. |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | Other Paris treaties (Saint-Germain, Trianon, Neuilly, Sèvres) also redrew maps, but Versailles uniquely combined punitive terms with an unstable enforcement mechanism—guaranteeing future revisionism from Europe's most powerful continental state. |

---

### 6. US Senate Rejects Versailles (November 1919 / March 1920)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | Senate refused to ratify Treaty of Versailles. USA never joined League of Nations. Anglo-American guarantee to France collapsed. |
| **Before** | Wilson had personally shaped the peace settlement; League was his creation; Anglo-American guarantee promised to protect France. |
| **After** | USA returned to isolationism. League lost world's strongest economy and navy. France left without promised security guarantee. |
| **Why it mattered** | Crippled League from birth. France, feeling abandoned, pursued aggressive unilateral security (→ Ruhr occupation). International system lacked the power to enforce its own rules. |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | Wilson's stroke, Lodge's reservations, and Republican opposition were all important, but the rejection itself was the turning point—it defined the interwar international system as one where the strongest power was absent. |

---

### 7. Dawes Plan (1924)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | Reparations restructured: annual payments scaled to Germany's capacity; 800M gold marks initial loan; French troops to withdraw from Ruhr. |
| **Before** | Ruhr occupation; hyperinflation; Franco-German hostility at peak; Weimar near collapse. |
| **After** | Stability returned; American loans flowed; "Golden Age" of Weimar (1924-29); cultural and economic recovery. |
| **Why it mattered** | Solved immediate crisis but created fatal dependency on American capital. When US loans stopped (1929), entire structure collapsed. |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | Stresemann's currency reform (Rentenmark, November 1923) stabilised domestically, but Dawes provided the international financial framework. Its weakness—dependency on US loans—was the hidden flaw that made the 1929 Crash so devastating for Europe. |

---

### 8. Locarno Conference (October 1925)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | Germany voluntarily accepted western borders (France, Belgium) as permanent. Rhineland demilitarisation confirmed. Arbitration treaties with Poland and Czechoslovakia (but NOT border guarantees). |
| **Before** | Hostility and distrust; Ruhr occupation fresh memory; Germany a pariah state. |
| **After** | "Spirit of Locarno": reconciliation; Germany admitted to League (1926); Nobel Peace Prizes for Stresemann, Briand, Chamberlain. |
| **Why it mattered** | Represented genuine hope for peace—but contained a critical omission: eastern borders not guaranteed. This told Poland and Czechoslovakia they were expendable, and told Germany its eastern borders were negotiable. |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928) renounced war but had no enforcement. Locarno at least had specific commitments—but the east/west asymmetry was its fatal weakness. |

---

### 9. Wall Street Crash (October 1929)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | US stock market collapsed. American banks recalled European loans. Global credit froze. |
| **Before** | "Roaring Twenties"; Dawes/Young Plans functioning; apparent international stability; "Spirit of Locarno." |
| **After** | Great Depression: global unemployment, trade collapse (65% fall), political extremism. End of Locarno spirit. Rise of Hitler and Japanese militarism. |
| **Why it mattered** | Destroyed the economic foundations on which interwar peace depended. Every element of 1920s stability—reparations, loans, trade, employment—collapsed simultaneously. |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | **Most important single cause of 1930s collapse.** Other events (Manchuria, Hitler's rise) were consequences. The Crash was the catalyst that turned structural weaknesses into active crises across every major power simultaneously. |

---

### 10. Japan Invades Manchuria (September 1931)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | Kwantung Army staged Mukden Incident; invaded Manchuria without government approval. Created puppet state of Manchukuo (1932). |
| **Before** | Japan was League member; Washington treaties in force; collective security theoretically operative. |
| **After** | Lytton Commission condemned Japan (but took a year). Japan left League (March 1933). No sanctions imposed. |
| **Why it mattered** | First major test of collective security—and it failed completely. Demonstrated that the League could not restrain a determined great power. Set precedent for Abyssinia and Rhineland. |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | The Mukden Incident itself was a fabrication, but the invasion's significance lay in the international response (or lack thereof). It proved collective security was a bluff. |

---

### 11. Hitler Becomes Chancellor (January 30, 1933)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | Hitler appointed Chancellor through legal constitutional process (invited by Hindenburg; von Papen's miscalculation). |
| **Before** | Weimar democracy; Stresemann's reconciliation legacy; Germany reintegrating into international system. |
| **After** | Rapid dismantling of democracy (Reichstag Fire → Enabling Act → one-party state). Versailles destruction becomes state policy. |
| **Why it mattered** | Transformed German resentment from passive grievance into active, aggressive revisionism backed by the full power of a major industrial state. |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | The Reichstag Fire (Feb 1933) and Enabling Act (March 1933) were the mechanisms, but Hitler's appointment was the irreversible moment—once in power, he used legal and illegal means to ensure he could never be removed. |

---

### 12. Rhineland Remilitarisation (March 7, 1936)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | German troops marched into demilitarised Rhineland, violating both Versailles and Locarno. |
| **Before** | Versailles demilitarised zone intact; French military superiority still decisive; German rearmament incomplete. |
| **After** | Germany could now defend its western border; French alliance system with Eastern Europe undermined (France could no longer threaten to march into Germany). |
| **Why it mattered** | **Last realistic chance to stop Hitler cheaply.** Hitler later admitted: "If the French had marched, we would have had to withdraw with our tails between our legs." German forces had orders to retreat if opposed. 22,000 German troops vs 250,000+ French. |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | Conscription announcement (1935) and Anglo-German Naval Agreement (1935) were violations too, but the Rhineland was strategically decisive—it changed the military balance and made all subsequent aggression possible. |

---

### 13. Munich Conference (September 29-30, 1938)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | Britain, France, Germany, Italy agreed to transfer Sudetenland to Germany. Czechoslovakia not invited to its own dismemberment. |
| **Before** | Czechoslovakia intact: 35 divisions, fortified border, alliances with France and USSR, Škoda arms works. |
| **After** | Sudetenland lost; border fortifications gone; Czechoslovakia defenceless. Chamberlain declared "peace for our time." |
| **Why it mattered** | Appeasement's high point and the beginning of its discrediting. When Hitler seized rest of Czechoslovakia (March 1939), Munich's promise was exposed as worthless. |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | Anschluss (March 1938) was also significant, but Munich was the deliberate, negotiated sacrifice of a democratic ally—morally and strategically more damaging. |

---

### 14. Nazi-Soviet Pact (August 23, 1939)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | Hitler and Stalin signed non-aggression pact with secret protocol dividing Poland and Eastern Europe into spheres of influence. |
| **Before** | Nazi Germany and Soviet Union = ideological enemies; possibility of Anglo-Franco-Soviet alliance against Hitler. |
| **After** | Poland indefensible (surrounded east and west). Hitler's two-front war problem solved. Stalin gained time and territory. |
| **Why it mattered** | Removed the last obstacle to German invasion of Poland. Made WWII inevitable. Shocked the world—communists and fascists cooperating. |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | British guarantee to Poland (March 1939) showed intent to resist, but without Soviet cooperation, the guarantee was militarily meaningless. The Pact made the guarantee unenforceable. |

---

### 15. Long March (October 1934 – October 1935)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | CCP broke out of KMT encirclement; retreated ~6,000 miles from Jiangxi to Yan'an (Shaanxi). ~90% casualties (86,000 started; ~8,000 survived by some estimates). Mao emerged as undisputed leader (Zunyi Conference, January 1935). |
| **Before** | CCP nearly destroyed; penned into shrinking base area; KMT Fifth Encirclement Campaign succeeding. |
| **After** | CCP survived in remote Yan'an base; Mao consolidated leadership; Long March became founding myth of CCP. |
| **Why it mattered** | Without Long March: no CCP survival, no Mao leadership, no communist China. Military defeat transformed into propaganda legend that defined party identity. |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | The Encirclement Campaigns were the threat; Yan'an was the destination; but the March itself—and especially the Zunyi Conference—was the moment that determined both the CCP's survival and its future character under Mao. |

---

### 16. Xi'an Incident (December 1936)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | Zhang Xueliang (Manchurian warlord) kidnapped Chiang Kai-shek; demanded end to civil war and united front against Japan. CCP (Zhou Enlai) negotiated Chiang's release. |
| **Before** | Chiang's policy: "First internal pacification, then external resistance." KMT focused on destroying CCP, not fighting Japan. |
| **After** | Second United Front formed (nominally). KMT and CCP cooperated against Japan (though cooperation was limited and distrustful). |
| **Why it mattered** | Gave CCP breathing space at critical moment. United front allowed CCP to rebuild, recruit, and gain patriotic legitimacy as anti-Japanese resistance fighters. |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | The Second United Front was the outcome, but the Xi'an Incident was the dramatic rupture that forced Chiang to change strategy—without Zhang's kidnapping, Chiang would likely have continued the extermination campaigns. |

---

### 17. Pearl Harbor (December 7, 1941)

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **What changed** | Japan attacked US Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor. USA declared war on Japan (December 8); Germany declared war on USA (December 11). |
| **Before** | US isolationism dominant; Neutrality Acts; Lend-Lease was maximum extent of involvement. |
| **After** | USA fully committed to global war. American industrial capacity mobilised. War became truly global (Pacific + European + African theatres). |
| **Why it mattered** | Ended American isolationism permanently. Transformed a European/Asian war into a genuinely world war. US industrial power made Allied victory inevitable (eventually). |
| **Why this > surrounding events** | The US oil embargo (July 1941) created the strategic pressure, and Lend-Lease (March 1941) showed sympathy, but Pearl Harbor was the psychological and political rupture that made full mobilisation possible—Roosevelt could not have achieved a declaration of war without it. |
