# Cambridge AS Level International History 1870–1945 — Master Timeline

Strict chronological order. Each entry: **Date/Year** | **Event** | **Description** | **Key actors** | **Immediate consequence** | **Why it matters** | **Linked topic**

---

## Pre-1870 context

| **1776** | *Wealth of Nations* | Adam Smith challenges mercantilist assumptions; argues for freer markets and limited state direction of trade. | Adam Smith; British political economy | Intellectual pressure against crude mercantilist rationales for empire. | Provides later ideological backdrop to debates on whether empire paid economically. | Ch1 — ideas behind imperialism |
| **1823** | Monroe Doctrine | USA warns European powers against further colonisation or intervention in the Americas. | USA; European empires | Asserted US primacy in Western Hemisphere affairs. | Long-run framework for US regional hegemony and later interventionism (Roosevelt Corollary). | Ch1 — US rise |
| **1830–47** | French conquest of Algeria | France consolidates control in North Africa after initial invasion. | France; Algerian resistance | Major French settler/colonial stake in Mediterranean Africa. | Algeria becomes core French imperial possession and source of nationalist grievance. | Ch1 — scramble precursors |
| **1839–42** | First Opium War | Britain forces open Chinese ports after Qing attempt to suppress opium trade. | Britain; Qing China | Treaty system begins; Hong Kong ceded; extraterritoriality and treaty ports expand. | Starts “century of humiliation” narrative; integrates China into coercive global trade. | Ch4 — Qing weakness |
| **1853–54** | Perry expedition (“opening” Japan) | US naval pressure ends Japanese sakoku isolation; unequal treaties follow. | USA; Tokugawa Japan | Accelerated crisis of shogunate; foreign concessions. | Catalyst for Meiji state-building and defensive modernisation. | Ch4 — Japan modernises |
| **1856** | Second Opium War | Anglo-French campaign deepens Western leverage over China. | Britain; France; Qing China | Further port openings; legalisation/ expansion of opium trade (Tientsin framework, 1860). | Weakens central Qing authority; intensifies foreign economic penetration. | Ch4 — imperialism in China |
| **1859–69** | Suez Canal | Canal cuts Europe–Asia sea routes; Egypt strategic/financial weight soars. | French company; Egypt; European investors | Massive trade/strategic gains for maritime powers. | British interest in controlling Egypt/Suez becomes acute → 1882 occupation. | Ch1 — strategic imperialism |
| **1861–65** | American Civil War | Union–Confederate conflict dominates US politics and resources. | USA (Union/Confederacy) | Temporary brake on large-scale overseas expansion. | Post-war industrial growth and navy revival later underpin US imperial turn. | Ch1 — US power |
| **1867** | Alaska Purchase | USA buys Alaska from Russia. | USA; Russia | Ends Russian North American presence; US gains Arctic/Pacific assets. | Symbol of continental consolidation before overseas empire. | Ch1 — US expansion |
| **1868** | Meiji Restoration | Emperor-centred regime replaces Tokugawa shogunate; modernising elite takes charge. | Meiji oligarchs; imperial court | Rapid state-led reform: army, industry, education, legal codes. | Japan becomes the key non-Western moderniser challenging regional order by 1890s. | Ch4 — Japan as world power |

## 1870s

| **1870–71** | Franco-Prussian War | Prussia defeats France; German unification completed excluding Austria. | Bismarck; Prussia/Germany; France | French defeat, Alsace-Lorraine annexed (Versailles treaty 1871); Third Republic. | Creates enduring Franco-German antagonism; Germany becomes dominant continental military-industrial state. | Ch1 — European power shift |
| **1871** | German unification | New German Empire proclaimed; Bismarck prioritises security over hasty colonialism initially. | Bismarck; German elites | Central European great power with strong army and growing economy. | Germany’s late entry still reshapes global imperial competition by 1880s. | Ch1 — Germany |
| **1873–96** | Long Depression | Global deflationary slump; agricultural and industrial distress. | Industrial/financial centres worldwide | Intensified hunt for markets, tariffs, and colonial outlets. | Helps explain sharper imperial rivalry and protectionism before 1914. | Ch1 — economic drivers |

## 1880s

| **1881** | Germany begins African colonisation | Bismarck yields to domestic pressure; acquisitions accelerate. | Bismarck; German industrialists | Germany joins scramble despite Bismarck’s earlier caution. | Adds a third major colonial competitor in Africa alongside Britain/France. | Ch1 — scramble for Africa |
| **1882** | British occupation of Egypt | Britain intervenes to secure financial/strategic control linked to Suez. | Britain; Egypt; France (interests) | Anglo-Egyptian administration; intensified European rivalry in Nile basin. | Triggers wider scramble; embeds Britain in Middle East/North Africa. | Ch1 — Suez/Egypt |
| **1884–85** | Berlin Conference | European powers set ground rules for African claims. | European states (esp. Bismarck’s diplomacy) | “Effective occupation”; mapped partition dynamics; slavery abolition on paper. | Formalises competitive imperialism; accelerates territorial seizures. | Ch1 — Africa partition |
| **1885** | German African empire consolidated | Kamerun, German East Africa, German South-West Africa, Togoland under German rule. | Germany | Colonial deficits; limited economic return vs cost. | Shows political prestige often outweighed profit in New Imperialism. | Ch1 — German colonies |
| **1888** | Wilhelm II becomes Kaiser | New emperor ends Bismarck era; assertive Weltpolitik follows. | Wilhelm II; German elites | Tirpitz naval programme; confrontational diplomacy. | Central driver of Anglo-German naval race and pre-1914 tension. | Ch1 — Weltpolitik |
| **1889** | Naval Defence Act (UK) | Two-power standard adopted for Royal Navy. | British government | Naval expansion benchmarked against next two fleets. | Locks Britain into arms competition when Germany challenges at sea. | Ch1 — naval arms race |
| **1889** | Meiji Constitution | Constitutional order with elected Diet; emperor remains sovereign. | Meiji oligarchy; Japanese political parties | Parliamentary politics within authoritarian modernising state. | Provides façade of constitutionalism while elite/military power endures. | Ch4 — Japanese state |

## 1890s

| **1890** | US Census: frontier “closed” | Statistical claim that unsettled contiguous land is exhausted. | USA | Psychological/economic push to look outward. | Feeds expansionist ideology (Turner) and overseas markets logic. | Ch1 — US imperial turn |
| **1893** | Turner’s “Frontier Thesis” | Historian argues frontier experience forged American democracy/character. | F. J. Turner | Intellectual rationalisation for continued national vigour via expansion. | Influences how Americans understood overseas empire as continuity. | Ch1 — US ideology |
| **1893** | US economic crisis | Banking collapse and depression. | USA | Unemployment; demand for foreign markets. | Strengthens political economy case for imperial/market expansion. | Ch1 — US drivers |
| **1894–95** | First Sino-Japanese War | Japan defeats Qing forces; gains treaty advantages. | Japan; Qing China | Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895): Taiwan, heavy indemnity, Korean influence. | First major proof Japan can reorder East Asia against China. | Ch4 — Sino-Japanese rivalry |
| **1895** | Triple Intervention | Russia, France, Germany force Japan to retrocede Liaodong/Port Arthur gains. | Russia; France; Germany; Japan | Temporary check on Japan; increased foreign leverage in China. | Fuels Japanese resentment and long-term naval/army build-up. | Ch4 — great-power China |
| **1895–96** | Jameson Raid | Failed raid from British South Africa into Transvaal. | Rhodes-linked forces; Transvaal; Britain | Kaiser’s Kruger Telegram congratulates Boers; Anglo-German row. | Sharpens imperial friction and Boer War prelude. | Ch1 — South Africa |
| **1898** | Fashoda Incident | Anglo-French confrontation in Sudan. | Britain; France | Diplomatic compromise recognising spheres (Egypt/Sudan vs Morocco). | Removes worst Anglo-French friction → enables later Entente. | Ch1 — imperial diplomacy |
| **1898** | Spanish-American War | US intervention after *Maine* and Cuban crisis. | USA; Spain | Treaty of Paris: Philippines, Puerto Rico, Guam; US protectorate logic for Cuba. | USA becomes overseas imperial power with Pacific stake. | Ch1 — US empire |
| **1898** | Annexation of Hawaii | US absorbs islands for naval/trade position. | USA; Hawaiian elite | Pacific stepping-stone consolidated. | Supports US Far East strategy (later Philippines–China links). | Ch1 — Pacific empire |
| **1898–1901** | Boxer Rebellion | Anti-foreign uprising; international expedition. | Qing court; Boxers; Eight-Nation alliance | Beijing occupied; Boxer Protocol; huge indemnity. | Deepens Chinese nationalist anger; shows joint imperial coercion. | Ch4 — China under pressure |
| **1899** | Open Door notes (USA) | US pushes equal commercial access in China. | USA; other powers | Diplomatic framework favouring open trade spheres. | Formalises competitive imperialism without Chinese consent. | Ch4 — China |
| **1899–1902** | Second Boer War | Britain fights Boer republics; brutal guerrilla phase. | Britain; Boers | British victory at high cost; concentration camps scandal. | Exposes limits of British military prestige; domestic/international criticism. | Ch1 — British Empire stress |

## 1900s

| **1900** | Scramble for Africa largely complete | ~90% of Africa under formal European control. | European empires | Intra-imperial rivalry shifts to diplomacy, arms, and “zones of influence.” | Sets colonial map that Versailles partially reshuffles via mandates. | Ch1 — Africa |
| **1901** | Platt Amendment | US legal framework for Cuban “independence.” | USA; Cuba | US oversight of Cuban external/financial affairs. | Template for informal empire in Caribbean. | Ch1 — US informal empire |
| **1902** | Anglo-Japanese Alliance | Mutual recognition and Far East security cooperation. | Britain; Japan | Deters Russian expansionism; elevates Japan diplomatically. | Shapes Russo-Japanese War diplomacy and regional balance. | Ch4 — Anglo-Japanese relations |
| **1902** | Hobson’s *Imperialism* | Economic critique: empire as sectional interest, not national gain. | J. A. Hobson | Influences socialist/liberal anti-imperial thought. | Foundational for later Leninist theories and debate on causes of imperialism. | Ch1 — critiques of empire |
| **1903** | Panama Canal authorised | US-backed separation from Colombia; canal zone secured. | USA; Panama; Colombia | Canal opens 1914; strategic shortcut. | Transforms US two-ocean power projection. | Ch1 — US strategic reach |
| **1904** | Entente Cordiale | Britain–France settle colonial disputes (e.g. Egypt vs Morocco). | Britain; France | Diplomatic alignment short of alliance; alarms Germany. | Key step toward Triple Entente vs Central Powers. | Ch1 — alliances |
| **1904** | Roosevelt Corollary | US claims right to intervene in Americas to prevent European intervention. | Theodore Roosevelt; USA | Justifies repeated US interventions. | Extends Monroe Doctrine into informal imperial policing. | Ch1 — US hemisphere |
| **1904–05** | Russo-Japanese War | Japan defeats Russia on land/sea (Tsushima). | Japan; Russia | Treaty of Portsmouth; Russian retreat in Manchuria/Korea sphere. | First major modern victory of Asian power over European great power. | Ch4 — Japan’s great-power status |
| **1905** | First Moroccan Crisis | Kaiser’s Tangier speech challenges French influence. | Germany; France; Britain (backing France) | Conference diplomacy; Germany isolated. | Strengthens Entente; shows German “world policy” risks. | Ch1 — pre-war crises |
| **1906** | Algeciras Conference | Great powers adjudicate Morocco; France gains. | Germany; France; Britain; others | German diplomatic setback. | Deepens German sense of encirclement. | Ch1 — diplomacy of tension |
| **1906** | German naval expansion | Tirpitz programme accelerates. | Germany; Britain | Anglo-German naval arms race intensifies. | Structural driver of hostility before 1914. | Ch1 — naval race |

## 1910s

| **1919 (28 Jun)** | Treaty of Versailles | Punitive terms on Germany: guilt clause, limits, reparations, territorial losses. | Allies; Germany | German signature under protest; colonies mandates. | Core grievance engine for Hitler’s rise and instability. | Ch2 — Versailles |

| **1919 (Mar)** | Comintern founded | Moscow organises international communist parties. | Lenin; Bolsheviks | Revolutionary export strategy. | Feeds Red Scare; polarises domestic politics in West. | Ch2 — ideological conflict |
