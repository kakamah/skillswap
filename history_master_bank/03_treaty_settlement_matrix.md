# 03 Treaty / Settlement Matrix

Source basis: uploaded markdown extraction of Cambridge International AS Level History: International History, 1870-1945. Page references are only included where the extraction made them reasonably clear. Any OCR damage, internal inconsistency, or weakly supported detail is flagged rather than normalised.


## Treaty of Berlin (1885)
- **Date:** 1885
- **Parties involved:** European powers at Berlin
- **Main terms:** Notification of occupation; effective occupation principle; disputes to be resolved by negotiation; free navigation in Congo and Niger areas.
- **Territorial terms:** No final map of all Africa, but rules for claiming territory.
- **Military terms:** None central.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Open trade/navigation language in parts of central Africa.
- **Who benefited:** European claimants, especially late imperial competitors.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** African peoples excluded from decision-making.
- **Why it mattered later:** Accelerated the scramble by setting rules for it.
- **What students often confuse:** The conference did not peacefully end the scramble; it helped structure and speed it.

## Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895)
- **Date:** 1895
- **Parties involved:** Japan, China
- **Main terms:** Chinese defeat recognised; Korea's status altered; Japan gained territory and influence.
- **Territorial terms:** Formosa / Taiwan and other gains for Japan; Port Arthur issue later linked to Triple Intervention in the text.
- **Military terms:** Outcome reflected Japanese military superiority.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Strengthened Japan's regional leverage.
- **Who benefited:** Japan.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** China; later Russia, France, and Germany pressured revisions through Triple Intervention.
- **Why it mattered later:** Triggered the diplomatic humiliation that fed Japanese anger before 1904-05.
- **What students often confuse:** The treaty itself versus the later Triple Intervention.

## Treaty of Portsmouth (1905)
- **Date:** 1905
- **Parties involved:** Japan, Russia; mediated by the USA
- **Main terms:** Ended the Russo-Japanese War and recognised Japanese gains.
- **Territorial terms:** Russian position in Manchuria weakened; Japanese rights in Korea recognised in the book's account.
- **Military terms:** End of hostilities after decisive Japanese victories.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** No major punitive reparations framework highlighted in the text.
- **Who benefited:** Japan diplomatically; USA as mediator.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Russia after defeat; some in Japan thought gains were too limited.
- **Why it mattered later:** Confirmed Japan as a great power and Roosevelt / US diplomatic importance.
- **What students often confuse:** Portsmouth settled a war; it was not a broader Pacific settlement like Washington.

## Entente Cordiale (1904)
- **Date:** 1904
- **Parties involved:** Britain, France
- **Main terms:** Settlement of imperial disputes and improved diplomatic relations.
- **Territorial terms:** Included understanding over Morocco / Egypt spheres.
- **Military terms:** Not a formal military alliance in the book's initial presentation.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Reduced colonial friction.
- **Who benefited:** Britain and France.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Germany, indirectly, once it failed to split them.
- **Why it mattered later:** Central background to the Moroccan crises and pre-1914 alignment.
- **What students often confuse:** It was not identical to the later wartime alliance relationship.

## Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902)
- **Date:** 1902
- **Parties involved:** Britain, Japan
- **Main terms:** Mutual support rules if attacked by multiple powers; recognition of interests in East Asia.
- **Territorial terms:** Recognition of Japanese interest in Korea in the text.
- **Military terms:** Helped deter Russian pressure; linked naval strategy.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Diplomatic recognition of Japan as a serious power.
- **Who benefited:** Both Britain and Japan.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Russia, indirectly.
- **Why it mattered later:** Encouraged Japanese confidence and shaped East Asian diplomacy.
- **What students often confuse:** It was not an open-ended blank cheque for all Japanese expansion.

## Treaty of Paris (1898)
- **Date:** 1898
- **Parties involved:** USA, Spain
- **Main terms:** Ended the Spanish-American War and transferred Spain's possessions.
- **Territorial terms:** Philippines, Puerto Rico, and Guam went to the USA; Cuba came under US influence.
- **Military terms:** Reflected US naval victory.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Marked US imperial expansion.
- **Who benefited:** USA.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Spain; anti-imperialists in the USA also criticised the outcome.
- **Why it mattered later:** Key moment in the USA's rise as a world power.
- **What students often confuse:** Not the same "Paris" as the 1919 peace conference.

## Platt Amendment / Cuban treaty (1901 / 1903)
- **Date:** 1901 / 1903
- **Parties involved:** USA, Cuba
- **Main terms:** The USA gained wide influence over Cuban affairs and bases including Guantanamo.
- **Territorial terms:** US base rights.
- **Military terms:** Enabled intervention and strategic control.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Restricted Cuban sovereignty.
- **Who benefited:** USA.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Cuban nationalists.
- **Why it mattered later:** Shows US imperialism through control rather than annexation alone.
- **What students often confuse:** Cuba was nominally independent, but not fully sovereign in practice.

## Treaty of Versailles (1919)
- **Date:** 28 June 1919
- **Parties involved:** Germany, Allied powers
- **Main terms:** War guilt, reparations framework, territorial losses, military restrictions, League Covenant.
- **Territorial terms:** Alsace-Lorraine returned to France; Polish Corridor created; Saar under League for 15 years; Rhineland demilitarised; colonies became mandates.
- **Military terms:** Army limited to 100,000; no tanks, military aircraft, or submarines; limited navy.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Article 231 underpinned reparations; Anschluss banned; Germany excluded from negotiation.
- **Who benefited:** France gained security clauses; Poland gained access; Britain gained less direct than France.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Germany most obviously; also some French critics thought it too soft, while others thought it too harsh economically.
- **Why it mattered later:** Central grievance for Weimar revisionism and Nazi propaganda.
- **What students often confuse:** Article 231 mattered because of reparations liability, not because it alone explained all later German politics.

## Treaty of Saint-Germain (1919)
- **Date:** 1919
- **Parties involved:** Austria, Allied powers
- **Main terms:** Recognised the break-up of the Habsburg Empire and reduced Austria to a much smaller state.
- **Territorial terms:** Bohemia and Moravia went to Czechoslovakia; Dalmatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina went to Yugoslavia; Bukovina to Romania; Galicia to Poland; Trentino, Istria, Trieste, and parts of South Tyrol to Italy.
- **Military terms:** Austria's armed forces limited.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Austria reduced to a much smaller state.
- **Who benefited:** Successor states.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Austrian nationalists; those who favoured Anschluss.
- **Why it mattered later:** Left Austria weak and vulnerable before 1938.
- **What students often confuse:** Saint-Germain is the Austrian treaty, not Trianon.

## Treaty of Neuilly (1919)
- **Date:** 1919
- **Parties involved:** Bulgaria, Allied powers
- **Main terms:** Territorial losses, reparations, and military limits were imposed on Bulgaria.
- **Territorial terms:** Bulgaria lost territory to Greece, Yugoslavia, and Romania.
- **Military terms:** Army capped at 20,000.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Reparations of over $400 million were imposed in the chapter account.
- **Who benefited:** Bulgaria's neighbours.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Bulgaria.
- **Why it mattered later:** One more example of dissatisfaction beyond Germany.
- **What students often confuse:** Bulgaria's treaty was Neuilly, not Trianon or Saint-Germain.

## Treaty of Trianon (1920)
- **Date:** 1920
- **Parties involved:** Hungary, Allied powers
- **Main terms:** Massive territorial losses formalised the break-up of historic Hungary.
- **Territorial terms:** Slovakia and Ruthenia went to Czechoslovakia; Transylvania to Romania; Croatia and Slovenia to Yugoslavia.
- **Military terms:** Hungarian military restricted.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Left many ethnic Hungarians outside Hungary.
- **Who benefited:** Successor states such as Romania and Czechoslovakia.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Hungary.
- **Why it mattered later:** A key source of revisionist bitterness in central Europe.
- **What students often confuse:** Trianon was the Hungarian treaty.

## Treaty of Sevres (1920)
- **Date:** 1920
- **Parties involved:** Ottoman Empire, Allied powers
- **Main terms:** Severe territorial reduction, mandates, and permanent opening of the Dardanelles.
- **Territorial terms:** Large losses in the Arab lands and elsewhere.
- **Military terms:** Ottoman weakness built into the settlement.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Deeply unpopular in Turkey.
- **Who benefited:** Allied powers and mandate holders.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Turkish nationalists.
- **Why it mattered later:** Prompted the successful Kemalist challenge.
- **What students often confuse:** Sevres did not last; it was replaced by Lausanne.

## Treaty of Lausanne (1923)
- **Date:** 1923
- **Parties involved:** Turkey, Allied powers
- **Main terms:** Recognised the Republic of Turkey and revised Sevres.
- **Territorial terms:** More favourable to Turkey than Sevres.
- **Military terms:** Settled the conflict generated by Turkish resistance.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Turkish reparations burden ended in the chapter account.
- **Who benefited:** Turkey.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** States and interests that expected Sevres to hold.
- **Why it mattered later:** Proof that force plus diplomacy could revise the post-war settlement.
- **What students often confuse:** Lausanne belongs to the eastern settlement crisis, not the Locarno era.

## Washington settlement (1921-22)
- **Date:** 1921-22
- **Parties involved:** USA, Britain, Japan, France, Italy, China, others
- **Main terms:** Five-Power naval ratios; Four-Power Pacific understanding; Nine-Power support for China and the Open Door.
- **Territorial terms:** Japan accepted limits and some restraint in China.
- **Military terms:** Battleship ratios set at 5:5:3 for USA, Britain, Japan in the chapter's treatment.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Sought stability without a coercive enforcement mechanism.
- **Who benefited:** USA and Britain strategically; Japan accepted limits but remained recognised.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Japanese nationalists who resented inferiority.
- **Why it mattered later:** Mid-1920s Pacific calm rested on this system until Japan abandoned restraint.
- **What students often confuse:** Washington was a package of treaties, not a single document.

## Treaty of Rapallo (1922)
- **Date:** 16 April 1922
- **Parties involved:** Germany, USSR
- **Main terms:** Diplomatic and economic normalisation; renunciation of claims.
- **Territorial terms:** No major frontier redraw.
- **Military terms:** In practice associated with secret military cooperation that helped Germany evade Versailles limits.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Both outcast states gained room to manoeuvre.
- **Who benefited:** Germany and USSR.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** France and states fearing German-Soviet cooperation.
- **Why it mattered later:** Showed the excluded powers could bypass the Paris system.
- **What students often confuse:** Rapallo is not the same as the 1926 Treaty of Berlin, though they are linked.

## Dawes Plan (1924)
- **Date:** 1924
- **Parties involved:** Germany, USA, France, Britain, reparations framework powers
- **Main terms:** Reparations rescheduled according to Germany's capacity; US loan support; Ruhr evacuation.
- **Territorial terms:** None.
- **Military terms:** None direct.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Stabilised German currency and revived payments.
- **Who benefited:** Germany in the short term; France got resumed payments; USA gained financial leverage.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Hardliners who wanted tougher coercion or fuller cancellation.
- **Why it mattered later:** Mid-1920s recovery depended on US finance through Dawes.
- **What students often confuse:** Dawes was not a final settlement; Young followed.

## Locarno Treaties (1925)
- **Date:** 1925
- **Parties involved:** Germany, France, Belgium, Britain, Italy, plus related arbitration agreements
- **Main terms:** Western frontiers guaranteed; arbitration procedures widened.
- **Territorial terms:** Germany accepted western borders.
- **Military terms:** Britain and Italy offered guarantees in the west.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Symbolised reconciliation and Germany's diplomatic rehabilitation.
- **Who benefited:** Stresemann and Briand politically; western Europe more broadly.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Eastern states because equivalent guarantees were weaker or absent.
- **Why it mattered later:** High point of interwar diplomatic optimism.
- **What students often confuse:** Locarno improved western security more than eastern security.

## Treaty of Berlin (1926)
- **Date:** 1926
- **Parties involved:** Germany, USSR
- **Main terms:** Renewed the Rapallo relationship and neutrality understandings.
- **Territorial terms:** None central.
- **Military terms:** Continued the logic of German-Soviet cooperation.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Balanced Germany's League membership with continued eastern options.
- **Who benefited:** Germany and USSR.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** France and states worried by revisionist cooperation.
- **Why it mattered later:** Stresemann's diplomacy was cooperative and revisionist at the same time.
- **What students often confuse:** There are two different "Treaty of Berlin" entries in this course - 1885 and 1926.

## Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928; in force 1929)
- **Date:** 1928 / 1929
- **Parties involved:** USA, France, and many others
- **Main terms:** War renounced as an instrument of policy.
- **Territorial terms:** None.
- **Military terms:** No enforcement machinery.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** High-profile moral-diplomatic statement.
- **Who benefited:** Supporters of legalist peace rhetoric.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Realists who saw it as toothless.
- **Why it mattered later:** A symbol of idealism at the end of the 1920s - and of how little paper promises meant without sanctions.
- **What students often confuse:** The pact did not prevent rearmament or aggression.

## Young Plan (1929)
- **Date:** 1929
- **Parties involved:** Germany, USA, France, Britain, reparations framework powers
- **Main terms:** Total reparations reduced and payments spread over a longer period; some external controls eased.
- **Territorial terms:** None.
- **Military terms:** None direct.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** More realistic than 1921 figures, but still unpopular in Germany.
- **Who benefited:** Germany in relative terms; France accepted compromise.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** German nationalists; anyone seeking either cancellation or strict enforcement.
- **Why it mattered later:** The last major reparations adjustment before the Depression destabilised the whole system.
- **What students often confuse:** Young was a reduction, not a cancellation.

## Stresa Front (1935)
- **Date:** April 1935
- **Parties involved:** Britain, France, Italy
- **Main terms:** Common opposition to treaty revision by force and support for Austrian independence.
- **Territorial terms:** No new settlement.
- **Military terms:** No strong enforcement mechanism.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Diplomatic front against Hitler.
- **Who benefited:** Potentially France most, if it had held.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Germany.
- **Why it mattered later:** Its collapse made later German advances easier.
- **What students often confuse:** Stresa was a declaration/front, not a robust alliance.

## Anglo-German Naval Agreement (1935)
- **Date:** June 1935
- **Parties involved:** Britain, Germany
- **Main terms:** Germany accepted a navy set as a fraction of British naval strength.
- **Territorial terms:** None.
- **Military terms:** Bilateral naval limitation that effectively accepted German naval rearmament.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Britain hoped for controlled revision; France saw betrayal.
- **Who benefited:** Germany diplomatically; Britain believed it gained predictability.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** France and Stresa supporters.
- **Why it mattered later:** It undermined Stresa and encouraged Hitler.
- **What students often confuse:** The source itself contains a percentage inconsistency; manual checking is sensible.

## Franco-Soviet Pact (1935)
- **Date:** 1935
- **Parties involved:** France, USSR
- **Main terms:** Mutual assistance against aggression in Europe.
- **Territorial terms:** None.
- **Military terms:** Defensive understanding.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Attempt to strengthen anti-German containment.
- **Who benefited:** France and USSR in theory.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Germany.
- **Why it mattered later:** Hitler used it as part of his justification over the Rhineland.
- **What students often confuse:** It was not enough on its own to create an effective anti-Hitler bloc.

## Anti-Comintern Pact / Axis agreements (1936-39)
- **Date:** 1936 onward
- **Parties involved:** Germany, Japan, later Italy
- **Main terms:** Anti-Soviet ideological and strategic alignment.
- **Territorial terms:** None directly.
- **Military terms:** Increasing strategic cooperation; later Pact of Steel deepened the Italo-German side.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Signalled bloc formation.
- **Who benefited:** Revisionist states seeking mutual support.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** USSR and anti-fascist powers.
- **Why it mattered later:** Helped turn separate crises into linked fronts.
- **What students often confuse:** Anti-Comintern is not identical to the Pact of Steel, though both belong to the Axis story.

## Tanggu Truce (1933)
- **Date:** 1933
- **Parties involved:** China, Japan
- **Main terms:** Chinese acceptance of a settlement favourable to Japan after Manchuria.
- **Territorial terms:** Left Japan dominant in Manchuria and stronger in north China.
- **Military terms:** Reduced immediate fighting while protecting Japanese gains.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Reflected Chinese weakness.
- **Who benefited:** Japan.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Chinese nationalists.
- **Why it mattered later:** Showed that Manchuria was not a temporary crisis but a strategic shift.
- **What students often confuse:** Tanggu is a China-Japan settlement, not a League agreement.

## Munich Agreement (1938)
- **Date:** 29 September 1938
- **Parties involved:** Germany, Britain, France, Italy; Czechoslovakia excluded
- **Main terms:** Sudetenland transferred to Germany.
- **Territorial terms:** Czechoslovakia lost borderland fortifications and strategic territory.
- **Military terms:** Weakened Czech defence capacity sharply.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Chamberlain presented it as preserving peace.
- **Who benefited:** Germany immediately.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Czechoslovakia, USSR critics, anti-appeasers.
- **Why it mattered later:** Peak appeasement and a major strategic gain for Hitler.
- **What students often confuse:** It did not settle Hitler's ambitions; Prague followed in March 1939.

## Nazi-Soviet Pact (1939)
- **Date:** 24 August 1939
- **Parties involved:** Germany, USSR
- **Main terms:** Public non-aggression; secret spheres of influence in eastern Europe.
- **Territorial terms:** Partition logic for Poland and influence over nearby territories.
- **Military terms:** Removed the immediate threat of a two-front war for Germany.
- **Economic / reparations / political terms:** Also had economic aspects in the wider relationship.
- **Who benefited:** Germany strategically in the short term; USSR gained time and buffer space.
- **Who was dissatisfied:** Poland most directly; Britain and France were strategically weakened.
- **Why it mattered later:** Immediate precondition for the invasion of Poland.
- **What students often confuse:** It was a tactical agreement, not evidence that Nazi and Soviet ideologies had become compatible.
