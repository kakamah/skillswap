## Imperialism and power
- New imperialism mattered less as a simple land-grab than as a sign that industrial power now demanded global reach.
- The deeper problem was not only competition for colonies but competition for status.
- Empire did not just supply raw materials; it supplied prestige and strategic routes.
- The real importance of Egypt was that route security could drive annexation as strongly as trade.
- Berlin solved the problem of European rules in Africa, not the problem of African consent.
- Imperial rivalry increased tension, but the powers often compromised overseas until the European core collapsed in 1914.
- The settlement of Fashoda matters because it shows colonial rivalry was dangerous but still negotiable.
- Triple Intervention mattered because it taught Japan that diplomatic weakness could erase military victory.
- The Boer War is best understood as a British victory that looked strategically expensive.
- The Moroccan crises mattered less for Morocco itself than for what they revealed about distrust inside Europe.
- Japan's rise did not just challenge China; it challenged the assumption that only western states could dominate international politics.
- The USA did not simply abandon isolation; it practised selective expansion long before 1917.

## Peace settlements and the 1920s
- Versailles solved the problem of ending war but not the problem of making the peace accepted.
- The settlement answered French fear better than it answered German consent.
- Article 231 mattered because it gave legal shape to the reparations issue and moral shape to German resentment.
- The real weakness of the Paris system was that it depended on powers that did not agree on how hard it should be enforced.
- US non-membership damaged the League not only materially but psychologically, especially for France.
- The Ruhr episode is best understood as coercion producing the opposite of what it was meant to secure.
- Dawes did not remove tension; it postponed and managed it.
- Locarno looked stronger than it was because western reassurance hid eastern insecurity.
- Kellogg-Briand shows how far diplomatic language had moved ahead of enforcement capacity.
- The League succeeded where consent was available and failed where power was contested.
- Lausanne matters because it proves the post-war order could be revised when force and diplomacy aligned.
- The 1920s were not a straight road to peace; they were a pause built on unstable finance and limited trust.

## 1930s crisis
- The Depression mattered because it weakened defenders of the status quo as well as emboldening its enemies.
- Manchuria is the first clear case in which aggression paid and the system could not reverse it.
- The failure in Manchuria made later disarmament rhetoric sound hollow.
- Hitler's rise mattered because German revisionism now fused with dictatorship and expansionist ideology.
- Abyssinia did not just expose League weakness; it exposed British and French unwillingness to choose principle over expediency.
- The real importance of the Hoare-Laval episode was the destruction of trust in sanctions politics.
- Rhineland changed less territory than Munich, but it changed the strategic balance more decisively.
- Appeasement is best understood as a policy of buying time under pressure, not as simple blindness.
- Appeasement mattered less because it was morally weak than because it repeatedly worsened the next strategic position.
- Spain shows how non-intervention can become one-sided in practice.
- Munich solved the immediate crisis but created a larger strategic one.
- March 1939 mattered because it destroyed the ethnic self-determination defence of Hitler's policy.
- The Nazi-Soviet Pact did not make war inevitable by itself, but it removed the main immediate obstacle to Hitler's timetable.
- Poland in 1939 is best understood as the end-point of accumulated failures, not a sudden isolated rupture.

## China and Japan
- The fall of the Qing dynasty solved the problem of monarchy but not the problem of power.
- Warlordism mattered because it blocked state-building and discredited central authority.
- May Fourth is best understood as an ideological and political turning point, not just a protest episode.
- Nationalism in China split into rival projects rather than producing automatic unity.
- Chiang's greatest strength was military-political control; his greatest weakness was that this did not equal deep legitimacy.
- The Shanghai Purge mattered because it forced the CCP to survive differently rather than disappear.
- The Long March was a defeat in military terms but a victory in organisational survival and later symbolism.
- Japanese aggression in Manchuria did not just damage China; it damaged Japanese parliamentary government.
- The deeper problem in East Asia was the link between Japanese resource insecurity and military autonomy.
- Xi'an matters because it shows anti-Japanese nationalism could redirect domestic power struggles.
- The anti-Japanese war helped the CCP because prolonged conflict exposed KMT limits.
- Pearl Harbor is best understood as a strategic gamble born from sanctions pressure and miscalculation, not as an isolated act of irrationality.
- The war in China did not merely parallel the European crisis; it interacted with and widened it.

## Comparative lines
- Japan and Germany both revised settlements, but Japan first exposed collective security's weakness.
- The League failed in both Manchuria and Abyssinia, but the second failure was even more destructive because Britain and France were directly compromised.
- Stresemann and Hitler both sought revision of Versailles, but by radically different methods and ends.
- Wilsonian principle and power politics coexisted in 1919; the problem is that power politics usually won where they collided.
- The 1920s and 1930s are best understood together: the later collapse grew out of unresolved weaknesses in the earlier "recovery."
