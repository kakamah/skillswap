## Topic 1: Empire and the emergence of world powers, 1870-1919
- **Date span:** 1870-1919 (main chapter coverage pp. 8-66)
- **Core issue:** Why imperialism accelerated after 1870, how it reshaped international relations, and why Japan and the USA emerged as major powers.
- **Big-picture explanation:** Industrialisation, tariffs, strategic competition, nationalism, racism, and technology made empire appear desirable and feasible. At the same time, new powers - Japan and the USA - entered international politics in ways that challenged the older European hierarchy.
- **Must-know events in order:**
  1. Franco-Prussian War and German unification, 1870-71
  2. Long Depression, 1873-96
  3. British occupation of Egypt, 1882
  4. Berlin Conference / Treaty of Berlin, 1884-85
  5. First Sino-Japanese War and Treaty of Shimonoseki, 1894-95
  6. Triple Intervention, 1895
  7. Fashoda, 1898
  8. Spanish-American War / Treaty of Paris, 1898
  9. Second Boer War, 1899-1902
  10. Anglo-Japanese Alliance, 1902
  11. Russo-Japanese War / Portsmouth, 1904-05
  12. First and Second Moroccan Crises, 1905-06 and 1911
  13. USA enters First World War, 1917
  14. Wilson's Fourteen Points, 1918
- **Must-know actors:** Britain, France, Germany, Leopold II, Cecil Rhodes, Japan, Russia, USA, Theodore Roosevelt, Wilson, Qing China, Boers.
- **Must-know treaties / agreements:** Treaty of Berlin (1885), Shimonoseki (1895), Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902), Entente Cordiale (1904), Portsmouth (1905), Treaty of Paris (1898), Platt Amendment / 1903 Cuban treaty.
- **5-10 essential facts:**
  - Imperialism after 1870 was tied to industrial production, market anxiety, and strategic route protection.
  - The book stresses that empire was justified publicly by "civilising" rhetoric but often driven by national and material interest.
  - Berlin 1884-85 did not stop empire; it regulated and accelerated the partition of Africa.
  - Colonial rivalry increased tension, but major powers often compromised rather than fight immediately over colonies.
  - Japan modernised rapidly after Meiji and defeated both China and Russia.
  - The USA moved from continental expansion to overseas empire and naval projection.
  - The Boer War exposed British weakness and international isolation.
  - The Moroccan crises hardened suspicion of Germany before 1914.
- **3-5 factual confusions students might make:**
  - Confusing the 1906 Algeciras conference with the 1911 Agadir crisis.
  - Treating the Entente Cordiale as a full military alliance from the start.
  - Forgetting that Triple Intervention humiliated Japan and mattered for 1904-05.
  - Assuming imperial clashes always caused war rather than sometimes ending in compromise.
  - Mixing Treaty of Paris (1898) with Paris Peace Conference (1919).
- **Why this topic matters analytically:** It explains how global competition widened beyond Europe, why imperialism mattered politically as well as economically, and how Japan and the USA altered the balance of power before 1919.

## Topic 2: The League of Nations and international relations in the 1920s
- **Date span:** 1919-1929 (main chapter coverage pp. 67-123)
- **Core issue:** Whether the post-war settlement and the League created a stable peace, and why improvement in the mid-1920s remained fragile.
- **Big-picture explanation:** The 1919-20 settlements were a compromise between security, revenge, self-determination, and practical politics. The League achieved some success in minor disputes, but major-power self-interest, reparations, US non-membership, and weak enforcement limited the new order from the start.
- **Must-know events in order:**
  1. Paris Peace Conference, 1919
  2. Versailles, Saint-Germain, Neuilly, Trianon, Sevres, 1919-20
  3. US Senate rejection of League membership, 1919
  4. Early League disputes: Vilna, Aaland, Upper Silesia, Albania, 1920-21
  5. Reparations fixed, 1921
  6. Washington Conference, 1921-22
  7. Rapallo, 1922
  8. Ruhr occupation and hyperinflation, 1923
  9. Corfu, 1923
  10. Lausanne, 1923
  11. Dawes Plan, 1924
  12. Locarno, 1925
  13. Germany joins League / Treaty of Berlin, 1926
  14. Kellogg-Briand Pact, 1928
  15. Young Plan, 1929
- **Must-know actors:** Wilson, Clemenceau, Lloyd George, Stresemann, Briand, Mussolini, Turkey under Mustafa Kemal, the USA, Germany, France, Britain, USSR, the League.
- **Must-know treaties / agreements:** Versailles, Saint-Germain, Neuilly, Trianon, Sevres, Lausanne, Washington Five/Four/Nine Power treaties, Rapallo, Dawes Plan, Locarno, Treaty of Berlin (1926), Kellogg-Briand, Young Plan.
- **5-10 essential facts:**
  - Germany expected a settlement closer to the Fourteen Points than the treaty it received.
  - Article 231 mattered because it underpinned reparations.
  - France wanted security; Britain wanted a settlement that also allowed German recovery.
  - US non-membership weakened both the League and French confidence.
  - The Ruhr crisis proved that security policy and economic policy were inseparable.
  - Dawes and Young did not solve reparations permanently; they reduced immediate pressure.
  - Locarno improved western relations but left eastern frontiers less secure.
  - The League worked better where major powers did not have vital interests at stake.
- **3-5 factual confusions students might make:**
  - Treating all post-war dissatisfaction as purely German, when Italy, Hungary, Turkey, and many minorities also had grievances.
  - Confusing the League's successes in small disputes with strength in great-power crises.
  - Assuming US isolationism meant no US importance, despite loans and diplomacy.
  - Treating Kellogg-Briand as enforceable.
  - Forgetting that Turkey successfully overturned Sevres via Lausanne.
- **Why this topic matters analytically:** It shows that the interwar order looked more stable in 1925-29 than it really was because its economic, strategic, and institutional foundations remained weak.

## Topic 3: The League of Nations and international relations in the 1930s
- **Date span:** 1929-1939 (main chapter coverage pp. 124-176)
- **Core issue:** Why the rise of extremism, economic crisis, and weak collective security led to the collapse of peace by 1939.
- **Big-picture explanation:** The Depression radicalised domestic politics and weakened cooperation. Japan, Italy, and Germany tested the international system repeatedly; each failed response made the next challenge easier. Appeasement grew from fear, weakness, and strategic calculation, but it also gave Hitler room to expand.
- **Must-know events in order:**
  1. Wall Street Crash and Depression, 1929 onward
  2. Manchurian Crisis, 1931-33
  3. Hitler becomes chancellor, 1933
  4. Germany leaves League and Disarmament Conference, 1933
  5. Saar plebiscite and rearmament, 1935
  6. Stresa Front, 1935
  7. Anglo-German Naval Agreement, 1935
  8. Abyssinian Crisis, 1935-36
  9. Rhineland remilitarisation, 1936
  10. Spanish Civil War, 1936-39
  11. Anschluss, 1938
  12. Munich Agreement, 1938
  13. Destruction of Czechoslovakia, March 1939
  14. Nazi-Soviet Pact, August 1939
  15. Invasion of Poland and outbreak of war, September 1939
- **Must-know actors:** Hitler, Mussolini, Stalin, Chamberlain, Baldwin, Daladier, Franco, Litvinov, the League, Japan.
- **Must-know treaties / agreements:** Stresa Front, Anglo-German Naval Agreement, Franco-Soviet Pact, Anti-Comintern Pact, Rome-Berlin Axis, Pact of Steel, Munich Agreement, Nazi-Soviet Pact.
- **5-10 essential facts:**
  - The Depression weakened the states that might have enforced the peace.
  - Manchuria was the first decisive proof that the League could not stop a major aggressor.
  - Abyssinia destroyed confidence in sanctions and collective security.
  - Rhineland was a low-risk gamble for Hitler but a huge strategic gain when it succeeded.
  - Spain showed ideological polarisation and the emptiness of non-intervention.
  - Munich did not just transfer territory; it crippled Czechoslovakia strategically and politically.
  - The occupation of the rest of Czechoslovakia mattered because it exposed Hitler as more than a revisionist nationalist.
  - The Nazi-Soviet Pact solved Hitler's immediate strategic problem and isolated Poland.
- **3-5 factual confusions students might make:**
  - Mixing appeasement with pacifism; the book presents appeasement as strategic calculation, not simple passivity.
  - Confusing Stresa with Locarno.
  - Treating Munich as the moment war began rather than a failed attempt to avoid immediate war.
  - Assuming the League "did nothing" in every case; often it investigated or condemned but could not enforce.
  - Confusing German rearmament stages: Saar, conscription, naval deal, Rhineland, Anschluss, Sudetenland, Prague, Poland.
- **Why this topic matters analytically:** It is the clearest test of whether the interwar order failed because it was badly designed, badly defended, or overwhelmed by the interaction of economic crisis and aggressive states.

## Topic 4: China and Japan, 1912-1945
- **Date span:** 1912-1945 (main chapter coverage pp. 177-221)
- **Core issue:** Why China remained politically fragmented and why Japan moved from limited cooperation in the 1920s to military dictatorship and aggressive war in the 1930s-40s.
- **Big-picture explanation:** The fall of the Qing dynasty did not create a stable republic. Warlordism, foreign pressure, and ideological conflict made state-building difficult. The KMT and CCP competed to define China's future while Japan exploited Chinese weakness and then drifted into military rule and wider war.
- **Must-know events in order:**
  1. 1911 Revolution and 1912 Republic
  2. Yuan's dictatorship and death, 1913-16
  3. Warlord era, 1916-28
  4. May Fourth Movement, 1919
  5. Rejection of Japan's racial equality proposal at Paris, 1919
  6. CCP founded, 1921
  7. Washington Conference, 1921-22
  8. First United Front, 1923
  9. Japanese Exclusion Act, 1924
  10. Northern Expedition, 1926-28
  11. Shanghai Purge / White Terror, 1927
  12. Nanking Decade, 1928-37
  13. Mukden / Manchurian Crisis, 1931
  14. May 15 Incident, 1932
  15. Long March, 1934-35
  16. Xi'an Incident, 1936
  17. Full-scale Sino-Japanese War, 1937
  18. Pearl Harbor, 1941
  19. Japanese defeat and withdrawal, 1945
- **Must-know actors:** Sun Yat-sen, Yuan Shih-kai, Chiang Kai-shek, Wang Jingwei, Mao Zedong, Zhou Enlai, Zhang Xueliang, KMT, CCP, Saionji Kinmochi, Shidehara, Inukai Tsuyoshi, Hirohito, Tojo, Japanese army, USA, Comintern.
- **Must-know treaties / agreements:** Twenty-One Demands, First United Front, Tanggu Truce, Second United Front, Washington settlement, Anti-Comintern Pact, US embargo measures.
- **5-10 essential facts:**
  - The republic began weak because military force, finance, and regional power still mattered more than constitutional form.
  - Warlordism damaged peasants, trade, and national authority.
  - May Fourth mattered not only for protest but for ideological change and political mobilisation.
  - Chiang unified China only partially; warlords, factionalism, and Japanese pressure remained.
  - The CCP survived 1927 because it rebuilt in rural areas rather than disappearing.
  - The Long March mattered politically more than militarily because it preserved the CCP and elevated Mao.
  - Japanese disappointment at Paris was about status as well as territory: rejection of racial equality and the Shantung reversal both fed resentment.
  - Washington cooperation calmed tension temporarily, but many Japanese nationalists saw the naval limits as second-rank treatment.
  - Japanese democracy weakened because party corruption, zaibatsu influence, the 1924 Exclusion Act, political violence, and depression discredited civilian government.
  - Japan's seizure of Manchuria exposed civilian weakness and accelerated military dominance.
  - War against Japan damaged the KMT's reputation and gave the CCP space to grow.
- **3-5 factual confusions students might make:**
  - Treating 1912 as the end of China's instability rather than the start of a new phase.
  - Assuming Northern Expedition created a fully centralised state.
  - Confusing Japanese anger at Paris with simple territorial disappointment; the racial equality issue mattered too.
  - Treating Washington as either total Japanese surrender or total success; the book presents a debate over whether Japan was forced or willing to compromise.
  - Treating CCP growth as purely military success against Japan; the book stresses political positioning and KMT weakness too.
  - Confusing Xi'an Incident with the outbreak of full-scale war in 1937.
  - Assuming Pearl Harbor destroyed total US naval capacity; the chapter stresses what survived.
- **Why this topic matters analytically:** It links domestic weakness, nationalism, revolution, foreign aggression, and the collapse of Japanese parliamentary government, showing why East Asia became central to the wider war crisis.
