# Topic map: Cambridge AS Level International History 1870–1945

Course overview: four major topics covering empire and world power to 1919, the League in the 1920s, the road to war in the 1930s, and China and Japan through 1945.

---

## Topic 1: Empire and the emergence of world powers 1870–1919

### Core issue

Why did European nations, the USA, and Japan pursue imperial expansion, and how did this reshape international relations?

### Big-picture explanation

The late 19th century saw a “New Imperialism” driven by economic competition (industrialisation, Long Depression, need for raw materials and markets), nationalism, strategic interests (Suez Canal, naval bases), and racial ideology. This scramble for overseas possessions—especially in Africa and Asia—created rivalries that contributed to the alliance systems and tensions leading to WWI. Simultaneously, two non-European powers (USA, Japan) emerged as world powers through rapid modernisation and military victories.

### Date span

1870–1919

### Must-know events (in order)

1. Franco-Prussian War (1870–71) → German unification; French humiliation  
2. “Long Depression” (1873–96) → economic competition drives imperialism  
3. Berlin Conference (1884–85) → regulates scramble for Africa  
4. British takeover of Egypt (1882) → triggers wider African scramble  
5. First Sino-Japanese War (1894) → Treaty of Shimonoseki; Japan defeats China  
6. Triple Intervention (1895) → Russia/France/Germany force Japan to return Port Arthur  
7. Jameson Raid (1895) → Kruger Telegram damages Anglo-German relations  
8. Fashoda Incident (1898) → Anglo-French compromise; avoids war  
9. Spanish-American War (1898) → Treaty of Paris; USA becomes imperial power  
10. Boxer Rebellion (1898–1901) → eight-nation alliance humiliates China  
11. Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902) → first recognition of Japan as equal  
12. Russo-Japanese War (1904–05) → Treaty of Portsmouth; first Asian defeat of European power  
13. Moroccan Crises (1905, 1911) → Germany challenges France; Entente strengthened  
14. WWI begins (1914) → alliance system triggers continental war  
15. Twenty-One Demands (1915) → Japan exploits European absence to expand in China  
16. USA enters WWI (April 1917) → submarine warfare + Zimmerman Telegram  
17. Wilson’s Fourteen Points (January 1918) → vision for post-war peace  
18. Armistice (November 1918) → end of WWI  

### Must-know actors

- **Bismarck (Germany)** — cautious; prioritised security; reluctant imperialist  
- **Kaiser Wilhelm II** — Weltpolitik; naval expansion; impetuous  
- **Cecil Rhodes** — British imperialist; “Cape to Cairo” vision  
- **King Leopold II** — brutal exploitation of Congo  
- **Jules Ferry** — French PM; “Mission Civilisatrice”  
- **Commodore Perry** — forced Japan open  
- **Emperor Meiji** — modernised Japan  
- **Theodore Roosevelt** — US imperialism; “big stick”; Nobel Peace Prize  
- **Woodrow Wilson** — Fourteen Points; League of Nations advocate  
- **Admiral Togo** — destroyed Russian fleet at Tsushima  

### Must-know treaties and agreements

- **Treaty of Berlin (1885)** — rules for African colonisation  
- **Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895)** — Japan gains Korea, Formosa, Port Arthur  
- **Treaty of Vereeniging (1902)** — ends Second Boer War  
- **Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902)** — mutual defence  
- **Entente Cordiale (1904)** — settles Anglo-French colonial rivalries  
- **Treaty of Portsmouth (1905)** — ends Russo-Japanese War  
- **Treaty of Paris (1898)** — USA gains Philippines, Puerto Rico, Guam  

### Essential facts (5–10)

1. By 1900, over 90% of Africa was under European control (only about 10% in 1870).  
2. Britain’s African possessions contained over 30% of Africa’s population.  
3. The “Long Depression” (1873–96) drove economic competition for overseas markets.  
4. Japan modernised in under 50 years from a largely closed society to defeating Russia (1905).  
5. USA’s industrial output surpassed European rivals in key sectors by 1900 (coal, steel, cotton).  
6. The Boer Wars cost Britain £200M+ and 22,000 soldiers killed.  
7. Germany’s colonies in Africa often cost more than they returned in direct economic benefit.  
8. Over 2 million American troops reached France during WWI.  
9. Japan’s Twenty-One Demands (1915) would have effectively ended Chinese independence if fully imposed.  
10. The USA emerged from WWI as the world’s greatest financial power.  

### Common factual confusions (3–5)

1. **Old vs New Imperialism** — “Old Imperialism” (Americas, 16th–18th century) is not the same as “New Imperialism” (Africa/Asia, from the 1870s to 1914): different regions, new players (USA, Japan, Germany), and attempts to manage rivalry without general European war.  
2. **Berlin Conference** — It did not “divide up Africa” in one sitting; it set rules for claiming territory while the scramble was already underway.  
3. **Russo-Japanese vs First Sino-Japanese War** — Different causes and opponents: Korea and China’s weakness vs Japan in 1894–95; Port Arthur/Manchuria and Russia vs Japan in 1904–05.  
4. **USA expansion** — Isolationism was a strong tradition; overseas empire was contested, not automatic.  
5. **Triple Alliance vs Triple Entente** — Triple Alliance: Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy; Triple Entente: Britain, France, Russia.  

### Why this topic matters analytically

This topic establishes structural causes of WWI and the post-war settlement. Imperial rivalry fed alliance systems, arms races, and nationalist tensions. The rise of the USA and Japan shifted the global balance. Analysing imperial motives (economic, political, strategic, ideological) is essential for explaining later crises and settlements.

## Topic 2: The League of Nations and international relations in the 1920s

### Core issue

How effective were attempts to maintain peace after WWI, and why did underlying tensions persist?

### Big-picture explanation

The Paris Peace Settlement (1919–20) produced treaties that fully satisfied none of the major powers. France wanted to crush Germany; Britain wanted moderate terms; Wilson wanted an idealistic peace. The compromise displeased almost everyone. The League of Nations was established but weakened by US non-membership, limited enforcement, and members putting national interest first. There were successes in smaller disputes and a thaw in the “Spirit of Locarno” (1925), but reparations, borders, and ideology kept tension high through the decade.

### Date span

1919–1929

### Must-know events (in order)

1. Treaty of Versailles (June 1919) — War Guilt Clause; territorial losses; 100,000 army limit; reparations  
2. US Senate rejects Versailles/League (Nov 1919) — return to isolationism  
3. Treaties of Saint-Germain, Neuilly, Sèvres, Trianon (1919–20) — reshape Eastern Europe  
4. League of Nations begins (Jan 1920) — Geneva HQ  
5. Vilna crisis (1920) — Poland defies League; Britain/France support Poland  
6. Washington Naval Conference (1921–22) — limits warship tonnage; reduces Far East tensions  
7. Genoa Conference (1922) — fails due to French intransigence on reparations  
8. Treaty of Rapallo (1922) — Germany–Russia restore relations; secret military collaboration  
9. French/Belgian occupation of Ruhr (1923) — triggers German hyperinflation  
10. Treaty of Lausanne (1923) — replaces Sèvres; first successful challenge to peace settlement  
11. Corfu Incident (1923) — Mussolini defies League  
12. Dawes Plan (1924) — restructures German reparations; US loans  
13. Locarno Conference (1925) — Germany accepts western borders  
14. Germany joins League (1926) — symbol of reconciliation  
15. Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928) — 60+ nations renounce war; no enforcement  
16. Young Plan (1929) — reduces total reparations from £6.6B to £2B  

### Must-know actors

- **Clemenceau** — “The Tiger”; wanted to destroy Germany  
- **Lloyd George** — moderate; wanted German economic recovery for British trade  
- **Wilson** — idealist; Fourteen Points; mediator  
- **Keynes** — predicted reparations would cause economic catastrophe  
- **Stresemann** — German FM; accepted Versailles at Locarno; aimed to revise peacefully  
- **Briand** — French FM; worked with Stresemann; Kellogg-Briand Pact  
- **Mussolini** — fascist Italy; Corfu; Fiume  
- **Charles Dawes** — American banker; reparations restructuring  
- **Owen Young** — American banker; reduced total reparations  

### Must-know treaties and agreements

- **Treaty of Versailles (1919)** — Germany  
- **Treaty of Saint-Germain (1919)** — Austria  
- **Treaty of Neuilly (1919)** — Bulgaria  
- **Treaty of Sèvres (1920)** — Turkey (replaced by Lausanne 1923)  
- **Treaty of Trianon (1920)** — Hungary  
- **Treaty of Rapallo (1922)** — Germany–Russia  
- **Treaty of Lausanne (1923)** — Turkey revised  
- **Dawes Plan (1924)**  
- **Locarno Treaties (1925)**  
- **Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928)**  
- **Young Plan (1929)**  

### Essential facts (5–10)

1. Germany lost ~70,000 sq km and 7 million people under Versailles.  
2. German reparations set at £6.6 billion (later reduced to £2B by Young Plan).  
3. German hyperinflation: $1 = 4.2 trillion marks by November 1923.  
4. Italy lost 600,000 soldiers in WWI; promised territory at Treaty of London (1915) largely denied.  
5. ~30 million people lived as minorities under foreign rule after the peace settlement.  
6. The League resolved disputes over Teschen, Åland Islands, Upper Silesia, and Greece–Bulgaria successfully.  
7. The League failed over Vilna, Ruhr, and Corfu — cases involving major power interests.  
8. France signed alliances with Poland (1921), Czechoslovakia (1924), Romania (1926), and Yugoslavia (1927).  
9. The USA never joined the League; the USSR was not admitted until 1934; Germany only from 1926.  
10. Dawes Plan circular flow: US loans → Germany → reparations to France/Britain → war-debt repayment to USA.  

### Common factual confusions (3–5)

1. **Harshness of Versailles** — It was a compromise; Clemenceau wanted harsher terms; Germany remained potentially the strongest European economy.  
2. **Dawes vs Young Plan** — Dawes (1924) restructured payments and stabilised the flow; Young (1929) reduced the total.  
3. **Locarno’s scope** — It guaranteed western borders (France/Belgium), not eastern borders with Poland/Czechoslovakia.  
4. **League as total failure** — It had notable 1920s successes; failures mounted when major powers’ interests clashed.  
5. **Sèvres vs Lausanne** — Sèvres (1920) with Turkey was not properly implemented; Lausanne (1923) replaced it and was the first major revision of the settlement.  

### Why this topic matters analytically

Weaknesses in the peace settlement and League design explain much of the 1930s collapse. Dependence on US credit (Dawes) made European stability fragile. French security fears were never fully met. The League’s inability to enforce against great powers foreshadowed later failures.

## Topic 3: The League of Nations and international relations in the 1930s

### Core issue

Why did international peace collapse in the 1930s, leading to WWII?

### Big-picture explanation

The Wall Street Crash (1929) undermined the economic basis of 1920s stability. American loans dried up; Germany was hit hard; political extremism grew. Hitler, Mussolini, and Japan’s military pursued expansion. The League failed over Manchuria (1931) and Abyssinia (1935). Britain and France followed appeasement — understandable given anti-war opinion, military limits, and fear of communism — but it encouraged escalating demands. The Nazi–Soviet Pact (1939) removed a key obstacle to Hitler’s attack on Poland.

### Date span

1929–1939

### Must-know events (in order)

1. Wall Street Crash (Oct 1929) → Great Depression → worldwide economic chaos  
2. Japan invades Manchuria (Sept 1931) → League sends Lytton Commission; Japan ignores  
3. Hitler becomes Chancellor (Jan 1933) → quickly establishes dictatorship  
4. Germany/Japan leave League (1933) → disarmament conference collapses  
5. Dollfuss murdered by Austrian Nazis (July 1934) → Mussolini blocks German invasion  
6. Stresa Front (April 1935) → Britain/France/Italy agree to resist Versailles changes → collapses quickly  
7. Anglo-German Naval Agreement (June 1935) → Britain condones German rearmament  
8. Italy invades Abyssinia (Oct 1935) → League sanctions ineffective; Hoare–Laval Pact exposed  
9. Rhineland remilitarised (March 1936) → Hitler’s biggest gamble; no opposition  
10. Spanish Civil War begins (July 1936) → international ideological battleground  
11. Rome–Berlin Axis (Oct 1936) → Italy–Germany alignment  
12. Anti-Comintern Pact (Nov 1936) → Germany–Japan (Italy joins 1937)  
13. Anschluss (March 1938) → Germany absorbs Austria  
14. Munich Conference (Sept 1938) → Germany gets Sudetenland; “peace for our time”  
15. Germany occupies rest of Czechoslovakia (March 1939) → appeasement discredited  
16. British guarantee to Poland (March 1939) → reversal of appeasement  
17. Nazi–Soviet Pact (Aug 1939) → secret protocol divides Poland  
18. Germany invades Poland (Sept 1, 1939) → WWII begins  

### Must-know actors

- **Hitler** — destroyed Versailles; Lebensraum; Anschluss; rearmament  
- **Mussolini** — shifted from opposing to supporting Hitler; Abyssinia; Rome–Berlin Axis  
- **Chamberlain** — British PM; appeasement; Munich; “peace for our time”  
- **Daladier** — French PM at Munich; reluctant appeaser  
- **Stalin** — sought alliance with West; rejected; signed Nazi–Soviet Pact  
- **Churchill** — opposed appeasement; predicted Munich would lead to war  
- **Franco** — Spanish Civil War; dictatorship; kept Spain out of WWII  
- **Haile Selassie** — Ethiopian emperor; appealed to League  
- **Litvinov** — Soviet representative; demanded collective security  

### Must-know treaties and agreements

- **Anglo-German Naval Agreement (1935)**  
- **Stresa Front (1935)**  
- **Franco-Soviet Treaty of Mutual Assistance (1935)**  
- **Rome–Berlin Axis (1936)**  
- **Anti-Comintern Pact (1936)**  
- **Munich Agreement (1938)**  
- **Pact of Steel (1939)**  
- **Nazi–Soviet Pact / Molotov–Ribbentrop (1939)**  
- **Anglo-Polish guarantee (1939)**  

### Essential facts (5–10)

1. After the Wall Street Crash, the US economy plunged into the Great Depression; unemployment rose into the tens of millions nationally in the early 1930s as industrial output collapsed.  
2. Hitler increased the German army target to 600,000 (six times the Versailles limit).  
3. At Munich, Czechoslovakia was not invited; the USSR was not invited either.  
4. Hitler described Rhineland remilitarisation as his biggest gamble — “most nerve-racking 48 hours of my life.”  
5. Britain spent £350M on armaments in 1937–38; Germany spent £1,600M.  
6. By 1939 Britain had fewer than 1,000 modern aircraft; Germany had about 2,800.  
7. British army was only 897,000 at war’s outbreak vs France’s 5M and Germany’s eventual 13M.  
8. A. J. P. Taylor called the Hoare–Laval Pact an event that “killed the League.”  
9. The Non-Intervention Committee over Spain included Germany and Italy, who both ignored it.  
10. The Nazi–Soviet Pact’s secret protocol gave the USSR a sphere in eastern Poland and leverage over Finland and the Baltic states.  

### Common factual confusions (3–5)

1. **Appeasement as cowardice only** — It had economic, military, and domestic political rationales at the time.  
2. **Stresa Front vs Anglo-German Naval Agreement** — Both 1935: Stresa was Britain/France/Italy vs Versailles revision; the Naval Agreement was Britain alone accepting German naval expansion — and undermined Stresa.  
3. **Manchuria vs Abyssinia** — 1931–33 Japan in China; 1935–36 Italy in Africa; both exposed League weakness in different regions.  
4. **Munich’s reception** — Many MPs backed it; Chamberlain was initially popular; condemnation grew after March 1939.  
5. **Axis vs Anti-Comintern vs Pact of Steel** — Rome–Berlin Axis (1936): political alignment; Anti-Comintern Pact (1936): Germany–Japan vs communism; Pact of Steel (1939): formal Italy–Germany military alliance.  

### Why this topic matters analytically

Structural weaknesses (League design, US absence, Anglo-French mistrust) interacted with the Depression, Hitler’s strategy, and appeasement logic. The central debate is whether firmer action earlier could have prevented war or whether deeper forces made conflict likely.

## Topic 4: China and Japan 1912–1945

### Core issue

Why did China descend into civil war and foreign invasion, and why did Japan shift from democracy to military dictatorship?

### Big-picture explanation

China’s 1911 revolution produced a republic that soon fragmented into warlord chaos. The KMT under Sun Yat-sen and then Chiang Kai-shek competed with the CCP under Mao for power. Japan adopted parliamentary institutions but democracy buckled under economic stress and military insubordination. Japan’s seizure of Manchuria (1931) and invasion of China proper (1937) devastated China and eventually drew the USA into the Pacific war after Pearl Harbor (1941).

### Date span

1912–1945

### Must-know events (in order)

1. Republic of China established (1912) → Sun Yat-sen briefly president; Yuan Shikai takes over  
2. Yuan dissolves KMT (1913) → becomes increasingly authoritarian  
3. Yuan dies (1916) → “warlord era” begins; China fragments  
4. May Fourth Movement (1919) → students protest Versailles treatment of China → nationalism surges  
5. CCP founded (1921) → initially tiny; Moscow-influenced  
6. First United Front: KMT–CCP alliance (1923–27) → Comintern arranges cooperation  
7. Northern Expedition (1926–28) → Chiang unifies much of China militarily  
8. Shanghai Massacre (1927) → Chiang purges communists → civil war begins  
9. Nanking Decade (1928–37) → KMT attempts modernisation with limited success  
10. Japan invades Manchuria (1931) → establishes Manchukuo puppet state  
11. Long March (1934–35) → CCP retreats ~9,000 km; heavy casualties; Mao secures leadership  
12. Xi’an Incident (1936) → Chiang kidnapped; forced into Second United Front with CCP vs Japan  
13. Marco Polo Bridge Incident (July 1937) → triggers full-scale Second Sino-Japanese War  
14. Nanking Massacre (Dec 1937) → estimated 300,000 killed by Japanese troops  
15. Mao’s Rectification Movement (1941–44) → purges CCP rivals; large death toll  
16. Pearl Harbor (Dec 1941) → Japan attacks USA; ends US isolationism in practice  
17. Japan defeated; withdraws from China (1945)  

### Must-know actors

- **Sun Yat-sen** — “Father of the Nation”; Three Principles; founded KMT  
- **Yuan Shikai** — first president; authoritarian; died 1916  
- **Chiang Kai-shek** — KMT leader; Northern Expedition; anti-communist; increasingly authoritarian  
- **Mao Zedong** — CCP leader; peasant revolution; Long March; Yenan base  
- **Emperor Hirohito** — presided over militarisation; refused to order army withdrawal from Manchuria  
- **General Tojo** — PM from 1941; military dominance; Pearl Harbor; executed for war crimes  
- **Zhang Xueliang** — Manchurian warlord; kidnapped Chiang at Xi’an  
- **Wang Ming** — Mao’s rival; “28 Bolsheviks”; urban/Comintern line  
- **Edgar Snow** — American journalist; *Red Star Over China* (1937); shaped Western views of the CCP  
- **Admiral Yamamoto** — planned Pearl Harbor attack  

### Must-know treaties and agreements

- **Twenty-One Demands (1915)** — Japan’s attempt to dominate China  
- **Washington Naval Conference agreements (1921–22)** — limited Japanese navy  
- **Tanggu Truce (1933)** — KMT recognises Japanese position in Manchuria  
- **Anti-Comintern Pact (1936)** — Germany–Japan vs communism  
- **Nazi–Soviet Pact secret protocol** — altered strategic calculus; freed Japan to expand southward  

### Essential facts (5–10)

1. Sun Yat-sen’s Three Principles: nationalism, democracy, people’s livelihood.  
2. May Fourth Movement was triggered by Versailles awarding Shantung to Japan.  
3. Chiang spent about 47% of government income on military, less than 5% on education.  
4. Long March: ~9,000 km; started with ~86,000; ~8,000 survived.  
5. By 1934, tens of millions of Chinese peasants were landless or severely impoverished.  
6. Japan’s textile exports fell about 50% between 1929 and 1931 because of the Depression.  
7. PM Inukai Tsuyoshi was assassinated (May 1932) by naval officers after criticising the Manchuria adventure.  
8. Japan’s proposed racial equality clause at Paris was blocked — Wilson insisted on unanimity.  
9. Pearl Harbor: 2,402 Americans killed; eight battleships damaged; three carriers were at sea and escaped.  
10. Mao’s Rectification Movement (1941–44) killed an estimated 10,000; dozens of CCP leaders were driven to suicide.  

### Common factual confusions (3–5)

1. **Sun Yat-sen vs Chiang Kai-shek** — Founder (died 1925) vs military successor; different roles and methods.  
2. **CCP popularity** — The party was tiny until the 1930s; growth owed much to land policy, anti-Japanese resistance, and KMT failures.  
3. **First vs Second United Front** — 1923–27: KMT–CCP cooperation; 1936–45: uneasy alliance against Japan.  
4. **Manchuria 1931** — Kwantung Army acted ahead of Tokyo; critics risked assassination.  
5. **First vs Second Sino-Japanese War** — 1894 (Korea/China vs Japan); 1937–45 (full-scale invasion of China).  

### Why this topic matters analytically

Internal fragmentation invited external aggression in China. Japan’s path shows how economic crisis, weak civilian control, and ultra-nationalism could overturn parliamentary rule. The CCP’s later victory rested heavily on peasant mobilisation, propaganda, and KMT weaknesses — not only battlefield success.
