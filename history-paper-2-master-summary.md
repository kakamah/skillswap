# 1. New Imperialism (c. 1870-1917)

## Big Picture: how the unit fits together

### The New Imperialism system

**Concept Summary**
- The provided material presents **New Imperialism** as a shift from older patterns of coastal footholds and informal influence to **accelerated, state-backed territorial acquisition**.
- Its distinctive feature was not merely expansion, but the fact that expansion became a **Great Power status test** and happened with unusual speed.
- The strongest overall framing in the PDFs is that imperialism worked as an interacting system: **industrial capability + strategic geography + prestige politics + ideological legitimation + enabling technology**.

**Mechanism (Cause -> Event -> Effect)**
- Industrial capacity + global economic pressures -> overseas expansion becomes more attractive -> states seek markets, resources, and outlets for influence.
- Strategic chokepoints + prestige competition ("if we don't take it, a rival will") -> specific territories become urgent -> rivalry accelerates annexation and crisis politics.
- Technology + communications + medicine + weaponry -> lower costs and raise feasibility -> the Scramble accelerates once competition begins.
- Civilising and racial ideologies -> conquest is framed as moral duty -> coercion and hierarchy become easier to justify and sustain.

**Key Arguments**
- The notes treat the late nineteenth-century imperial system as a **"security-prestige spiral"**: each acquisition raised fears of rival encirclement, making further acquisitions appear necessary.
- **Economics** explains why expansion was attractive, but often under-explains why specific territories mattered immediately.
- **Strategy** best explains urgency around routes, bases, and chokepoints.
- **Prestige** is causal when it changes risk tolerance and turns optional expansion into status necessity.
- **Ideology** was "rarely sufficient to trigger annexation alone," but was crucial in sustaining empire and justifying coercion.
- **Technology** should be treated as a **multiplier** rather than a primary motive: it explains why powers could expand rapidly, not why they wanted to.
- A high-level judgement from the imperialism guide is that imperialism was **"simultaneously a product of capability and insecurity."**

**Historical Evaluation (Extent/Importance)**
- The material makes clear that top answers should judge importance by **timing, scale, uniqueness, and downstream consequences**, not by listing causes.
- Across the block, the most consistent hierarchy is: **economic pressures made expansion attractive, but strategic geography and prestige rivalry often determined decisive choices**.
- The broad significance of New Imperialism was that it expanded control, intensified rivalry, and helped turn imperial politics into a training ground for twentieth-century international conflict.

**🚨 Trap:** Do not write that "everything was economic." The PDFs repeatedly insist that economics is often a **structural pressure**, while **strategy and prestige** explain decisive moments such as Suez/Egypt or Germany's late-1890s posture.

**📝 Model Conclusion:** **Economic pressures made expansion attractive, but strategic chokepoints and prestige competition determined where and when empires acted; technology lowered costs, while diplomacy institutionalised competition.**

| Power / case | Primary logic in the PDFs | Best supporting examples | Best overall judgement |

| **Britain** | Imperial network defence; route and corridor security | Suez, Egypt, East Africa, Fashoda | Best understood as **system maintenance** rather than simple expansion for profit |
| **Germany** | Status recognition, domestic mobilisation, bargaining leverage | Berlin Conference, "place in the sun," Kruger Telegram | A classic **rising-power status** case; symbolic value often outweighed material gain |

## Africa and the acceleration of imperial rivalry

### The Scramble for Africa

**Concept Summary**
- The Scramble for Africa is presented in the PDFs as the clearest laboratory for the whole imperial system because its acceleration is measurable: **roughly 10% European control in the 1870s to about 90% by 1914**, with **Ethiopia and Liberia** as the famous exceptions.
- The central analytical problem is not simply why Europe wanted colonies, but **why the Scramble intensified and why Africa became the main arena**.

**Mechanism (Cause -> Event -> Effect)**
- Economic incentives + strategic competition + prestige rivalry + enabling technology -> rapid territorial claims in Africa -> partition accelerates from limited control to near-total domination.
- Rival acquisitions -> fear of encirclement and loss of status -> further acquisitions appear necessary -> the **security-prestige spiral** deepens.
- Rules for recognising claims + improved means of control -> competition becomes easier to pursue -> annexation speeds up.

**Key Arguments**
- The strongest synthesis in the guide is that the Scramble must be explained by **capability and competitive pressure**, not by opportunistic trade alone.
- Africa became the central arena because strategic and diplomatic turning points such as **Suez** and **Berlin** converted broad motives into concrete annexation.
- A strong exam formulation from the notes is: **"Economic incentives mattered, but crisis points and strategic geography determined urgency, while technology lowered barriers and Berlin created a legal pathway for competitive annexation."**
- The PDFs treat the Scramble as evidence that imperialism was not a single-cause process, but an interactive system in which diplomacy, rivalry, and technology reinforced one another.

**Historical Evaluation (Extent/Importance)**
- The Scramble is historically central because it gives the clearest evidence for the scale and speed of New Imperialism.
- It is also the best test case for weighing causes: the material repeatedly suggests that **economics alone is too weak**, while **strategy, prestige, and enabling technology** better explain the pattern and acceleration.
- In essay terms, the Scramble matters because it turns abstract motives into a concrete causal system.

### Suez, Egypt, and British route strategy

**Concept Summary**
- The Suez Canal and Egypt form the clearest example in the PDFs of strategic geography turning imperial involvement into necessity.
- The notes treat Suez as a true **turning point** because it elevated Egypt from a territory of interest into a **core imperial security issue**.

**Mechanism (Cause -> Event -> Effect)**
- Long-term need for faster Europe-Asia routes -> **Suez Canal opens (1869)** -> Egypt becomes strategically crucial.
- Egypt's financial troubles + British strategic interest -> **Britain buys Suez shares (1875)** -> informal leverage over canal governance increases.
- Local unrest + strategic fears around Suez + rivalry context -> **British occupation of Egypt (1882)** -> canal-zone security is anchored and British route strategy becomes more fixed.

**Key Arguments**
- The canal is not background detail; it is an **enabling strategic mechanism** that reshaped imperial priorities.
- The occupation of Egypt is treated as strategic control **"disguised by arguments of order and stability."**
- Britain in Egypt fits the category of strategic **need** rather than strategic ambition: it is the clearest case for route defence as a causal driver.
- The notes describe Egypt after 1882 as a **"fixed point" in Britain's imperial geometry**.
- Suez also helps explain why African involvement shifted from opportunism to strategic imperative.

**Historical Evaluation (Extent/Importance)**
- The Suez-Egypt sequence is one of the most important causal chains in the entire unit because it shows how geography turned broad imperial interest into urgent policy.
- It is especially valuable for ranking motives: the PDFs strongly imply that in Egypt **strategy outranked economics**.
- As a turning point, Suez matters less because it created imperialism from nothing than because it accelerated and redirected it.

**🚨 Trap:** Do not treat the occupation of Egypt as merely a generic land-grab. The material stresses **route security and chokepoint logic** as the central explanation.

### The Berlin Conference (1884-85)

**Concept Summary**
- The Berlin Conference is presented as the key moment of **legal-diplomatic standardisation** in the Scramble for Africa.
- Its importance lies not in stopping imperialism, but in creating a framework for recognising claims through **"effective occupation."**

**Mechanism (Cause -> Event -> Effect)**
- Rising risk of clashes over colonial claims -> **Berlin Conference** -> common rules for recognising claims are agreed.
- Recognition framework + notification procedures -> clearer standards for valid possession -> competition is regularised and accelerated.

**Key Arguments**
- The guide is explicit that Berlin did **not** restrain imperial expansion; it **"regularised and accelerated it."**
- By clarifying what counted as a valid claim, Berlin turned rivalry into a more organised race.
- Berlin therefore supports the argument that diplomacy could institutionalise competition rather than soften it.
- In essay terms, Berlin works best when used as a mechanism: it converted motives into a rule-governed process.

**Historical Evaluation (Extent/Importance)**
- The Berlin Conference is historically important because it shows that cooperation and rivalry could coexist inside the same imperial system.
- It is especially useful for explaining **timing and acceleration**: without a recognition framework, claims were riskier and less standardised.
- The PDFs support a balanced judgement: Berlin was evidence of diplomatic cooperation, but the cooperation served imperial competition rather than limiting it.

### The Fashoda Incident (1898)

**Concept Summary**
- Fashoda is used in the PDFs as the classic case of imperial rivalry reaching near-war level while still being managed diplomatically.
- The crisis emerged from incompatible corridor ambitions: Britain aimed at a north-south Nile linkage, while France pursued a west-east vision.

**Mechanism (Cause -> Event -> Effect)**
- Rival corridor strategies in the Nile basin -> **Fashoda confrontation** -> acute Anglo-French tension.
- Diplomatic settlement + French withdrawal -> no war -> clearer spheres and later movement toward accommodation.

**Key Arguments**
- Fashoda is the clearest illustration of **"managed competition."**
- It supports two arguments at once: imperial rivalry intensified international tension, but imperial diplomacy could still prevent war.
- The guide treats it as proof that competition was not peaceful, but **crisis-managed**.
- It is also a model case for showing that significance depends on what changed and what did not: the risk of war in that theatre fell, but imperial rivalry did not disappear.

**Historical Evaluation (Extent/Importance)**
- Fashoda matters because it shows the dual character of imperial politics: cooperation in crisis management alongside deep rivalry.
- It is highly significant as evidence against simplistic claims that imperialism either produced constant war or stable peace.
- For essay judgement, the best line is that Fashoda reduced the immediate Anglo-French danger but did not end the wider competitive logic of empire.

**📝 Model Conclusion:** **Fashoda showed that imperial competition could be managed without war, but it did not remove the structural rivalry that imperial expansion itself had created.**

## Britain and Germany in the imperial system

### British expansion in East and Southern Africa

**Concept Summary**
- The PDFs present British imperialism as a **region-specific** process whose logic changed by theatre.
- In East Africa, the dominant logic was corridor defence and the need to secure influence inland; in Southern Africa, resource politics, settler conflict, and prestige were more visible.

**Mechanism (Cause -> Event -> Effect)**
- Fear of rival corridors + need to consolidate the Nile and Great Lakes connection -> protectorates in **Uganda** and **East Africa** + **Uganda Railway** -> stronger administrative and military reach inland.
- Imperial ambition + settler politics + resource zones -> **Boer conflicts** and the **Second Boer War** -> British victory, but at high diplomatic and reputational cost.
- Imperial pressure in South Africa + rivalry sensitivity -> **Jameson Raid** and **Kruger Telegram** -> public hostility in Britain and sharper Anglo-German antagonism.

**Key Arguments**
- The notes insist that British imperialism cannot be explained by one motive across all regions.
- **Egypt/Suez = route security**; **East Africa = corridor defence against rivals**; **South Africa = resources + settler politics + prestige**.
- The Uganda Railway is valuable evidence that technology was not just for conquest, but for **strategic consolidation**.
- The Boer War is presented as proof that empire carried severe **costs**, not just gains.
- The Jameson Raid reveals **private-state entanglement**, while the Kruger Telegram shows how colonial incidents could become Europe-wide rivalry triggers.

**Historical Evaluation (Extent/Importance)**
- The historical importance of British African expansion lies in the way it exposes the limits of single-cause explanations.
- The most persuasive ranking in the PDFs is that strategy dominates in Egypt and East Africa, while South Africa requires more weight for resources, settler politics, and prestige.
- Britain's wider significance is as a case of imperial **system maintenance**: a global network defended through expansion at pressure points.

**🚨 Trap:** Do not answer British expansion as if all regions worked the same way. The source material explicitly rewards **regional ranking of motives**.

### Germany's colonial entry and Weltpolitik

**Concept Summary**
- Germany is presented as a newly unified power whose empire-building must be read through **status anxiety**, domestic politics, and bargaining strategy.
- The notes distinguish clearly between **Bismarck-era caution** and the later, more assertive posture associated with **Weltpolitik** and the language of **"a place in the sun."**

**Mechanism (Cause -> Event -> Effect)**
- New great-power status + industrial rise + desire for recognition -> colonial entry in the 1880s -> Germany joins imperial competition.
- Hosting Berlin + acquiring colonies + defining spheres with rivals -> empire becomes a tool of diplomatic leverage -> Germany gains symbolic world-power standing.
- Rising prestige politics + more assertive world policy -> "place in the sun" rhetoric + crises such as the **Kruger Telegram** -> Anglo-German suspicion worsens.
- Colonial resistance + coercive suppression -> **Herero and Nama genocide (1904-08)** -> civilising rhetoric is exposed by colonial violence.

**Key Arguments**
- Germany is the strongest case in the PDFs for **prestige as a real cause**, not mere packaging.
- Colonies often mattered less for profit than for recognition, domestic mobilisation, and diplomatic bargaining.
- The **Heligoland-Zanzibar Agreement (1890)** complicates any crude claim of constant hostility with Britain: the imperial system could still produce negotiated settlements.
- The **"place in the sun"** speech is treated as a key historiographical marker of Germany's shift toward assertive global policy.
- The Herero and Nama case is central for the ideology debate because it **undercuts civilising claims** and shows violence as a mechanism of colonial control.

**Historical Evaluation (Extent/Importance)**
- Germany's imperialism is historically important because it illustrates how a **latecomer empire** could have great symbolic importance even where material gain was limited.
- The sources strongly support the judgement that **prestige/status** and domestic mobilisation were often more important than straightforward economic profit.
- Germany also matters because its colonial gestures and crises helped worsen the wider diplomatic climate, especially with Britain.

## Emerging world powers and imperial transformation

### The United States and overseas expansion after 1898

**Concept Summary**
- The PDFs argue that the United States did not simply move from isolation to intervention; rather, it shifted from a mainly hemispheric posture under the **Monroe Doctrine** to **overseas war, territories, bases, and canal-building**.
- The best overall phrasing in the material is **"selective imperial activism."**

**Mechanism (Cause -> Event -> Effect)**
- Economic stress in the 1890s + frontier-closure thinking + navalism/sea power arguments -> expansionist pressure rises -> overseas empire becomes politically imaginable and strategically attractive.
- **Spanish-American War (1898)** + **Treaty of Paris** -> overseas possessions gained -> the US becomes an overseas colonial power.
- Resistance in the Philippines -> **Philippine-American War** -> empire requires coercive governance and exposes the cost of expansion.
- Doctrinal and infrastructural consolidation -> **Platt Amendment**, **Roosevelt Corollary**, **Panama Canal** -> regional hegemony and global reach are institutionalised.

**Key Arguments**
- The Monroe Doctrine should not be treated as pure isolation; the notes define it as a doctrine of separate spheres and regional assertion.
- The 1898 war is the clearest turning point because it forced a lasting debate about empire and created concrete possessions.
- The **Platt Amendment** is the guide's best example of **"informal empire through legal constraint"** and **"formal independence, constrained sovereignty."**
- The Roosevelt Corollary marks a crucial continuity/change point: the US moved from warning Europe away from the hemisphere to policing order within it.
- The Panama Canal is not background; it is evidence of **structural world-power capacity**.

**Historical Evaluation (Extent/Importance)**
- The US case is historically important because it shows imperialism extending beyond Europe while also taking a mixed form: annexation in some places, legal and strategic control in others.
- The most defensible judgement in the PDFs is that the United States became a world power through **war + doctrine + infrastructure**, not by joining European alliance politics.
- The strongest evaluative conclusion is that 1898 did not produce total abandonment of restraint, but a more assertive and outward-reaching version of American power.

**🚨 Trap:** Do not use the weak binary "isolationist -> interventionist." The provided material explicitly prefers **continuity plus selective activism**.

### Japan's rise to world-power status

**Concept Summary**
- The notes present Japan's rise as a **two-stage mechanism**: first, state modernisation under Meiji created capacity; second, war and diplomacy converted capacity into recognised status.
- Fear of Western domination after forced opening is treated as the catalyst for reform.

**Mechanism (Cause -> Event -> Effect)**
- Forced opening + fear of Western domination -> **Meiji Restoration** and reforms such as conscription -> stronger state and military capacity.
- Modernised state capacity -> **First Sino-Japanese War** and Treaty of Shimonoseki -> Japan proves military effectiveness and begins an imperial posture.
- **Triple Intervention (1895)** -> victory is turned into humiliation -> further militarisation and alliance-seeking become more urgent.
- **Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902)** + **Russo-Japanese War (1904-05)** -> diplomatic recognition and military proof -> Japan reaches world-power status by 1905.
- Post-1905 dominance -> **Korea annexation (1910)** -> formal empire is consolidated.

**Key Arguments**
- The crucial causal distinction in the PDFs is that **internal reform was the necessary cause; wars and alliance were the sufficient proof**.
- The 1902 alliance matters because it signalled recognition as a legitimate partner against Russian expansion.
- The Russo-Japanese War is the strongest single piece of evidence for "Japan as a world power by 1905."
- The sources also stress the tension within Japan's rise: reform undertaken for defence against Western pressure evolved into an imperialism structurally similar to European models.

**Historical Evaluation (Extent/Importance)**
- Japan is historically important because it shows that the imperial system was not exclusively European.
- The rise of Japan matters not just for military success, but because it demonstrates how reform, diplomacy, and war combined to change global hierarchy.
- The most persuasive judgement from the notes is that 1905 is the key threshold for status recognition, while 1910 confirms that recognition through formal imperial consolidation.

**🚨 Trap:** Do not treat Japan's rise as only military. The source material insists that wars should be read as **tests of capacity created by reform**, not as self-explaining events.

## Imperialism beyond Africa: China

### International involvement in China in the late nineteenth century

**Concept Summary**
- The History2 PDF presents international involvement in China as beginning in the logic of trade but mutating into a more aggressive imperial structure by the late nineteenth century.
- Its core judgement is that trade was the **initial catalyst**, but the mechanisms used to enforce trade **"rapidly mutated into aggressive political imperialism."**

**Mechanism (Cause -> Event -> Effect)**
- Trade imbalance + opium trade pressures -> Opium Wars and unequal treaties -> foreign access is imposed on China.
- Need to secure and deepen access + resistance from Qing China -> extraterritoriality, concessions, and spheres of influence -> sovereignty is eroded.
- Geopolitical rivalry and prestige competition -> **"Scramble for Concessions" (1898)** -> involvement goes beyond trade into overt imperial competition.

**Key Arguments**
- The material is explicit that **trade was the gateway motive**, not the full explanation.
- By the late nineteenth century, international involvement was driven not just by markets, but by **geopolitical rivalry and prestige**, with trade used as a pretext for colonisation.
- **Extraterritoriality** is singled out as a particularly sharp form of imperial humiliation because it exempted foreigners from Qing law.
- The guide's strongest formulations include: **"trade was merely the Trojan Horse"** and **"trade was the vector that introduced European imperialism into 19th-century China."**

**Historical Evaluation (Extent/Importance)**
- China is important within this block because it broadens New Imperialism beyond Africa and shows how empire could operate through unequal treaties, legal privilege, and spheres of influence rather than formal annexation alone.
- The best-supported judgement is that trade remained important, but by the late nineteenth century **imperialism had become the dominant operating mechanism**.
- The PDFs do not present China simply as a trade case; they present it as evidence of the transformation from economic pressure into political subordination.

### The Boxer Rebellion (1899-1901)

**Concept Summary**
- The Boxer Rebellion is described as a violent, anti-imperialist and anti-Christian uprising rooted in the local realities of foreign domination.
- The notes insist it was **not merely blind xenophobia**, but a response to **legal humiliation, missionary interference, and acute social distress**.

**Mechanism (Cause -> Event -> Effect)**
- Long-term imperial humiliation + extraterritoriality + missionary protection of converts in local disputes -> intense rural resentment -> Boxers target missionaries and converts.
- **1898 Yellow River floods** + famine and economic desperation -> popular radicalisation -> Boxer violence intensifies.
- Anti-foreign uprising -> siege and international suppression -> **Boxer Protocol (1901)** imposes indemnities and entrenches foreign power.

**Key Arguments**
- The best judgement in the PDF is that the Boxer Rebellion was **"the violent intersection of long-term imperial humiliation with acute ecological and economic desperation."**
- Missionaries mattered because they were not only cultural agents; they were protected by imperial legal structures and could disrupt local legal and land disputes.
- The guide stresses that the Boxers reacted less against abstract global trade than against the **daily local manifestations of lost sovereignty**.
- The modern synthesis in the notes treats the movement as a genuine anti-imperialist response, while still recognising the role of ecological crisis in turning resentment into revolt.

**Historical Evaluation (Extent/Importance)**
- The Boxer Rebellion is historically significant because it reveals the social consequences of imperialism at the local level.
- It is also one of the clearest examples in the provided material of how economic motives, legal inequality, religious intrusion, and environmental crisis could combine.
- The strongest ranking in the PDFs is: **extraterritoriality / imperialism first, missionary interference second, ecological crisis third as the immediate spark**.

**🚨 Trap:** Do not write that the Boxers "just hated foreigners." The material specifically argues that **extraterritorial legal immunity and missionary interference** made anti-foreign anger concrete and politically explosive.

**📝 Model Conclusion:** **Trade was the gateway motive, but in China it generated a harsher imperial structure of legal privilege, territorial pressure, and cultural intrusion; the Boxer Rebellion was the localized eruption of that wider loss of sovereignty.**
