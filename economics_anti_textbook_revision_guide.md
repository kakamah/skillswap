# The Economics Anti-Textbook — 10-Page High-Density Revision Guide
### Rod Hill & Tony Myatt | Synthesised for Exam Use

---

## THE BOOK'S CENTRAL ARGUMENT (ONE PARAGRAPH)

Mainstream introductory economics textbooks are not value-free science — they encode a conservative, market-fundamentalist ideology that treats competitive markets as efficient, government as an interferer, and inequality as a natural result of productivity differences. They achieve this through definitional choices, topic selection, suppression of contrary evidence, and the persistent use of perfect competition as both a generic model and an ideal standard. The Anti-Textbook exposes these omissions and biases chapter by chapter, drawing on Nobel laureates and empirical evidence to show that externalities are pervasive, inequality is harmful, corporate power is real, and the standard policy prescriptions (minimum wages always increase unemployment; redistribution always reduces efficiency) are empirically contested or false.

---

## INTRODUCTION & OVERARCHING THEMES

**Core claim**: Economics textbooks claim to be objective, yet every page contains ideological choices about what matters, what to measure, and what to ignore.

**What the textbook worldview assumes**:
- Rational, self-interested individuals are the fundamental unit.
- Competitive markets allocate resources efficiently.
- Government intervention causes inefficiency.
- Equity concerns are secondary to efficiency.
- Economic growth increases welfare.

**What the Anti-Textbook says is wrong with this**:
- Individuals are not always rational (behavioural economics).
- Competitive markets are rare; most firms have market power.
- Government intervention can correct real failures.
- The equity–efficiency trade-off is largely a myth in practice.
- Income growth does not increase average happiness in wealthy countries (Easterlin Paradox).

**Key method**: Ask not only what the textbook says, but what it *omits* and whose interests are served by the omission.

## CHAPTER 1: WHAT IS ECONOMICS?

**Standard view**: Economics = science of choice; scarcity + unlimited wants → efficiency goal; rationality; the invisible hand; efficiency–equity trade-off.

**Anti-text**: The definition is a value judgement.
- **Keynes**: economic problem = meeting *absolute needs* (food, shelter, health) → equity is central, not peripheral.
- **Post-Keynesians**: in recession, no scarcity — unemployed workers + idle factories → opportunity cost = zero.
- **Northrop/Marglin**: "unlimited wants" are socially constructed through advertising and community destruction.
- Treating all wants as equal (yacht = subsistence food) is a value judgement masquerading as neutrality.

**Individual vs. community**: Marglin shows rational-choice framework undermines community ties that give life meaning. Marwell and Ames (1981), Frank et al. (1993): economics exposure reduces cooperative behaviour.

**Individual vs. corporation**: Galbraith — the real economy has two parts: small competitive firms (disappearing) + a few hundred enormous corporations that shape government policy. Textbooks focus on the first and ignore the second.

**Equity–efficiency trade-off challenged**:
- Taxes on pollution improve efficiency (double dividend).
- Inequality reduces trust, raises crime, worsens health → bad for efficiency and growth.
- Persson & Tabellini (1994), Alesina & Rodrik (1994): greater inequality → lower growth.

**Behavioural economics** (Kahneman, Thaler):
- Bounded rationality: bat-and-ball problem; systematic cognitive errors.
- Bounded willpower: procrastination; Save More Tomorrow programme (3.5% → 13.6% savings rate).
- Bounded selfishness: ultimatum game; financial incentives crowd out altruism (blood donors; nursery fines).
- Framing effects: 90% survival = 10% death → different decisions despite identical information.
- Textbooks treat this as a footnote of "anomalies."

**Memory hook**: *"The map is not the territory — and the textbook map was drawn by someone with an agenda."*

## CHAPTER 2: INTRODUCING ECONOMIC MODELS

**Standard view**: Models judged by predictive accuracy, not assumption realism. Positive/normative distinction. Broad consensus among economists on empirical questions (citing 79-93% agreement on minimum wage → unemployment).

**Anti-text**:

**Methodology is fudged**: Textbooks present models (comparative advantage) without testing their predictions. The methodology is invoked for authority, then abandoned.

**Consensus is invented**:
- Minimum wage surveys: "generally agree" fell from 68% (1979) → 56% (1992) → 46% (2003).
- Card and Krueger (1994): minimum wage increases in NJ vs. PA → no unemployment increase.
- Textbooks still assert consensus and cite a 1982 "typical study."
- Dewald et al. (1986): published results often cannot be replicated even with identical data.

**Core propositions cannot be refuted** (Lakatos): Protective belt of auxiliary assumptions always allows negative evidence to be blamed on methodology, not the theory. The competitive model is an article of faith.

**Economics as rhetoric** (McCloskey): Economists actually persuade through analogies, thought experiments, natural experiments, historical precedents, authority — not scientific testing. The pool-player analogy explains why textbooks don't need to survey people about marginal analysis.

**Paradigms and ideology**: Neoclassical economics is one paradigm among many. Its roots: classical liberalism (Locke, Mill) — individual responsibility, property rights, minimal government. Textbooks present this as neutral science; it is conservative political philosophy.

**Comparative advantage as rhetoric**: Over 70% of industrialised country exports go to other industrialised countries — inconsistent with comparative advantage (Pugel 2007). US, Canada, Germany industrialised behind high tariffs. Samuelson (2004): free trade producing net losses is a "popular polemical untruth."

**Memory hook**: *"The textbook claims to be a thermometer; it is actually a thermostat — setting the temperature of thought."*

## CHAPTER 3: HOW MARKETS WORK (IN AN IMAGINARY WORLD)

**Standard view**: Supply and demand as generic tool. Price floors → surpluses (minimum wage). Price ceilings → shortages (rent control). Tax incidence follows relative elasticities.

**Anti-text**:

**Supply curves require perfect competition**: Krugman and Wells (their own textbook): "The supply and demand model ... is a model of a perfectly competitive market" (revealed only in Chapter 9 of their own text). Supply curves don't exist in oligopoly or monopoly — yet textbooks apply S&D everywhere.

**Perfect competition is almost nonexistent**:
- Blinder et al. (1998): surveyed 200 representative US firms — *all* are price-makers; prices change on average 1.4 times per year.
- Stiglitz: even infinitesimal search costs destroy competitive pricing (epsilon argument).
- Agriculture: only ~2% of GDP; even farmers face monopsonistic buyers (supermarkets, fast food).

**Predictions are weak**:
- Minimum wage: consensus has broken down; evidence is mixed.
- Rent control: Arnott (1995) — modern second-generation controls have "almost imperceptible" effects.
- Tax incidence: supply curves don't exist in cigarettes/gasoline (the textbook's favourite examples) — no evidence is presented because it can't be gathered.

**Internal inconsistencies**:
- Arrow's Paradox: if everyone is a price-taker, who changes prices? Solution: invent a fictional auctioneer.
- Sraffa (1926): requirements for supply/demand independence are incompatible with requirements for many small firms.
- Multiple equilibria: backward-bending labour supply (Prasch) can produce poverty traps — minimum wages and hours laws can escape them.
- SMD Theorem: a competitive economy may not have a unique stable equilibrium — "Anything Goes."

**Legal framework is invisible**: Markets require contract law, property law, regulation. Paris eviction-protection failure shows non-price regulation can cause housing problems just as severe as rent control.

**Memory hook**: *"Supply and demand is the Swiss Army knife used for everything, even when the job requires something different."*

## CHAPTER 4: PEOPLE AS CONSUMERS

**Standard view**: Preferences are given and stable. Perfect information. Utility independent of others' consumption. More income = more welfare.

**Anti-text**:

**Preferences are not given**:
- Advertising: 2.1% of US GDP; Galbraith (1958): too big to ignore.
- Children: sophisticated marketing targeting children (Channel One); Quebec ban on ads to children under 12 → reduced junk food purchases.
- Changing preferences problem: whose preferences count — before or after advertising changed them?

**Asymmetric information is the rule**:
- Infant formula: WHO/UNICEF — aggressive marketing causes 1 million+ preventable infant deaths per year. Breastfeeding rates: US 27%, UK 21% vs. Sweden 72%, Norway 80%.
- Prozac: no more effective than placebo for most users; evidence suppressed by manufacturer; 40 million users exposed to risk of lethal side effects.
- Food: unknown additives, declining nutritional content, factory farming — consumers systematically underinformed.

**The Easterlin Paradox** (1974):
- Within countries: richer → happier. ✓
- Across countries over time: income growth does *not* raise average happiness.
- US happiness: flat since 1946 despite enormous income growth (Figure 4.5).
- Explanation: **adaptation** (new income becomes the norm) and **social comparison** (relative position matters, not absolute level).
- Robert Frank (*Luxury Fever*): rising inequality raises social norms → futile positional arms race → consumer debt, overwork.
- Linder: more goods but no more time → "increasingly hectic tempo of life."

**Implications**: Growth in wealthy countries may not improve well-being. Redistribution, public goods, and leisure might do more for welfare than more GDP.

**Memory hook**: *"The textbook consumer is Robinson Crusoe. The real consumer is bombarded with marketing, comparing themselves to their neighbours, and eating food of unknown composition."*

## CHAPTER 5: THE FIRM

**Standard view**: Profit maximisation; U-shaped costs limit firm size; firms don't shape politics; managers serve shareholders; capitalist firm is the natural form.

**Anti-text**:

**U-shaped costs are fictional**:
- Short run: Blinder et al. (1998) — 89% of firms have constant or decreasing marginal costs. Firms hold idle capacity.
- Long run: Mansfield (1994) — "long-run average cost function in most industries seems to be L-shaped ... not U-shaped." Nothing limits firm size.
- These curves exist to justify perfect competition, not to describe reality (Cohen 1983, 1996).

**The competitive equilibrium is incoherent**: In textbook equilibrium, if one firm raises its price slightly, it loses *no* customers (competitors can't expand to serve them). Firms would *always* want to raise prices. Dixon (1990): competitive equilibrium is a disequilibrium.

**Firms shape their external environment** (ignored by textbooks):
- Washington: 65 lobbyists per Congress member; $200m/month corporate lobbying.
- Healthcare reform: 6 lobbyists per Congress member; outcome = "total victory for health insurance industry."
- Tobacco: "Citizens Against Lawsuit Abuse" (CALA) — front organisation for Philip Morris.
- Olson (1971): concentrated industries overcome the free-rider problem; dispersed citizens cannot → "systematic tendency for exploitation of the great by the small."
- Rational ignorance: it doesn't pay citizens to learn about complex policy issues when their vote makes no difference anyway.

**Power within the firm**: Principal-agent problem; Bebchuk and Fried (2004): executive pay rose from 5.2% to 8.1% of net income; shareholders lost $69bn in excess compensation.

**Democratic alternatives**: Worker cooperatives (Mondragón: 80+ co-ops, 30,000 workers); evidence of higher productivity. Absent from textbooks. John Stuart Mill (1848) predicted worker-managed firms would predominate.

**Memory hook**: *"The textbook firm converts inputs into outputs. The real firm shapes the laws, regulations, and cultural norms within which it operates."*

## CHAPTER 6: MARKET STRUCTURE AND EFFICIENCY

**Standard view**: Perfect competition = socially optimal; monopoly = deadweight loss; competitive model is the ideal benchmark for policy.

**Anti-text**:

**Three reasons perfect competition is not ideal**:

1. **Equity**: Efficiency depends on the income distribution being just. Willingness to pay reflects income, not need. The "ideal" outcome could have orphans starving while the rich have luxury yachts. Most textbooks do not mention this condition (Colander 2003).

2. **Dynamic efficiency**: Schumpeter — monopoly profits are the *reward* for innovation. Perfect competition produces no innovation incentives. The most important feature of capitalism (creativity and growth) is invisible in the competitive model.

3. **Conditions never met**: No externalities, perfect information, all markets existing — impossible in practice.

**Deadweight loss is overstated**: When monopoly arose from innovation (patent), DWL must be weighed against dynamic benefit. Estimates are highly sensitive to assumptions.

**Financial markets are not efficient**: Japanese bubble (1990), dot-com (2001), housing (2008) — enormous price distortions. "Smart money" often follows "dumb money" rather than correcting it (Russell and Thaler 1985). Closed-end fund anomaly: price deviates persistently from net asset value.

**Memory hook**: *"The invisible hand only works if the income distribution is already just — a condition textbooks never check."*

## CHAPTER 7: EXTERNALITIES AND THE UBIQUITY OF MARKET FAILURE

**Standard view**: Externalities are exceptions; Pigouvian taxes and Coase theorem can correct them; market failure is manageable.

**Anti-text**: Externalities are pervasive and the textbook's placement of them at the back of the book is a structural choice that marginalises them.

**Scale of externalities**:
- Air pollution: 2 million premature deaths/year globally; 21,000/year in Canada alone.
- Climate change: potentially the largest market failure in history; costs borne by future generations who cannot participate in today's markets.
- Cancer rates rising across all industrialised countries (excluding lung cancer): workplace exposures and industrial chemicals.
- Asbestos: 100,000 cancers/year; industry concealed dangers for a century; Canadian government lobbied against international ban while exporting 250,000 tonnes/year to developing countries.
- Fisheries: 29% of fished species collapsed by 2003; Newfoundland cod fishery closed, possibly permanently.
- Financial externalities: systemic risk in banking — one bank's failure threatens all connected banks (Kaufman and Scott 2003) → 2008 crisis.
- Consumption externalities (Robert Frank): visible consumption raises social norms → positional arms race → excess labour supply, consumer debt, insufficient leisure and public goods.

**Why correction fails**:
- Corporations suppress information (asbestos, tobacco, chemicals, drugs).
- Corporate lobbying blocks regulation.
- Regulatory capture: revolving door between industry and regulator.
- Rational ignorance: citizens don't monitor regulatory agencies.
- Cognitive dissonance: workers choose to believe risks are lower than they are.

**Key point**: External costs are often *deliberately* imposed for profit, not incidental side effects.

**Memory hook**: *"The textbook treats pollution as a footnote. The real world treats it as the price of doing business — paid by the public."*

## CHAPTER 8: MARGINAL PRODUCTIVITY THEORY OF INCOME DISTRIBUTION

**Standard view**: Wages = MRP; wage differentials reflect productivity; unions cause unemployment; discrimination is eliminated by competition; markets produce a just distribution.

**Anti-text**:

**The adding-up problem** (Cambridge Capital Controversy): Measuring capital's marginal product requires knowing the rate of return; the rate of return is what we're trying to determine. **Reswitching** (Robinson, Sraffa): a technique can become unprofitable and then profitable again as the interest rate changes — no monotonic relationship between capital supply and its return. The entire theory has no sound logical foundation.

**Predictions are false**:
- No single market wage: workers of identical quality earn systematically different wages across firms and industries (large wage variance in data).
- Efficiency wages: above-market wages to elicit effort → unemployment as a "discipline device." Theory *admits* the competitive labour market fails.
- Monopsony: employers have market power because workers face search and mobility costs. **Dynamic monopsony** is pervasive (Manning 2003), not rare. Minimum wages can *increase* employment by offsetting this power.

**Unions get a bad press**: Textbooks present unions as purely self-interested wage-raisers causing unemployment. Alternative: unions offset monopsony power, enforce safety standards, support public goods.

**CEO pay**: US CEOs earn 300x average wages (2000s) vs. 30x (1970). This correlates with *stock market performance*, not firm performance relative to competitors. Bebchuk and Fried: managerial power over boards, not marginal productivity, drives executive pay.

**Discrimination**: Competitive markets eliminate discrimination only if (a) non-discriminating firms exist in sufficient numbers and (b) customers are not themselves prejudiced. Both conditions often fail.

**Memory hook**: *"'You're worth what you can get' — which means wages reflect bargaining power, not just productivity."*

## CHAPTER 9: GOVERNMENT, TAXATION AND REDISTRIBUTION

**Standard view**: Equity–efficiency trade-off (Okun's leaky bucket); taxes reduce work incentives; redistribution reduces growth; the trade-off is real and significant.

**Anti-text**:

**Structural bias against redistribution**:
- Chapter typically appears 20th of 22 in textbooks — many courses never reach it.
- Language: government "uses up" resources; taxes are "imposed"; intervention "distorts."
- David George (1990): textbooks frame government as an "interferer" rather than a constitutive part of market systems.

**The trade-off is empirically unsupported**:
- **Lindert (2004)**: 30+ years of OECD data → no significant negative relationship between social transfers and growth. "It is well-known that higher taxes and transfers reduce productivity. Well known — but unsupported by statistics or history."
- **Denmark**: taxes = 50% of GDP; near-zero child poverty; among world's happiest countries; strong economic performance.
- **Figure 9.4**: Over the observed range of inequality, growth-equality relationship is essentially flat.
- New growth theory: investing in human capital (health, education, nutrition) raises growth rates.

**Pareto principle is not value-free**:
- Feldstein: policies raising incomes of the rich without reducing others' incomes are Pareto improvements — "even though it increases inequality." Those who object are "spiteful egalitarians."
- This encodes the assumption that relative position doesn't matter — directly contradicted by the Easterlin evidence.

**The social gradient of health** (Marmot 2004):
- Glasgow: men in deprived areas die at 54; affluent areas at 82 — a 28-year gap.
- Washington DC: 20-year life expectancy gap between rich and poor.
- 886,000 fewer US deaths 1991-2000 if African-Americans had same death rates as whites.
- Mechanism: chronic social stress → cardiovascular disease, immune suppression.
- WHO Commission (2008): "Social injustice is killing people on a grand scale."
- **Entirely absent from mainstream textbooks.**

**Memory hook**: *"Denmark proves it: high taxes, low inequality, high happiness, and strong growth — the textbook's trade-off exists mainly on the chalkboard."*

## CHAPTER 10: TRADE AND GLOBALIZATION

**Standard view**: Free trade based on comparative advantage benefits all countries; tariffs create deadweight losses; FDI benefits host countries; WTO creates mutually beneficial rules.

**Anti-text**:

**Comparative advantage model fails** (extending Chapter 2):
- Over 70% of industrialised country trade is with other industrialised countries — inconsistent with comparative advantage predictions.
- Capital flows dominate exchange rate determination (not current account balances).
- Samuelson (2004): offshoring can produce *net* losses for the home country — "popular polemical untruth" that long-run gains must exceed losses.
- History: US, Canada, Germany, South Korea all industrialised behind tariff walls.

**Distributional consequences are real**:
- Stolper-Samuelson: free trade with low-wage countries reduces real wages of workers in high-wage countries — standard neoclassical theory that textbooks downplay.
- Winners could compensate losers; in practice, they don't.
- Community destruction is permanent and has no monetary equivalent.

**WTO reflects power, not neutrality**:
- Developed countries maintain agricultural subsidies while demanding agricultural market access from developing countries.
- TRIPS extends pharmaceutical patents worldwide, restricting access to medicines.
- Large countries shape rules in their favour.

**MNCs and corporate power**:
- Philippines: US government pressured Supreme Court to overturn infant formula health regulation at the request of the US Chamber of Commerce.
- British imperialism: mechanism for transferring income from middle class (via taxes) to upper-class investors (Davis & Huttenback 1988).
- US Marine interventions for corporate interests: Iran (1953), Guatemala (1954), Chile (1973).
- Colombia: 2,000+ trade unionists killed; US and Canada signed free trade agreement anyway.

**Environmental costs**: Greenhouse gas emissions from global transportation are ignored. "Pollution haven" effects.

**Memory hook**: *"Free trade is presented as the invisible hand going global. In reality, it's the visible fist of the powerful shaping the rules."*

## CHAPTER 11: CONCLUSION AND POSTSCRIPT

**The Conclusion gathers 10 key problems with mainstream textbooks**:
1. Positive/normative distinction is fictitious — value judgements pervade positive claims.
2. Rational individual assumption — contradicted by systematic behavioural evidence.
3. Efficiency over equity — equity is always subordinate with insufficient evidence.
4. "More is better" — Easterlin paradox shows this is false in wealthy countries.
5. Methodological pretension — hypothesis testing is ornamental.
6. Over-reliance on perfect competition — applied as generic tool creating anti-intervention bias.
7. Power is absent — corporate power over markets, government, and opinion is invisible.
8. Free trade bias — distributional and power dimensions suppressed.
9. Community is invisible — markets undermine community; the loss is excluded from welfare calculations by definition.
10. Ecological crisis is ignored — "empty-world economics" taught in a full world.

**Prasch (Table 11.2)**: Invisible hand works only for inspection goods in spot markets (the textbook's "widget"). Most real goods are experience goods in relational contracts — the blank cells that require regulation.

**Akerlof and Shiller (Table 11.1)**: Textbooks inhabit one cell (economic motives + rational responses). Financial crises, bubbles, and crashes inhabit the other three cells.

**The 2008 Financial Crisis** (Postscript):
- Principal-agent problem: bank managers maximised short-term bonuses at shareholder and depositor expense. NINJA mortgages.
- Moral hazard: originate-and-sell model removed creditworthiness incentives. Credit rating agency fee structure encouraged generous ratings. AIG insured CDOs without adequate reserves.
- Adverse selection: risky borrowers disproportionately seek loans as rates rise.
- Systemic externality: one bank's risk-taking increases risk for all connected banks.
- Limited rationality: bubble driven by "real estate always goes up" narrative; panic in the downswing.
- Deregulation: Glass-Steagall repealed 1999; CFMA 2000 exempted credit default swaps from regulation.
- Corporate power: financial industry lobbied for every piece of deregulation that enabled the crisis.
- **Conclusion**: the crisis required exactly the analytical tools the textbook omits.
